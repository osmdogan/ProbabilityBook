21  Lecture 10.1: Integrals and Derivatives

21.1 1-Parameter Families and Leibniz’s Integral Rule

In probability theory and measure-theoretic analysis, we frequently encounter 1-parameter families of random variables or integrands \(\{X_t : t \in (a, b)\}\) defined on a measure space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\). A central problem is establishing conditions under which differentiation and integration can be interchanged.

Theorem 21.1 (Differentiation Under the Integral Sign (Driver [10.31])) Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) be a measure space, \((a, b) \subset \mathbb{R}\) an open interval, and \(f: (a, b) \times \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\) a function satisfying:

  1. Measurability: \(\omega \mapsto f(t, \omega)\) is \(\mathcal{F}\)-measurable for each fixed \(t \in (a, b)\).
  2. Integrability: \(f(t_0, \cdot) \in \mathcal{L}^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) for at least one point \(t_0 \in (a, b)\).
  3. Differentiability: The partial derivative \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t, \omega)\) exists for \(\mu\)-a.e. \(\omega \in \Omega\) and for every \(t \in (a, b)\).
  4. Domination: There exists a non-negative function \(g \in \mathcal{L}^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) such that: \[ \left| \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t, \omega) \right| \le g(\omega) \quad \text{for } \mu\text{-a.e. } \omega \in \Omega \text{ and all } t \in (a, b) \tag{21.1}\]

Then \(f(t, \cdot) \in \mathcal{L}^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) for all \(t \in (a, b)\), the map \(G(t) := \int_{\Omega} f(t, \omega) \, d\mu(\omega)\) is differentiable on \((a, b)\), and: \[ \frac{d}{dt} \int_{\Omega} f(t, \omega) \, d\mu(\omega) = \int_{\Omega} \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t, \omega) \, d\mu(\omega) \tag{21.2}\]

Proof (Proof of Theorem 21.1). Fix \(t \in (a, b)\) and select any sequence \((h_n)_{n=1}^\infty \subset \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}\) such that \(t + h_n \in (a, b)\) for all \(n\) and \(h_n \to 0\) as \(n \to \infty\).

21.1.1 Step 1: Integrability of \(f(t, \cdot)\)

By the Mean Value Theorem applied to \(u \mapsto f(u, \omega)\) on the interval between \(t_0\) and \(t\), there exists a point \(t^*(\omega)\) strictly between \(t_0\) and \(t\) such that: \[ |f(t, \omega) - f(t_0, \omega)| = \left| \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t^*(\omega), \omega) \right| |t - t_0| \le |t - t_0| g(\omega) \quad \text{a.e.} \]

Thus, \(|f(t, \omega)| \le |f(t_0, \omega)| + |t - t_0| g(\omega)\) a.e. Integrating both sides yields \(\int |f(t, \omega)| \, d\mu \le \int |f(t_0, \omega)| \, d\mu + |t - t_0| \int g \, d\mu < \infty\), so \(f(t, \cdot) \in \mathcal{L}^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) for all \(t \in (a, b)\).

21.1.2 Step 2: Interchanging the Limit and Integral

Define the sequence of difference quotients: \[ D_n(\omega) := \frac{f(t + h_n, \omega) - f(t, \omega)}{h_n} \]

  1. Pointwise Convergence: By assumption (3), \(D_n(\omega) \to \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t, \omega)\) a.e. as \(n \to \infty\).
  2. Uniform Domination: By the Mean Value Theorem, for each \(n\) and a.e. \(\omega\), there exists \(t_n^*(\omega)\) between \(t\) and \(t + h_n\) such that \(|D_n(\omega)| = \left| \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t_n^*(\omega), \omega) \right| \le g(\omega)\).

Since \(f(t+h_n, \cdot)\) and \(f(t, \cdot)\) are measurable (condition 1), \(D_n\) is measurable. Since \(|D_n| \le g \in \mathcal{L}^1\), we have \(D_n \in \mathcal{L}^1\). The limit \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t, \cdot)\) is measurable as the a.e. pointwise limit of the measurable functions \((D_n)\).

By linearity of the \(\mathcal{L}^1\) integral: \[ \begin{align*} \frac{G(t + h_n) - G(t)}{h_n} &= \frac{1}{h_n}\left(\int_{\Omega} f(t+h_n, \omega)\,d\mu - \int_{\Omega} f(t, \omega)\,d\mu\right) \\ &= \int_{\Omega} D_n(\omega)\,d\mu(\omega). \end{align*} \]

Applying the Dominated Convergence Theorem (Theorem 20.2) with \(|D_n| \le g\) and \(D_n \to \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t, \cdot)\) a.e.: \[ \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dt} G(t) &= \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{G(t + h_n) - G(t)}{h_n} \\ &= \lim_{n \to \infty} \int_{\Omega} D_n(\omega) \, d\mu(\omega) \\ &= \int_{\Omega} \lim_{n \to \infty} D_n(\omega) \, d\mu(\omega)\\ &= \int_{\Omega} \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t, \omega) \, d\mu(\omega) \end{align*} \]

21.2 Moment-Generating Functions and Analyticity

Definition 21.1 (Moment-Generating Function (MGF)) Let \(X\) be a real-valued random variable on a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\). The moment-generating function \(M_X(t)\) is defined by: \[ M_X(t) := \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}] = \int_{\Omega} e^{tX(\omega)} \, d\mathbb{P}(\omega) \in (0, \infty] \tag{21.3}\]

Proposition 21.1 (Differentiation and Moments of MGF) Suppose there exists \(\epsilon > 0\) such that \(\mathbb{E}[e^{\epsilon |X|}] < \infty\). Then \(M_X(t) < \infty\) for all \(t \in (-\epsilon, \epsilon)\), \(M_X\) is infinitely differentiable on \((-\epsilon, \epsilon)\), and: \[ M_X^{(n)}(t) = \mathbb{E}[X^n e^{tX}] \quad \forall t \in (-\epsilon, \epsilon), \, n \in \mathbb{N} \tag{21.4}\] In particular, evaluating at \(t = 0\) yields the raw moments: \[ M_X^{(n)}(0) = \mathbb{E}[X^n] \tag{21.5}\]

Proof (Proof of Proposition 21.1). We prove by induction on \(n \in \mathbb{N}_0\) that \(M_X^{(n)}(t) = \mathbb{E}[X^n e^{tX}]\) for all \(t \in (-\epsilon, \epsilon)\).

21.2.1 Step 0: \(M_X(t) < \infty\) on \((-\epsilon, \epsilon)\)

Key polynomial bound. For any \(\alpha \in (0,1)\) and \(n \in \mathbb{N}_0\), the function \(u \mapsto u^n e^{-\alpha\epsilon u}\) attains its maximum on \([0,\infty)\) at \(u = n/(\alpha\epsilon)\), so: \[ \sup_{u \ge 0}\, u^n e^{-\alpha\epsilon u} = \left(\frac{n}{\alpha\epsilon e}\right)^n < \infty. \] Consequently, there exists a finite constant \(C_{n,\alpha}\) such that: \[ |x|^n \le C_{n,\alpha}\, e^{\alpha\epsilon|x|} \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathbb{R}. \tag{21.6}\]

In particular, for \(n = 0\) and any \(t \in (-\epsilon, \epsilon)\), write \(t = (1-\alpha)\epsilon \cdot \operatorname{sgn}(t) \cdot r\) for some \(r < 1\) (choosing \(\alpha\) small enough so \(|t| < (1-\alpha)\epsilon\)). Then: \[ e^{tX} \le e^{|t||X|} \le e^{(1-\alpha)\epsilon|X|} \le e^{\epsilon|X|}, \] so \(M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}] \le \mathbb{E}[e^{\epsilon|X|}] < \infty\).

21.2.2 Step 1: Base case \(n = 0 \to 1\) (differentiability and \(M_X' = \mathbb{E}[X e^{tX}]\))

Fix \(t \in (-\epsilon, \epsilon)\) and choose \(\alpha \in (0,1)\) such that \(|t| < (1-\alpha)\epsilon\); this is possible since \((1-\alpha)\epsilon \nearrow \epsilon\) as \(\alpha \searrow 0\). We verify the four conditions of Theorem 21.1 for \(f(t, \omega) = e^{tX(\omega)}\) on the interval \(I_\alpha := (-(1-\alpha)\epsilon,\, (1-\alpha)\epsilon) \ni t\):

  1. Measurability: \(\omega \mapsto e^{tX(\omega)}\) is measurable for each \(t\), since \(X\) is measurable and the exponential is continuous.
  2. Integrability at \(t_0 = 0\): \(\mathbb{E}[e^{0 \cdot X}] = 1 < \infty\).
  3. Differentiability: \(\frac{\partial}{\partial t} e^{tX(\omega)} = X(\omega)\,e^{tX(\omega)}\) exists for every \(\omega \in \Omega\) and every \(t \in I_\alpha\).
  4. Domination: Using the polynomial bound Equation 21.6 with \(n=1\), \(C_\alpha := C_{1,\alpha} = \frac{1}{e\alpha\epsilon}\): \[ \left|\frac{\partial}{\partial t} e^{tX}\right| = |X|\,e^{tX} \le |X|\,e^{|t||X|} \le |X|\,e^{(1-\alpha)\epsilon|X|} \le C_\alpha\,e^{\alpha\epsilon|X|}\cdot e^{(1-\alpha)\epsilon|X|} = C_\alpha\,e^{\epsilon|X|}. \] Since \(\mathbb{E}[e^{\epsilon|X|}] < \infty\) by hypothesis, \(g(\omega) := C_\alpha\,e^{\epsilon|X(\omega)|}\) is an integrable dominating function.

All conditions of Theorem 21.1 are satisfied on \(I_\alpha\), hence: \[ M_X'(t) = \frac{d}{dt}\,\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t} e^{tX}\right] = \mathbb{E}[X e^{tX}]. \]

21.2.3 Step 2: Inductive step (\(n \to n+1\))

Suppose that \(M_X^{(n)}(t) = \mathbb{E}[X^n e^{tX}]\) for all \(t \in (-\epsilon,\epsilon)\). We differentiate the integral \(t \mapsto \mathbb{E}[X^n e^{tX}]\) by applying Theorem 21.1 to \(f(t,\omega) = X(\omega)^n e^{tX(\omega)}\) on the same \(I_\alpha\):

  1. Measurability: \(\omega \mapsto X(\omega)^n e^{tX(\omega)}\) is measurable for each \(t\).
  2. Integrability at \(t_0 = 0\): By the polynomial bound Equation 21.6, \(\mathbb{E}[|X|^n] \le C_{n,\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}[e^{\alpha\epsilon|X|}] \le C_{n,\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}[e^{\epsilon|X|}] < \infty\).
  3. Differentiability: \(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigl(X^n e^{tX}\bigr) = X^{n+1} e^{tX}\) exists pointwise for every \(\omega\) and \(t \in I_\alpha\).
  4. Domination: Again by Equation 21.6

\[ \left|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigl(X^n e^{tX}\bigr)\right| = |X|^{n+1} e^{|t||X|} \le |X|^{n+1} e^{(1-\alpha)\epsilon|X|} \le C_{n+1,\alpha}\,e^{\epsilon|X|} \in \mathcal{L}^1. \]

Therefore: \[ M_X^{(n+1)}(t) = \frac{d}{dt}\,\mathbb{E}[X^n e^{tX}] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigl(X^n e^{tX}\bigr)\right] = \mathbb{E}[X^{n+1} e^{tX}]. \]

By induction, \(M_X^{(n)}(t) = \mathbb{E}[X^n e^{tX}]\) for all \(n \in \mathbb{N}_0\) and all \(t \in (-\epsilon, \epsilon)\).

21.2.4 Step 3: Moment formula

Setting \(t = 0\) in the identity just established: \[ M_X^{(n)}(0) = \mathbb{E}[X^n e^{0 \cdot X}] = \mathbb{E}[X^n]. \]

Proposition 21.2 (Analyticity of the MGF) If \(\mathbb{E}[e^{\epsilon |X|}] < \infty\) for some \(\epsilon > 0\), then \(M_X(t)\) is analytic on \((-\epsilon, \epsilon)\) and expands as a power series: \[ M_X(t) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^n}{n!} \mathbb{E}[X^n] \quad \forall t \in (-\epsilon, \epsilon) \tag{21.7}\]

Proof (Proof of Proposition 21.2). We establish the power series representation in three steps.

21.2.5 Step 1: All moments are finite

By the polynomial bound Equation 21.6 from the proof of Proposition 21.1, for any fixed \(\alpha \in (0,1)\) there exists \(C_{n,\alpha} < \infty\) with \(|x|^n \le C_{n,\alpha}\,e^{\alpha\epsilon|x|}\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\). Taking expectations: \[ \mathbb{E}[|X|^n] \le C_{n,\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}[e^{\alpha\epsilon|X|}] \le C_{n,\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}[e^{\epsilon|X|}] < \infty. \] Hence all moments of \(X\) are finite.

21.2.6 Step 2: The power series converges absolutely for \(|t| < \epsilon\)

Fix \(t \in (-\epsilon, \epsilon)\). Since \(\frac{|t|^n}{n!}|X(\omega)|^n \ge 0\) for all \(n\) and \(\omega\), Tonelli’s theorem (or the Monotone Convergence Theorem) allows us to exchange the sum and expectation: \[ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{|t|^n}{n!}\,\mathbb{E}[|X|^n] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{|t|^n}{n!}\,|X|^n\right] = \mathbb{E}[e^{|t||X|}] \le \mathbb{E}[e^{\epsilon|X|}] < \infty, \] where the last inequality uses \(|t| < \epsilon\). This confirms that the power series \(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^n}{n!}\mathbb{E}[X^n]\) converges absolutely for every \(t \in (-\epsilon, \epsilon)\), so its radius of convergence is at least \(\epsilon\).

21.2.7 Step 3: \(M_X(t)\) equals the power series via DCT

Define the partial-sum sequence \(S_N(t, \omega) := \sum_{n=0}^{N} \frac{t^n}{n!}\,X(\omega)^n\). We verify the conditions for the Dominated Convergence Theorem (Theorem 20.2):

  • Pointwise convergence: For every fixed \(\omega \in \Omega\), the Taylor series of the exponential converges: \[ S_N(t, \omega) \;\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\; e^{tX(\omega)}. \]

  • Integrable domination: For \(|t| < \epsilon\): \[ |S_N(t, \omega)| \le \sum_{n=0}^{N} \frac{|t|^n}{n!}\,|X(\omega)|^n \le \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{|t|^n}{n!}\,|X(\omega)|^n = e^{|t||X(\omega)|} \le e^{\epsilon|X(\omega)|} \in \mathcal{L}^1. \]

Since all moments are finite (Step 1), linearity of expectation gives \(\mathbb{E}[S_N] = \sum_{n=0}^N \frac{t^n}{n!}\mathbb{E}[X^n]\). Applying the Dominated Convergence Theorem: \[ M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}] = \lim_{N\to\infty} \mathbb{E}[S_N(t,\cdot)] = \lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^N \frac{t^n}{n!}\,\mathbb{E}[X^n] = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^n}{n!}\,\mathbb{E}[X^n]. \]

21.2.8 Step 4: Analyticity

A function that is represented by a convergent power series on an open interval is real-analytic on that interval. Since the series \(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^n}{n!}\mathbb{E}[X^n]\) converges for all \(t \in (-\epsilon, \epsilon)\) (Step 2) and equals \(M_X(t)\) there (Step 3), we conclude that \(M_X\) is analytic on \((-\epsilon, \epsilon)\).

21.3 Counterexample: Failure of Uniform Domination

Example 21.1 (Failure of Interchange: Indicator Family on \([0,1]\)) Setup. Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \lambda) = ([0, 1], \mathcal{B}([0, 1]), \lambda)\) be the Lebesgue measure space and \((a,b) = (0,1)\). Define: \[ f(t, \omega) := \mathbf{1}_{[0, t]}(\omega) \quad \text{for } (t, \omega) \in (0, 1) \times [0, 1]. \] This is the indicator of the “sub-diagonal” region: \(f(t,\omega) = 1\) if \(\omega \le t\) and \(f(t,\omega) = 0\) if \(\omega > t\).

Part 1: The two computations disagree.

Direct differentiation. The integral equals the length of \([0,t]\): \[ G(t) := \int_{[0,1]} f(t, \omega)\,d\lambda(\omega) = \lambda([0,t]) = t, \] so \(G'(t) = 1\) for all \(t \in (0,1)\).

Formal interchange. For \(\lambda\)-a.e. fixed \(\omega\) (specifically, all \(\omega \ne t\)), \(f(t,\omega)\) is locally constant in \(t\), giving \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t,\omega) = 0\). The formal interchange therefore yields: \[ \int_{[0,1]} \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t,\omega)\,d\lambda(\omega) = \int_{[0,1]} 0\,d\lambda(\omega) = 0. \]

Since \(G'(t) = 1 \ne 0\), the interchange \(\frac{d}{dt}\int = \int\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\) is false here.

Part 2: Checking which conditions of Theorem 21.1 hold.

Condition 1 (Measurability): \(\checkmark\) For each fixed \(t\), \(\omega \mapsto \mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}(\omega)\) is a Borel-measurable indicator function.

Condition 2 (Integrability): \(\checkmark\) At \(t_0 = 1/2\), for example, \(\int_{[0,1]} f(1/2,\omega)\,d\lambda = 1/2 < \infty\).

Condition 3 (Differentiability): \(\boldsymbol{\times}\) — fails jointly.

For any fixed \(t \in (0,1)\), compute the difference quotient explicitly:

  • If \(\omega < t\): then \(f(t+h,\omega) = 1\) and \(f(t,\omega) = 1\) for all \(|h|\) small enough (so that \(\omega < t + h\) and \(\omega < t\)), giving quotient \(0 \to 0\).
  • If \(\omega > t\): then \(f(t+h,\omega) = 0\) and \(f(t,\omega) = 0\) for all \(|h|\) small enough, giving quotient \(0 \to 0\).
  • If \(\omega = t\): \(f(t,t) = 1\) (since \(t \in [0,t]\)). For \(h > 0\): \(f(t+h,t) = 1\), so the right quotient is \(0\). For \(h < 0\): \(f(t+h,t) = \mathbf{1}_{[0,t+h]}(t) = 0\) (since \(t > t+h\)), so the left quotient is \(\frac{0-1}{h} = \frac{-1}{h} \to +\infty\) as \(h \to 0^-\).

Hence for each fixed \(t\), the partial derivative \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t,\omega)\) fails to exist at the single point \(\omega = t\), which is a \(\lambda\)-null set. So condition 3 holds for each fixed \(t\) separately. However, condition 3 requires a single null set \(N\) independent of \(t\) outside which the derivative exists for every \(t \in (0,1)\) simultaneously. But for every \(\omega \in (0,1)\), the derivative fails at \(t = \omega\). Thus any such \(N\) would need to contain all of \((0,1)\), which has positive measure. The joint condition fails.

Condition 4 (Domination): \(\boldsymbol{\times}\) — fails as well.

For \(h > 0\), the difference quotient is: \[ D_h(t, \omega) := \frac{f(t+h,\omega) - f(t,\omega)}{h} = \frac{\mathbf{1}_{(t,\,t+h]}(\omega)}{h}. \] This equals \(1/h\) on the interval \((t, t+h]\) (of length \(h\)) and \(0\) elsewhere. For any candidate dominating function \(g \in \mathcal{L}^1([0,1])\), one needs \(g(\omega) \ge 1/h\) for all \(\omega \in (t, t+h]\). Taking \(h = \omega - t\) (i.e., for each \(\omega > t\) just above \(t\)), this forces \(g(\omega) \ge \frac{1}{\omega - t}\) for \(\omega\) near \(t\). But: \[ \int_t^{t+\delta} \frac{1}{\omega - t}\,d\omega = \Big[\log(\omega - t)\Big]_t^{t+\delta} = +\infty, \] so no integrable \(g\) can dominate \(\{D_h\}\) uniformly over all \(h > 0\).

Lesson. The example shows that both conditions 3 and 4 are genuinely necessary. The a.e.-pointwise derivative exists for each fixed \(t\), giving a sensible-looking “answer” of \(0\) — but the integral of that answer disagrees with the true derivative \(G'(t) = 1\). The failure is not visible slice-by-slice; it only manifests in the joint structure required by the theorem.
