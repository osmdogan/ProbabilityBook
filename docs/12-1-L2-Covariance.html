25  Lecture 12.1: \(L^2\) and Covariance

25.1 The \(L^2\) Space and Integral Inequalities

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) be a measure space.

Definition 25.1 The space \(L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) is the collection of all real-valued, \(\mathcal{F}\)-measurable functions \(f: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\) that are square-integrable: \[ \begin{aligned} &L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\\ &\triangleq \left\{ f: \Omega \to \mathbb{R} \;\middle|\; f \text{ is } \mathcal{F}\text{-measurable and } \int_{\Omega} f^2 \, d\mu < \infty. \right\} \end{aligned} \]

25.2 Inclusions and Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

The following proposition shows that the product of two \(L^2\) functions is integrable, which is a key fact in defining covariance and correlation.

Proposition 25.1 If \(f, g \in L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\), then \(fg \in L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\).

Proof (Proof of Proposition 25.1). For any \(f, g \in \mathbb{R}\), expanding \(0 \le (|f| - |g|)^2 = f^2 - 2|fg| + g^2\) yields the elementary point-wise inequality: \[ |fg| \le \frac{1}{2} \left( f^2 + g^2 \right). \tag{25.1}\]

Integrating Equation 25.1 over \(\Omega\) with respect to \(\mu\): \[ \int_{\Omega} |fg| \, d\mu \le \frac{1}{2} \int_{\Omega} f^2 \, d\mu + \frac{1}{2} \int_{\Omega} g^2 \, d\mu < \infty. \]

Thus, \(fg \in L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\).

This result implies the following corollary, which shows that \(L^2\) functions are also \(L^1\) functions when the measure is finite.

Corollary 25.1 If \(\mu\) is a finite measure (\(\mu(\Omega) < \infty\)), then \(L^2(\mu) \subseteq L^1(\mu)\).

Proof (Proof of Corollary 25.1). Let \(f \in L^2(\mu)\). Taking \(g = \mathbf{1}_{\Omega} \in L^2(\mu)\), we have \(\int_{\Omega} \mathbf{1}_{\Omega}^2 \, d\mu = \mu(\Omega) < \infty\). By Proposition 25.1, \(f \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\Omega} = f \in L^1(\mu)\).

25.3 Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

For \(f, g \in L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\), define the inner product and norm: \[ \langle f, g \rangle_{L^2} \triangleq \int_{\Omega} fg \, d\mu, \quad \|f\|_{L^2} \triangleq \left( \int_{\Omega} f^2 \, d\mu \right)^{1/2}. \tag{25.2}\]

Theorem 25.1 (Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality) If \(f, g \in L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\), then: \[ \left| \int_{\Omega} fg \, d\mu \right| \le \int_{\Omega} |fg| \, d\mu \le \|f\|_{L^2} \|g\|_{L^2}. \tag{25.3}\]

Proof (Proof of Equation 25.3). The inequality \(\left|\int_{\Omega} fg \, d\mu\right| \le \int_{\Omega} |fg| \, d\mu\) is standard. To establish the second inequality, define the quadratic polynomial for \(t \in \mathbb{R}\): \[ p(t) \triangleq \int_{\Omega} (|f| - t|g|)^2 \, d\mu \ge 0. \]

Expanding the integrand: \[ \begin{aligned} p(t) &= \int_{\Omega} f^2 \, d\mu - 2t \int_{\Omega} |fg| \, d\mu + t^2 \int_{\Omega} g^2 \, d\mu \\ &= \|f\|_{L^2}^2 - 2t \int_{\Omega} |fg| \, d\mu + t^2 \|g\|_{L^2}^2. \end{aligned} \]

Since \(p(t) \ge 0\) for all \(t \in \mathbb{R}\), its discriminant \(\Delta\) must be non-positive: \[ \Delta = \left( -2 \int_{\Omega} |fg| \, d\mu \right)^2 - 4 \|f\|_{L^2}^2 \|g\|_{L^2}^2 \le 0. \]

\[ 4 \left( \int_{\Omega} |fg| \, d\mu \right)^2 \le 4 \|f\|_{L^2}^2 \|g\|_{L^2}^2 \implies \int_{\Omega} |fg| \, d\mu \le \|f\|_{L^2} \|g\|_{L^2}. \]

25.4 Vector Space Structure of \(L^2\)

Corollary 25.2 \(L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) is a vector space, and \(\|\cdot\|_{L^2}\) defines a norm on it (up to \(\mu\)-almost sure equivalence classes).

Proof (Proof of Corollary 25.2). We verify the norm axioms:

  1. Homogeneity: For \(\alpha \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(f \in L^2\), \(\|\alpha f\|_{L^2}^2 = \int_{\Omega} (\alpha f)^2 \, d\mu = \alpha^2 \int_{\Omega} f^2 \, d\mu = \alpha^2 \|f\|_{L^2}^2\), so \(\|\alpha f\|_{L^2} = |\alpha| \|f\|_{L^2}\).
  2. Positive Definiteness: \(\|f\|_{L^2} = 0 \implies \int_{\Omega} f^2 \, d\mu = 0 \implies f^2 = 0 \quad \mu\text{-a.s.} \implies f = 0 \quad \mu\text{-a.s.}\)
  3. Triangle Inequality (Minkowski’s Inequality): For \(f, g \in L^2\): \[ \begin{aligned} \|f + g\|_{L^2}^2 &= \int_{\Omega} (f + g)^2 \, d\mu = \int_{\Omega} f^2 \, d\mu + 2 \int_{\Omega} fg \, d\mu + \int_{\Omega} g^2 \, d\mu \\ &\le \|f\|_{L^2}^2 + 2 \|f\|_{L^2} \|g\|_{L^2} + \|g\|_{L^2}^2 = \left( \|f\|_{L^2} + \|g\|_{L^2} \right)^2. \end{aligned} \]

Taking square roots yields \(\|f + g\|_{L^2} \le \|f\|_{L^2} + \|g\|_{L^2} < \infty\).

Remark 25.1. \(L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) is a Hilbert space under the inner product \[ \langle f, g \rangle_{L^2} = \int fg \, d\mu. \] It is Cauchy complete. That is, if \((f_n)_{n=1}^\infty \subset L^2\) is Cauchy (\(\|f_n - f_m\|_{L^2} \to 0\) as \(n, m \to \infty\)), there exists \(f \in L^2\) such that \(\|f_n - f\|_{L^2} \to 0\). Completeness holds in general \(L^p\) spaces for \(1 \le p \le \infty\) and plays a crucial role in constructing conditional expectations.

25.5 Covariance, Variance, and Correlation

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) be a probability space. Because \(\mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1 < \infty\), \(L^2(\mathbb{P}) \subseteq L^1(\mathbb{P})\). In particular: \[ \begin{aligned} \|X\|_{L^1} &= \int_{\Omega} |X| \, d\mathbb{P} = \int_{\Omega} |X \cdot 1| \, d\mathbb{P} \\ &\le \|X\|_{L^2} \|1\|_{L^2} = \|X\|_{L^2} = \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X^2]}. \end{aligned} \tag{25.4}\]

Thus, \(|\mathbb{E}[X]| \le \mathbb{E}[|X|] \le \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X^2]}\).

For \(X, Y \in L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\), define the centered random variables: \[ \dot{X} \triangleq X - \mathbb{E}[X], \quad \dot{Y} \triangleq Y - \mathbb{E}[Y] \tag{25.5}\]

Definition 25.2  

  1. Covariance: \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Cov}(X, Y) &\triangleq \mathbb{E}[\dot{X} \dot{Y}] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]. \end{aligned} \]

  2. Variance: \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Var}(X) &\triangleq \text{Cov}(X, X) = \mathbb{E}[\dot{X}^2] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 \ge 0. \end{aligned} \]

Lemma 25.1 If \(X \in L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) and \(\text{Var}(X) = 0\), then \(X = \mathbb{E}[X]\) \(\mathbb{P}\)-almost surely (i.e., \(X\) is constant a.s.).

Proof (Proof of Lemma 25.1). \(0 = \text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[\dot{X}^2] = \int_{\Omega} \dot{X}^2 \, d\mathbb{P}\). Since \(\dot{X}^2 \ge 0\), \(\dot{X}^2 = 0\) \(\mathbb{P}\)-a.s., which implies \(\dot{X} = 0 \implies X = \mathbb{E}[X]\) \(\mathbb{P}\)-a.s.

25.6 Variance Examples

  1. Normal Distribution \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\alpha, t)\): Since \(X \overset{d}{=} \sqrt{t} Z + \alpha\) with \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\), we have \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \alpha\) (computed in Lecture 11.2), so \(X - \mathbb{E}[X] = \sqrt{t}\,Z\). Therefore: \[ \text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\bigl[(X - \alpha)^2\bigr] = \mathbb{E}\bigl[(\sqrt{t}\,Z)^2\bigr] = t\,\mathbb{E}[Z^2] = t \cdot 1 = t. \]

  2. Exponential Distribution \(T \sim \text{Exp}(\alpha)\): We use \(\text{Var}(T) = \mathbb{E}[T^2] - (\mathbb{E}[T])^2\) and compute \(\mathbb{E}[T^2]\) by differentiating the Laplace integral twice. Since \(\frac{d^2}{d\alpha^2}e^{-\alpha t} = t^2 e^{-\alpha t}\), the interchange with integration is justified by DCT (the dominator \(t^2 e^{-\alpha_0 t}\) is integrable for any fixed \(\alpha_0 > 0\)): \[ \int_{0}^{\infty} t^2 e^{-\alpha t} \, dt = \frac{d^2}{d\alpha^2} \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-\alpha t} \, dt = \frac{d^2}{d\alpha^2}\!\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right) = \frac{2}{\alpha^3}. \] Therefore \(\mathbb{E}[T^2] = \alpha \cdot \dfrac{2}{\alpha^3} = \dfrac{2}{\alpha^2}\), and: \[ \text{Var}(T) = \mathbb{E}[T^2] - \bigl(\mathbb{E}[T]\bigr)^2 = \frac{2}{\alpha^2} - \frac{1}{\alpha^2} = \frac{1}{\alpha^2}. \]

  3. Poisson Distribution \(N \sim \text{Pois}(\alpha)\): We use \(\text{Var}(N) = \mathbb{E}[N(N-1)] + \mathbb{E}[N] - (\mathbb{E}[N])^2\). The \(k=0\) and \(k=1\) terms vanish, so: \[ \mathbb{E}[N(N-1)] = \sum_{k=2}^{\infty} k(k-1)\, e^{-\alpha} \frac{\alpha^k}{k!} = \sum_{k=2}^{\infty} e^{-\alpha} \frac{\alpha^k}{(k-2)!}. \] Substituting \(j = k - 2\): \[ \mathbb{E}[N(N-1)] = \alpha^2 e^{-\alpha} \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{\alpha^j}{j!} = \alpha^2 e^{-\alpha} e^{\alpha} = \alpha^2. \] Therefore: \[ \text{Var}(N) = \mathbb{E}[N(N-1)] + \mathbb{E}[N] - \bigl(\mathbb{E}[N]\bigr)^2 = \alpha^2 + \alpha - \alpha^2 = \alpha. \]

25.7 Correlation and Uncorrelated Variables

Definition 25.3 Two random variables \(X, Y \in L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) are uncorrelated if \(\text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0\).

Their correlation is defined as: \[ \text{Corr}(X, Y) \triangleq \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sqrt{\text{Var}(X)\text{Var}(Y)}} = \frac{\langle \dot{X}, \dot{Y} \rangle_{L^2}}{\|\dot{X}\|_{L^2} \|\dot{Y}\|_{L^2}}. \tag{25.6}\]

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, \(|\text{Corr}(X, Y)| \le 1\).

Proposition 25.2  

  1. For any \(\alpha \in \mathbb{R}\), \(\text{Cov}(X + \alpha, Y) = \text{Cov}(X, Y)\).

  2. If \(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n \in L^2\) are pairwise uncorrelated, then: \[ \text{Var}(X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_n) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \text{Var}(X_j). \tag{25.7}\]

Proof (Proof of Proposition 25.2). Observe that \(\dot{(X + \alpha)} = (X + \alpha) - \mathbb{E}[X + \alpha] = X - \mathbb{E}[X] = \dot{X}\).

For the sum of random variables: \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Var}\left( \sum_{j=1}^{n} X_j \right) &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \left( \sum_{j=1}^{n} \dot{X}_j \right)^2 \right] = \mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{j=1}^{n} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \dot{X}_j \dot{X}_k \right] = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \mathbb{E}[\dot{X}_j \dot{X}_k] \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{n} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \text{Cov}(X_j, X_k). \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\text{Cov}(X_j, X_k) = 0\) for \(j \neq k\), only terms with \(j = k\) remain: \[ \text{Var}\left( \sum_{j=1}^{n} X_j \right) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \text{Cov}(X_j, X_j) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \text{Var}(X_j). \]

25.8 Indicator Functions and Independence

Let \(A, B \in \mathcal{F}\), and consider \(X = \mathbf{1}_A\) and \(Y = \mathbf{1}_B\). \(X\) and \(Y\) are Bernoulli random variables with \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{P}(A)\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_B] = \mathbb{P}(B)\).

Since \(\mathbf{1}_A \mathbf{1}_B = \mathbf{1}_{A \cap B}\): \[ \text{Cov}(\mathbf{1}_A, \mathbf{1}_B) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{A \cap B}] - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_A]\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_B] = \mathbb{P}(A \cap B) - \mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B). \]

Therefore, \(\text{Cov}(\mathbf{1}_A, \mathbf{1}_B) = 0\) if and only if \(A\) and \(B\) are independent events.

25.9 Example: Coin Tossing Sequence

Consider tossing a fair coin \(n\) times.

  • Sample Space: \(\Omega = \{ (\omega_1, \dots, \omega_n) \mid \omega_j \in \{0, 1\} \}\), so \(|\Omega| = 2^n\), where \(1 = \text{Heads}\) and \(0 = \text{Tails}\).
  • \(\sigma\)-algebra: \(\mathcal{F} = 2^{\Omega}\).
  • Probability Measure: Uniform measure \(\mathbb{P}(A) = \frac{|A|}{2^n}\) for \(A \subseteq \Omega\).
  • Coordinate Random Variables: \(X_j(\omega) = \omega_j \in \{0,1\}\) for \(j = 1, \dots, n\). Since each \(X_j\) is bounded, \(X_j \in L^2(\mathbb{P})\).

Exercise 25.1 Show that \(\text{Cov}(X_j, X_k) = \frac{1}{4} \delta_{jk}\), where \(\delta_{jk}\) is the Kronecker delta.

Solution. Step 1: Mean. For each \(j\), exactly half of the \(2^n\) outcomes have \(\omega_j = 1\): \[ \mathbb{E}[X_j] = \mathbb{P}(X_j = 1) = \frac{2^{n-1}}{2^n} = \frac{1}{2}. \]

Step 2: Second moment. Since \(X_j \in \{0,1\}\), we have \(X_j^2 = X_j\), so \(\mathbb{E}[X_j^2] = \mathbb{E}[X_j] = \frac{1}{2}\).

Step 3: Diagonal case \(j = k\). By the shortcut formula: \[ \text{Cov}(X_j, X_j) = \text{Var}(X_j) = \mathbb{E}[X_j^2] - \bigl(\mathbb{E}[X_j]\bigr)^2 = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{4}. \]

Step 4: Off-diagonal case \(j \neq k\). The product \(X_j X_k = 1\) if and only if \(\omega_j = 1\) and \(\omega_k = 1\). For \(j \neq k\), the remaining \(n-2\) coordinates are free, giving \(2^{n-2}\) such outcomes: \[ \mathbb{E}[X_j X_k] = \mathbb{P}(X_j = 1,\, X_k = 1) = \frac{2^{n-2}}{2^n} = \frac{1}{4}. \] Therefore: \[ \text{Cov}(X_j, X_k) = \mathbb{E}[X_j X_k] - \mathbb{E}[X_j]\mathbb{E}[X_k] = \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 0. \]

Conclusion. Combining both cases: \[ \text{Cov}(X_j, X_k) = \frac{1}{4}\,\delta_{jk}. \qquad \square \]
