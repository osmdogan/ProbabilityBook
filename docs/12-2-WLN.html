26  Lecture 12.2: The Weak Law of Large Numbers

26.1 Markov’s and Chebyshev’s Inequalities

26.2 Markov’s Inequality

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) be a measure space. Recall Markov’s inequality: if \(f \ge 0\) is a non-negative measurable function and \(\epsilon > 0, p > 0\), then: \[ \mu(\{f \ge \epsilon\}) \le \frac{1}{\epsilon^p} \int_{\Omega} f^p \, d\mu \tag{26.1}\]

26.3 Chebyshev’s Inequality

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) be a probability space and let \(X \in L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\).

Setting \(p = 2\) and applying Markov’s inequality to the non-negative random variable \(f = |\dot{X}| = |X - \mathbb{E}[X]|\), we obtain Chebyshev’s inequality:

Theorem 26.1 (Chebyshev’s Inequality) Let \(X \in L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\). For any \(\epsilon > 0\): \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}\left(|X - \mathbb{E}[X]| \ge \epsilon\right) &= \mathbb{P}\left(|\dot{X}|\ge \epsilon\right) \\ &\le \frac{1}{\epsilon^2} \mathbb{E}\left[|\dot{X}|^2\right] = \frac{\text{Var}(X)}{\epsilon^2}. \end{aligned} \tag{26.2}\]

Proof. By definition of expectation and non-negativity of \(|\dot{X}|^2\): \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Var}(X) &= \mathbb{E}\left[|\dot{X}|^2\right] = \int_{\Omega} |\dot{X}|^2 \, d\mathbb{P}\\ & \ge \int_{\{|\dot{X}| \ge \epsilon\}} |\dot{X}|^2 \, d\mathbb{P} \ge \epsilon^2 \int_{\{|\dot{X}| \ge \epsilon\}} \, d\mathbb{P} = \epsilon^2 \mathbb{P}(|\dot{X}| \ge \epsilon). \end{aligned} \]

Dividing both sides by \(\epsilon^2 > 0\) completes the proof.

Alternative Form (Standard Deviation Units):

Let \(\sigma(X) \triangleq \sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}\) denote the standard deviation of \(X\). Setting \(\epsilon = k \sigma(X)\) for \(k > 0\) in Equation 26.2 yields: \[ \mathbb{P}\left(|X - \mathbb{E}[X]| \ge k \sigma(X)\right) \le \frac{\text{Var}(X)}{(k \sigma(X))^2} = \frac{1}{k^2} \tag{26.3}\]

26.4 Interpretation of Expectation and the Empirical Mean

Consider tossing a fair coin \(n\) times:

  • Sample Space: \(\Omega = \{ \omega = (\omega_1, \dots, \omega_n) \mid \omega_j \in \{0, 1\} \}\).
  • \(\sigma\)-algebra: \(\mathcal{F} = 2^{\Omega}\).
  • Probability Measure: \(\mathbb{P}(\{\omega\}) = 2^{-n}\) for each elementary outcome \(\omega \in \Omega\).
  • Indicator Random Variables: \(X_j(\omega) = \omega_j \in \{0, 1\}\) for \(j = 1, \dots, n\).

Each coordinate \(X_j\) takes value 1 for \(2^{n-1}\) outcomes and 0 for \(2^{n-1}\) outcomes, giving: \[ \mathbb{E}[X_j] = \sum_{\omega \in \Omega} X_j(\omega) \mathbb{P}(\omega) = \sum_{\omega \in \Omega} \omega_j 2^{-n} = \frac{1}{2} \]

26.5 Total Number of Heads

Define the total number of heads \(S_n \triangleq \sum_{j=1}^n X_j\). By linearity of expectation: \[ \mathbb{E}[S_n] = \sum_{j=1}^n \mathbb{E}[X_j] = \frac{n}{2} \]

The range of \(S_n(\omega)\) is \(\{0, 1, 2, \dots, n\}\). The probability mass function is: \[ \begin{align*} \mathbb{P}(S_n = k) &= \frac{1}{2^n} \# \{ \omega \in \Omega \mid \omega_1 + \dots + \omega_n = k \} \\ &= \frac{1}{2^n} \binom{n}{k}, \quad k \in \{0, 1, \dots, n\} \end{align*} \]

Thus, \(S_n \sim \text{Bin}\left(n, \frac{1}{2}\right)\).

26.6 Proportion of Heads

Consider the sample mean \(\frac{S_n}{n}\). Its expectation is \(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{S_n}{n}\right] = \frac{1}{2}\).

Since the \(X_j\) are pairwise uncorrelated (shown in Lecture 12.1, with \(\text{Var}(X_j) = \frac{1}{4}\)), Bienaymé’s identity gives: \[ \text{Var}(S_n) = \sum_{j=1}^n \text{Var}(X_j) = n \cdot \frac{1}{4} = \frac{n}{4}. \]

Using \(\text{Var}(cX) = c^2 \text{Var}(X)\) with \(c = 1/n\): \[ \text{Var}\!\left(\frac{S_n}{n}\right) = \frac{1}{n^2} \text{Var}(S_n) = \frac{n/4}{n^2} = \frac{1}{4n}. \]

Applying Chebyshev’s inequality to \(\frac{S_n}{n}\): \[ \mathbb{P}\left(\left| \frac{S_n}{n} - \frac{1}{2} \right| \ge \epsilon\right) \le \frac{\text{Var}(S_n/n)}{\epsilon^2} = \frac{1}{4n\epsilon^2} = \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right). \]

This concentration property is not restricted to binomial distributions; it holds because \(S_n = \sum_{j=1}^n X_j\) is a sum of uncorrelated random variables normalized by \(n\).

26.7 The Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN)

Theorem 26.2 (Weak Law of Large Numbers (Uncorrelated \(L^2\) Case)) Let \(\{X_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\) be a sequence of \(L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) random variables on a common probability space. Suppose \(\{X_n\}\) are pairwise uncorrelated: \[ \text{Cov}(X_n, X_m) = 0 \quad \text{for } n \neq m, \]

and share a common mean \(\alpha \in \mathbb{R}\) and common variance \(t > 0\): \[ \mathbb{E}[X_n] = \alpha, \quad \text{Var}(X_n) = t \quad \forall n \in \mathbb{N}. \]

Let \(S_n \triangleq \sum_{j=1}^n X_j\). Then for any \(\epsilon > 0\): \[ \mathbb{P}\left(\left| \frac{S_n}{n} - \alpha \right| \ge \epsilon\right) \le \frac{t}{n \epsilon^2} = \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right) \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 0. \tag{26.4}\]

Proof (Proof of Equation 26.4). By linearity of expectation: \[ \mathbb{E}[S_n] = \mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{j=1}^n X_j \right] = \sum_{j=1}^n \mathbb{E}[X_j] = n \alpha \implies \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{S_n}{n} \right] = \alpha \]

Because the \(\{X_j\}\) are pairwise uncorrelated, Bienaymé’s identity applies: \[ \text{Var}(S_n) = \text{Var}\left( \sum_{j=1}^n X_j \right) = \sum_{j=1}^n \text{Var}(X_j) = n t \]

Therefore, the variance of the sample average is: \[ \text{Var}\left( \frac{S_n}{n} \right) = \frac{1}{n^2} \text{Var}(S_n) = \frac{n t}{n^2} = \frac{t}{n} \]

Applying Chebyshev’s inequality to \(\frac{S_n}{n}\) yields: \[ \mathbb{P}\left(\left| \frac{S_n}{n} - \alpha \right| \ge \epsilon\right) \le \frac{\text{Var}(S_n/n)}{\epsilon^2} = \frac{t}{n \epsilon^2} \]

Taking \(n \to \infty\) proves the assertion.

26.8 Convergence Modes: Convergence in Probability vs. Almost Sure Convergence

The convergence established by the Weak Law of Large Numbers: \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}\left(\left| \frac{S_n}{n} - \alpha \right| \ge \epsilon\right) = 0 \quad \forall \epsilon > 0 \]

is called convergence in probability, written \(\frac{S_n}{n} \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}} \alpha\).

26.9 Question: Does Convergence in Probability Imply Almost Sure Convergence?

Does \(\frac{S_n}{n} \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}} \alpha\) imply that \(\frac{S_n}{n} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \alpha\), i.e., \(\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{ \omega \in \Omega \mid \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{S_n(\omega)}{n} = \alpha \right\}\right) = 1\)?

Example 26.1 Consider an independent sequence of indicator random variables \(\{X_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\) such that: \[ \mathbb{P}(X_n = 1) = \frac{1}{n}, \quad \mathbb{P}(X_n = 0) = 1 - \frac{1}{n} \]

  1. Convergence in Probability: Since \(X_n \in \{0, 1\}\), for any \(\epsilon > 0\):

    • If \(\epsilon > 1\): \(\{|X_n| \ge \epsilon\} = \emptyset\), so \(\mathbb{P}(|X_n| \ge \epsilon) = 0\).
    • If \(0 < \epsilon \le 1\): \(\{|X_n| \ge \epsilon\} = \{X_n = 1\}\), so \(\mathbb{P}(|X_n| \ge \epsilon) = \frac{1}{n}\).

    In both cases \(\mathbb{P}(|X_n - 0| \ge \epsilon) \le \dfrac{1}{n} \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 0\), so \(X_n \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}} 0\).

  2. Almost Sure Behavior: Summing the probabilities: \[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}(X_n = 1) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n} = \infty \]

    Since \(\{X_n\}\) are independent, the second Borel-Cantelli Lemma (which requires independence) implies: \[ \mathbb{P}\bigl(X_n = 1 \text{ infinitely often (i.o.)}\bigr) = 1. \]

    Consequently, \(X_n(\omega)\) does not converge to \(0\) almost surely, because \(X_n(\omega) = 1\) infinitely many times for almost every \(\omega \in \Omega\).
