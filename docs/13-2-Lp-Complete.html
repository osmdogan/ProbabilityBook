28  Lecture 13.2: \(L^p\) is Complete

28.1 Definition and Norm Structure of \(L^p\) Spaces

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) be a measure space.

Definition 28.1 For \(1 \le p < \infty\), the space \(L^p(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) is defined as the set of equivalence classes of \(\mathcal{F}\)-measurable functions \(f: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\) under \(\mu\)-almost sure equality such that \(|f|^p\) is integrable: \[ L^p(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu) \triangleq \left\{ f \in L^0(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu) \;\middle|\; \int_{\Omega} |f|^p \, d\mu < \infty \right\} \Bigg/ \sim_{\mu\text{-a.s.}} \tag{28.1}\]

Theorem 28.1 (Minkowski’s Inequality and \(L^p\) Norm) For \(1 \le p < \infty\), the function \(\|\cdot\|_{L^p}: L^p(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu) \to [0, \infty)\) defined by: \[ \|f\|_{L^p} \triangleq \left( \int_{\Omega} |f|^p \, d\mu \right)^{1/p} \tag{28.2}\]

defines a norm on \(L^p(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\). In particular, Minkowski’s inequality holds for all \(f, g \in L^p\): \[ \|f + g\|_{L^p} \le \|f\|_{L^p} + \|g\|_{L^p} \tag{28.3}\]

Thus, \(L^p(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) is a normed vector space.

28.2 \(L^p\) Convergence vs. Convergence in Measure

Lemma 28.1 Let \(1 \le p < \infty\) and \(f, f_1, f_2, \dots \in L^p(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\). If \(\|f_n - f\|_{L^p} \to 0\) as \(n \to \infty\), then \(f_n \xrightarrow{\mu} f\).

Proof (Proof of Lemma 28.1). By Markov’s inequality applied to \(|f_n - f|^p\), for any fixed \(\epsilon > 0\): \[ \begin{aligned} &\mu\left( \{ \omega \in \Omega \mid |f_n(\omega) - f(\omega)| \ge \epsilon \} \right) \\ &\le \frac{1}{\epsilon^p} \int_{\Omega} |f_n - f|^p \, d\mu = \frac{1}{\epsilon^p} \|f_n - f\|_{L^p}^p \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 0 \end{aligned} \]

Thus, \(f_n \xrightarrow{\mu} f\).

28.3 Counterexample: The Converse Is False

Convergence in measure does not imply \(L^p\) convergence.

Example 28.1 Consider the Lebesgue measure space \(([0, 1], \mathcal{B}([0, 1]), \lambda)\). Define the sequence: \[ f_n \triangleq n \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\left[0, \frac{1}{n}\right]}, \quad n \in \mathbb{N} \tag{28.4}\] We can express \(f_n\) explicitly as: \[ f_n(x) = \begin{cases} n & \text{if } 0 \le x \le \frac{1}{n}, \\ 0 & \text{if } \frac{1}{n} < x \le 1. \end{cases} \]

Consider the set \(\{x \in [0,1]: |f_n(x)| \ge \epsilon\}\) for any \(\epsilon > 0\). On the interval \([0, 1/n]\), \(|f_n(x)| = n \ge \epsilon\). On the interval \((1/n, 1]\), \(|f_n(x)| = 0 < \epsilon\). Therefore, \(\{x \in [0,1]: |f_n(x)| \ge \epsilon\}= [0, 1/n]\).

  1. Convergence in Measure: For any \(\epsilon > 0\), taking \(n > \epsilon\) yields: \[ \lambda\left( \{ |f_n - 0| \ge \epsilon \} \right) =\lambda\left( \left[0, \frac{1}{n}\right] \right) = \frac{1}{n} \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 0 \] Therefore, \(f_n \xrightarrow{\lambda} 0\).

  2. \(L^p\) Norm Evaluation: For \(1 \le p < \infty\): \[ \|f_n - 0\|_{L^p}^p = \int_{[0, 1]} |f_n|^p \, d\lambda = \int_{0}^{1/n} n^p \, d\lambda = \frac{1}{n} \cdot n^p = n^{p-1} \]

    Taking the \(p\)-th root: \[ \|f_n - 0\|_{L^p} = n^{1 - \frac{1}{p}} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } p = 1, \\ \to \infty & \text{if } p > 1. \end{cases} \]

    In either case, \(\|f_n - 0\|_{L^p} \not\to 0\) as \(n \to \infty\).

28.4 Completeness of \(L^p\) Spaces (Riesz-Fischer Theorem)

Theorem 28.2 (Riesz-Fischer Theorem) For \(1 \le p < \infty\), the space \(L^p(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) is Cauchy complete (i.e., a Banach space). Namely, if \(\{f_n\} \subset L^p\) is an \(L^p\)-Cauchy sequence (\(\|f_n - f_m\|_{L^p} \to 0\) as \(n, m \to \infty\)), there exists \(f \in L^p\) such that \(\|f_n - f\|_{L^p} \to 0\).

Proof (Proof of Theorem 28.2). Step 1: Extracting an Almost Sure Convergent Subsequence. By Lemma 28.1, every \(L^p\)-Cauchy sequence is \(L^0\)-Cauchy in measure: \[ \mu(\{|f_n - f_m| \ge \epsilon\}) \le \frac{1}{\epsilon^p} \|f_n - f_m\|_{L^p}^p \xrightarrow{n, m \to \infty} 0 \]

By \(L^0\) completeness, there exists a subsequence \(\{f_{n_k}\}\) and a measurable function \(f \in L^0\) such that \(f_{n_k} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} f\) as \(k \to \infty\).

Step 2: \(L^p\) Limit Identification via Fatou’s Lemma. Note that \(|f_{n_k} - f_{n_j}|^p \to |f_{n_k} - f|^p\) a.s. as \(j \to \infty\). Then, applying Fatou’s Lemma to the non-negative sequence \(|f_{n_k} - f_{n_j}|^p\) as \(j \to \infty\): \[ \begin{aligned} \|f_{n_k} - f\|_{L^p}^p &= \int_{\Omega} \lim_{j \to \infty} |f_{n_k} - f_{n_j}|^p \, d\mu \\ &\le \liminf_{j \to \infty} \int_{\Omega} |f_{n_k} - f_{n_j}|^p \, d\mu \\ &= \liminf_{j \to \infty} \|f_{n_k} - f_{n_j}\|_{L^p}^p \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\{f_n\}\) is \(L^p\)-Cauchy, for any \(\epsilon > 0\), choosing \(k, j\) sufficiently large makes \(\|f_{n_k} - f_{n_j}\|_{L^p}^p < \epsilon\). Thus: \[ \|f_{n_k} - f\|_{L^p}^p \le \epsilon \implies \lim_{k \to \infty} \|f_{n_k} - f\|_{L^p} = 0 \]

By Minkowski’s inequality, \(\|f\|_{L^p} \le \|f - f_{n_k}\|_{L^p} + \|f_{n_k}\|_{L^p} < \infty\), so \(f \in L^p\).

Step 3: Convergence of Full Sequence. By triangle inequality: \[ \|f_n - f\|_{L^p} \le \|f_n - f_{n_k}\|_{L^p} + \|f_{n_k} - f\|_{L^p} \xrightarrow{n, k \to \infty} 0 \]

28.5 Summary of Modes of Convergence and Relationships

The logical connections between the primary modes of convergence are summarized in the following structural map:

  • \(L^p\) Convergence (\(1 \le p < \infty\)): Implies convergence in measure (\(L^0\) convergence) via Markov’s inequality.
  • Almost Sure Convergence: Implies convergence in measure on finite measure spaces.
  • Convergence in Measure (\(L^0\) Convergence): Implies almost sure convergence of a suitable subsequence.

28.6 General \(L^0\)-\(L^1\) Dominated Convergence Theorem

The following theorem generalizes the classical Dominated Convergence Theorem to the setting where the limit function is only assumed to be in \(L^0\) (measurable) rather than \(L^1\).

Theorem 28.3 (\(L^0\)-\(L^1\) Generalized Dominated Convergence Theorem) Let \(\{f_n\}, \{g_n\} \subset L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\), \(g \in L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\), and \(f \in L^0(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\). Suppose that:

  1. \(|f_n| \le g_n\) \(\mu\)-a.s. for all \(n \in \mathbb{N}\);
  2. \(f_n \xrightarrow{\mu} f\) and \(g_n \xrightarrow{\mu} g\);
  3. \(\int_{\Omega} g_n \, d\mu \to \int_{\Omega} g \, d\mu\) as \(n \to \infty\).

Then \(f \in L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) and \(\|f_n - f\|_{L^1} \to 0\) as \(n \to \infty\). In particular, \(\int_{\Omega} f_n \, d\mu \to \int_{\Omega} f \, d\mu\).

Proof (Proof of Theorem 28.3). Step 1: Dominating Function and Pointwise Subsequences. Because \(f_n \xrightarrow{\mu} f\) and \(g_n \xrightarrow{\mu} g\), we can extract a sub-subsequence \(\{n_k\}\) along which \(f_{n_k} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} f\) and \(g_{n_k} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} g\).

Taking pointwise limits on the inequality \(|f_{n_k}| \le g_{n_k}\) yields \(|f| = \lim_{k \to \infty} |f_{n_k}| \le \lim_{k \to \infty} g_{n_k} = g\) \(\mu\)-a.s. Since \(g \in L^1\), \(f \in L^1\).

Step 2: Subsequence Criterion for \(L^1\) Convergence. Suppose \(\|f_n - f\|_{L^1} \not\to 0\). Then there exists \(\epsilon > 0\) and a subsequence \(\{f_{n_k}\}\) such that \(\|f_{n_k} - f\|_{L^1} \ge \epsilon\) for all \(k\).

However, passing to a further sub-subsequence \(\{n_{k_j}\}\) such that \(f_{n_{k_j}} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} f\) and \(g_{n_{k_j}} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} g\), the classical Generalized Dominated Convergence Theorem applies to \(f_{n_{k_j}}\) dominated by \(g_{n_{k_j}}\). This guarantees \(\|f_{n_{k_j}} - f\|_{L^1} \to 0\), contradicting \(\|f_{n_k} - f\|_{L^1} \ge \epsilon\).

Step 3: Integral Convergence. By triangle inequality: \[ \begin{aligned} \left| \int_{\Omega} f_n \, d\mu - \int_{\Omega} f \, d\mu \right| &= \left| \int_{\Omega} (f_n - f) \, d\mu \right| \\ &\le \int_{\Omega} |f_n - f| \, d\mu \\ &= \|f_n - f\|_{L^1} \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 0 \end{aligned} \]
