33  Lecture 15.2: Independent Random Variables

33.1 Definition and \(\pi\)-System Criteria for Independence

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\) be a probability space, and let \(X_i: (\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P) \to (S_i, \mathcal{B}_i)\) be random variables taking values in measurable spaces \((S_i, \mathcal{B}_i)\) for \(i \in I\).

  • Generated \(\sigma\)-Algebra: \(\sigma(X_i) \triangleq X_i^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_i) = \{ X_i^{-1}(B_i) \mid B_i \in \mathcal{B}_i \}\).

Definition 33.1 The random variables \(\{ X_i \}_{i \in I}\) are independent if the generated \(\sigma\)-algebras \(\{ \sigma(X_i) \}_{i \in I}\) are independent.

Equivalently, for any finite subset \(\{ i_1, \dots, i_n \} \subseteq I\) of distinct indices and any sets \(B_k \in \mathcal{B}_{i_k}\): \[ P(X_{i_1} \in B_1, X_{i_2} \in B_2, \dots, X_{i_n} \in B_n) = \prod_{k=1}^n P(X_{i_k} \in B_k). \] Note that the condition ranges over all finite subsets of \(I\), not merely over initial segments; when \(I = \{1, \dots, N\}\) or \(I = \mathbb{N}\) we abbreviate \(\{i_1, \dots, i_n\}\) by \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) whenever no confusion arises.

  • Equivalently, \(\{ X_i \}_{i \in I}\) are independent if and only if, for every finite collection \(\{i_1, \dots, i_n\} \subseteq I\) of distinct indices, we have \[ P\left(\bigcap_{k=1}^n X_{i_k}^{-1}(B_k)\right) = \prod_{k=1}^n P\left(X_{i_k}^{-1}(B_k)\right), \] for all \(B_k \in \mathcal{B}_{i_k}\).

33.2 The \(\pi\)-System Criterion for Random Variables

The following lemma gives a convenient criterion for checking independence of random variables by verifying the independence condition on a \(\pi\)-system generating the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra of each random variable’s codomain.

Lemma 33.1 For each \(i \in I\), let \(\mathcal{E}_i \subseteq \mathcal{B}_i\) be a \(\pi\)-system such that \(\sigma(\mathcal{E}_i) = \mathcal{B}_i\). Then \(\{ X_i \}_{i \in I}\) are independent if and only if, for every finite collection \(\{i_1, \dots, i_n\} \subseteq I\) of distinct indices and all \(E_k \in \mathcal{E}_{i_k}\): \[ P(X_{i_1} \in E_1, X_{i_2} \in E_2, \dots, X_{i_n} \in E_n) = \prod_{k=1}^n P(X_{i_k} \in E_k). \]

Proof (Proof of Lemma 33.1). \(({\Rightarrow})\) If \(\{X_i\}\) are independent, then for every finite collection of distinct indices \(i_1,\ldots,i_n\), \[ P(X_{i_1} \in B_1, \ldots, X_{i_n} \in B_n) = \prod_{k=1}^n P(X_{i_k} \in B_k) \] for all \(B_k \in \mathcal{B}_{i_k}\). Since \(\mathcal{E}_{i_k} \subseteq \mathcal{B}_{i_k}\), the identity holds in particular for all \(E_k \in \mathcal{E}_{i_k}\).

\(({\Leftarrow})\) Define \(\mathcal{C}_i \triangleq X_i^{-1}(\mathcal{E}_i) = \{ X_i^{-1}(E_i) \mid E_i \in \mathcal{E}_i \}\).

  1. \(\mathcal{C}_i\) is a \(\pi\)-system on \(\Omega\) because \(X_i^{-1}(E_1) \cap X_i^{-1}(E_2) = X_i^{-1}(E_1 \cap E_2) \in \mathcal{C}_i\) for \(E_1, E_2 \in \mathcal{E}_i\).
  2. \(\sigma(\mathcal{C}_i) = \sigma(X_i^{-1}(\mathcal{E}_i)) = X_i^{-1}(\sigma(\mathcal{E}_i)) = X_i^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_i) = \sigma(X_i)\).
  3. For every finite collection of distinct indices \(i_1,\ldots,i_n\), the hypothesis says precisely that the \(\pi\)-systems \(\mathcal{C}_{i_1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{i_n}\) are independent. By Theorem 32.1, their generated \(\sigma\)-algebras \(\sigma(\mathcal{C}_{i_k}) = \sigma(X_{i_k})\) are independent. Since this holds for every finite subfamily, the family \(\{\sigma(X_i)\}_{i\in I}\) is independent.

The following corollary gives a convenient criterion for checking independence of real-valued random variables by verifying the independence condition on their cumulative distribution functions (CDFs).

Corollary 33.1 Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be \(\mathbb{R}\)-valued random variables. Taking \(\mathcal{E}_i = \{ (-\infty, t] \mid t \in \mathbb{R} \}\), \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are independent if and only if their joint cumulative distribution function (CDF) factorizes into the product of their marginal CDFs: \[ F_{X_1, \dots, X_n}(t_1, \dots, t_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n F_{X_i}(t_i) \quad \forall (t_1, \dots, t_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n \]

Proof. The forward implication follows from independence. Conversely, fix a nonempty subset \(J \subseteq \{1,\ldots,n\}\) and \(t_j \in \mathbb{R}\) for \(j\in J\). For \(r\in\mathbb{R}\), apply the assumed CDF factorization with threshold \(t_j\) in coordinates \(j\in J\) and threshold \(r\) in the remaining coordinates. As \(r\to\infty\), the corresponding events increase to \(\bigcap_{j\in J}\{X_j\le t_j\}\), so continuity from below and \(F_{X_\ell}(r)\to1\) give \[ P\left(\bigcap_{j\in J}\{X_j\le t_j\}\right) = \prod_{j\in J}F_{X_j}(t_j). \] Thus the lower-half-line \(\pi\)-system factorization holds for every finite subfamily. The reverse direction of Lemma 33.1 now gives independence.

33.3 Joint Law Factorization and Equivalent Criteria

Given \(X = (X_1, \dots, X_n)\) with \(X_i: (\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P) \to (S_i, \mathcal{B}_i)\), the joint law \(\mu_X\) is the pushforward probability measure on \(\bigotimes_{i=1}^n \mathcal{B}_i\) defined by \(\mu_X \triangleq P \circ X^{-1}\), so that \(\mu_X(B) = P(X \in B)\).

Theorem 33.1 The random variables \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are independent if and only if their joint distribution equals the product of their marginal distributions: \[ \mu_{(X_1, \dots, X_n)} = \mu_{X_1} \otimes \mu_{X_2} \otimes \dots \otimes \mu_{X_n} \tag{33.1}\]

Proof. \((\Leftarrow)\) If \(\mu_X = \bigotimes_{i=1}^n \mu_{X_i}\), then for any product rectangle \(B_1 \times \dots \times B_n\) with \(B_i \in \mathcal{B}_i\): \[ \begin{aligned} P(X_1 \in B_1, \dots, X_n \in B_n) &= \mu_X(B_1 \times \dots \times B_n) \\ &= \prod_{i=1}^n \mu_{X_i}(B_i) = \prod_{i=1}^n P(X_i \in B_i) \end{aligned} \]

\((\Rightarrow)\) If \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are independent, \(\mu_X\) and \(\bigotimes_{i=1}^n \mu_{X_i}\) agree on all product rectangles \(B_1 \times \dots \times B_n\). Because product rectangles form a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\bigotimes_{i=1}^n \mathcal{B}_i\), uniqueness of extension implies the measures are identical by Lemma 32.1.

33.4 General Equivalent Criteria for Independence

The following theorem gives several equivalent criteria for independence of random variables, including the factorization of expectations of products of bounded measurable functions.

Theorem 33.2 Let \(X_i: (\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P) \to (S_i, \mathcal{B}_i)\) for \(i \in \{1, \dots, n\}\). The following conditions are equivalent:

  1. \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are independent.
  2. \(\mu_{(X_1, \dots, X_n)} = \bigotimes_{i=1}^n \mu_{X_i}\).
  3. For all bounded measurable functions \(f_i \in \mathbb{B}(S_i, \mathcal{B}_i)\): \[ \mathbb{E}\left[ \prod_{i=1}^n f_i(X_i) \right] = \prod_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[f_i(X_i)] \tag{33.2}\]

If \(S_i = \mathbb{R}^{d_i}\) with Borel \(\sigma\)-algebras, the following are also equivalent:

  1. Condition Equation 33.2 holds for all compactly supported continuous functions \(f_i \in C_c(\mathbb{R}^{d_i})\).
  2. Condition Equation 33.2 holds for all indicator functions of lower unbounded rectangles \(f_i = \mathbf{1}_{(-\infty, t_i]}\).

Proof (Proof of Theorem 33.2).

  • \((1 \iff 2)\): Proven in Theorem 33.1.
  • \((2 \implies 3)\): By the change-of-variables formula \(\mathbb{E}[g(X_1,\ldots,X_n)] = \int g\,d\mu_{(X_1,\ldots,X_n)}\) and condition 2: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^n f_i(X_i)\right] &= \int_{S_1 \times \dots \times S_n} \prod_{i=1}^n f_i(x_i) \, \mu_{(X_1,\ldots,X_n)}(dx) \\ &= \int_{S_1 \times \dots \times S_n} \prod_{i=1}^n f_i(x_i) \, (\mu_{X_1} \otimes \dots \otimes \mu_{X_n})(dx). \end{aligned} \] Since each \(f_i\) is bounded measurable and \(\mu_{X_i}\) is a probability measure, \(\prod_i |f_i|\) is bounded and the product measure is finite, so \(\prod_i f_i \in L^1(\bigotimes_i \mu_{X_i})\). Fubini’s theorem factors the product-space integral: \[ \begin{aligned} &\int_{S_1 \times \dots \times S_n} \prod_{i=1}^n f_i(x_i) \, (\mu_{X_1} \otimes \dots \otimes \mu_{X_n})(dx) \\ &= \prod_{i=1}^n \int_{S_i} f_i(x_i) \, \mu_{X_i}(dx_i) = \prod_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[f_i(X_i)]. \end{aligned} \]
  • \((3 \implies 1)\): For \(B_i \in \mathcal{B}_i\), take \(f_i = \mathbf{1}_{B_i}\) (bounded measurable). Then \(\mathbb{E}[f_i(X_i)] = P(X_i \in B_i)\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\prod_i f_i(X_i)] = P(X_1 \in B_1, \dots, X_n \in B_n)\). Condition 3 then gives \(P(X_1 \in B_1, \ldots, X_n \in B_n) = \prod_i P(X_i \in B_i)\) for all \(B_i \in \mathcal{B}_i\), which is condition 1.
  • \((4 \implies 3)\): Let \(\mathbb{M} = \bigl\{(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\mapsto\prod_{i=1}^n f_i(x_i) \mid f_i \in C_c(\mathbb{R}^{d_i})\bigr\}\). This is a multiplicative system on \(\prod_i \mathbb{R}^{d_i}\) (the pointwise product of two such functions is again in \(\mathbb{M}\)). Its members are continuous, separate points, and for every point at least one member is nonzero there; hence \(\sigma(\mathbb{M}) = \bigotimes_i \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d_i})\). Define \[ \mathbb{H} = \bigl\{g \in \mathbb{B}\bigl(\textstyle\prod_i \mathbb{R}^{d_i},\, \bigotimes_i \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d_i})\bigr) : \mathbb{E}[g(X_1,\ldots,X_n)] = \int g\,d(\bigotimes_i \mu_{X_i})\bigr\}. \] Then \(\mathbb{H}\) is a vector space containing \(1\). If \(g_m\in\mathbb{H}\), \(g_m\to g\) pointwise, and \(\sup_m\|g_m\|_\infty<\infty\), DCT under both probability measures shows that \(g\in\mathbb{H}\). To verify \(\mathbb{M}\subseteq\mathbb{H}\): for \(g = \prod_i f_i \in \mathbb{M}\) with \(f_i \in C_c(\mathbb{R}^{d_i})\), condition 4 gives \(\mathbb{E}[g(X_1,\ldots,X_n)] = \prod_i \mathbb{E}[f_i(X_i)]\), while Fubini applied to the product probability measure gives \(\int g\,d(\bigotimes_i \mu_{X_i}) = \prod_i \int_{S_i} f_i\,d\mu_{X_i} = \prod_i \mathbb{E}[f_i(X_i)]\); both sides agree, so \(g \in \mathbb{H}\). By Dynkin’s MST, \(\mathbb{H}\) contains all bounded \(\bigotimes_i\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d_i})\)-measurable functions; taking \(g=\prod_i f_i\) with arbitrary bounded measurable \(f_i\) gives condition 3.
  • \((5 \implies 3)\): The collection \(\mathcal{E}_i = \{(-\infty, t_i] : t_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_i}\}\) of lower-orthant sets is a \(\pi\)-system (since \((-\infty,s]\cap(-\infty,t]=(-\infty,s\wedge t]\)) that generates \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d_i})\). Condition 5 gives \(P(X_1 \in E_1, \ldots, X_n \in E_n) = \prod_i P(X_i \in E_i)\) for all \(E_i \in \mathcal{E}_i\). By Lemma 33.1 this is equivalent to condition 1, and condition \(1 \implies 3\) was established above.

33.5 Properties of Independent Random Variables

The following proposition gives a useful property of independent random variables: the expectation of their product factorizes into the product of their expectations.

Proposition 33.1 (Expectation Factorization for Independent Random Variables) If \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are independent real-valued random variables in \(L^1\), then \(\prod_{i=1}^n X_i \in L^1\) and: \[ \mathbb{E}\left[ \prod_{i=1}^n X_i \right] = \prod_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[X_i] \tag{33.3}\]

Proof. Since \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) are independent, Theorem 33.1 gives \(\mu_{(X_1,\ldots,X_n)} = \mu_{X_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mu_{X_n}\). By the change-of-variables formula (\(\mathbb{E}[g(X_1,\ldots,X_n)] = \int g\,d\mu_{(X_1,\ldots,X_n)}\)) and Tonelli’s theorem applied to the non-negative integrand \(\prod |x_i|\): \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[ \left| \prod_{i=1}^n X_i \right| \right] &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \prod_{i=1}^n |x_i| \, (\mu_{X_1} \otimes \dots \otimes \mu_{X_n})(dx) \\ &= \prod_{i=1}^n \int_{\mathbb{R}} |x_i| \, \mu_{X_i}(dx_i) \\ &= \prod_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[|X_i|] < \infty \end{aligned} \] where the second equality uses Tonelli’s theorem to factor the product-space integral into a product of marginal integrals. Since \(\prod X_i\) is absolutely integrable, Fubini’s theorem applies to the signed function \(\prod X_i\) and yields Equation 33.3 by the same factorization.

Corollary 33.2 If \(X, Y\) are independent real-valued random variables in \(L^2\), then \(\text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0\).

Uncorrelatedness Does Not Imply Independence:

Let \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) and let \(Z\) be a discrete random variable independent of \(X\) with \(P(Z = 1) = P(Z = -1) = \frac{1}{2}\). Define \(Y \triangleq X Z\).

  1. Covariance: \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Cov}(X, Y) &= \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[X^2 Z] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[XZ] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[X^2]\mathbb{E}[Z] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Z] = 0, \end{aligned} \] since \(\mathbb{E}[Z] = 0\). Thus, \(X\) and \(Y\) are uncorrelated.
  2. Dependence via Higher Moments: \[ \mathbb{E}[X^2 Y^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2 \cdot X^2 Z^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^4 Z^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^4] = 3, \] where the third equality uses \(Z^2 = 1\) a.s. (since \(Z \in \{1,-1\}\)), and \(\mathbb{E}[X^4] = 3\) is the fourth moment of a standard normal. However, \[ \mathbb{E}[X^2]\,\mathbb{E}[Y^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2]\cdot \mathbb{E}[X^2 Z^2] = 1 \cdot \mathbb{E}[X^2]\mathbb{E}[Z^2] = 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 1, \] where \(\mathbb{E}[X^2 Z^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2]\mathbb{E}[Z^2]\) by independence of \(X\) and \(Z\), and \(\mathbb{E}[Z^2] = 1\) since \(Z^2 = 1\) a.s. Since \(\mathbb{E}[X^2 Y^2] = 3 \neq 1 = \mathbb{E}[X^2] \mathbb{E}[Y^2]\), \(X\) and \(Y\) are not independent.

33.6 Block Groupings and Method of Moments

The following lemma gives a useful property of independent \(\sigma\)-algebras: if we group independent \(\sigma\)-algebras into blocks, the block-generated \(\sigma\)-algebras are also independent.

Lemma 33.2 If \(\mathcal{F}_1, \dots, \mathcal{F}_n\) are independent \(\sigma\)-algebras, \(k\in\mathbb{N}\), and \(n_1,\ldots,n_k\in\mathbb{N}\) satisfy \(n = n_1 + \dots + n_k\), then the block-generated \(\sigma\)-algebras: \[ \mathcal{G}_1 \triangleq \sigma(\mathcal{F}_1 \cup \dots \cup \mathcal{F}_{n_1}), \quad \mathcal{G}_2 \triangleq \sigma(\mathcal{F}_{n_1+1} \cup \dots \cup \mathcal{F}_{n_1+n_2}), \quad \dots \] are independent.

Proof (Proof of Lemma 33.2). Let \(N_0 = 0\) and \(N_j = n_1 + \cdots + n_j\) for \(j = 1, \ldots, k\), so block \(j\) consists of \(\mathcal{F}_{N_{j-1}+1}, \ldots, \mathcal{F}_{N_j}\).

Step 1: Define a \(\pi\)-system for each block. For each \(j \in \{1, \ldots, k\}\), define: \[ \mathcal{C}_j \triangleq \left\{ \bigcap_{i=N_{j-1}+1}^{N_j} A_i \;\middle|\; A_i \in \mathcal{F}_i \text{ for each } i \in (N_{j-1}, N_j] \right\}. \] In words, \(\mathcal{C}_j\) consists of all finite intersections of one set chosen from each \(\mathcal{F}_i\) in block \(j\).

Step 2: \(\mathcal{C}_j\) is a \(\pi\)-system. Given \(C = \bigcap_i A_i\) and \(C' = \bigcap_i A_i'\) in \(\mathcal{C}_j\): \[ C \cap C' = \bigcap_{i=N_{j-1}+1}^{N_j} (A_i \cap A_i') \in \mathcal{C}_j, \] since \(A_i \cap A_i' \in \mathcal{F}_i\) for each \(i\). In particular \(\Omega \in \mathcal{C}_j\) (take every \(A_i = \Omega \in \mathcal{F}_i\)).

Step 3: \(\sigma(\mathcal{C}_j) = \mathcal{G}_j\). Since every set in \(\mathcal{C}_j\) is a finite intersection of sets in \(\bigcup_{i \in \text{block}_j} \mathcal{F}_i \subseteq \mathcal{G}_j\), we have \(\mathcal{C}_j \subseteq \mathcal{G}_j\), hence \(\sigma(\mathcal{C}_j) \subseteq \mathcal{G}_j\). For the reverse inclusion, fix any \(i_0 \in (N_{j-1}, N_j]\) and \(A_{i_0} \in \mathcal{F}_{i_0}\). Setting \(A_i = \Omega\) for all \(i \neq i_0\) in the block shows \(A_{i_0} \in \mathcal{C}_j\). Therefore \(\mathcal{F}_{i_0} \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{C}_j)\) for every \(i_0\) in block \(j\), giving: \[ \mathcal{G}_j = \sigma\!\left(\bigcup_{i=N_{j-1}+1}^{N_j} \mathcal{F}_i\right) \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{C}_j). \]

Step 4: \(\mathcal{C}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{C}_k\) are independent. Take any \(C_j = \bigcap_{i \in \text{block}_j} A_i \in \mathcal{C}_j\) for each \(j\), with \(A_i \in \mathcal{F}_i\). Then: \[ \bigcap_{j=1}^k C_j = \bigcap_{j=1}^k \bigcap_{i \in \text{block}_j} A_i = \bigcap_{i=1}^n A_i. \] By independence of \(\mathcal{F}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{F}_n\) (applied to the full family \(\{A_i\}_{i=1}^n\)): \[ P\!\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^k C_j\right) = P\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^n A_i\right) = \prod_{i=1}^n P(A_i). \] For each individual block, independence of \(\mathcal{F}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{F}_n\) (restricted to block \(j\)) gives: \[ P(C_j) = P\!\left(\bigcap_{i \in \text{block}_j} A_i\right) = \prod_{i \in \text{block}_j} P(A_i). \] Therefore: \[ \prod_{j=1}^k P(C_j) = \prod_{j=1}^k \prod_{i \in \text{block}_j} P(A_i) = \prod_{i=1}^n P(A_i) = P\!\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^k C_j\right). \] Since \(\Omega \in \mathcal{C}_j\) for every \(j\), the convenient-notation form of the independence definition (Equation 32.4) shows \(\mathcal{C}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{C}_k\) are independent.

Conclusion. By Theorem 32.1, since \(\mathcal{C}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{C}_k\) are independent \(\pi\)-systems with \(\sigma(\mathcal{C}_j) = \mathcal{G}_j\), the \(\sigma\)-algebras \(\mathcal{G}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{G}_k\) are independent.

Corollary 33.3 Let \(X_i:(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\to(S_i,\mathcal{B}_i)\), \(i=1,\ldots,n\), be independent random variables, let \(1\le n_1<n\), and let \[ f_1:\prod_{i=1}^{n_1}(S_i,\mathcal{B}_i)\to(T_1,\mathcal{T}_1),\qquad f_2:\prod_{i=n_1+1}^{n}(S_i,\mathcal{B}_i)\to(T_2,\mathcal{T}_2) \] be measurable. Then the random variables \(Y_1 = f_1(X_1, \dots, X_{n_1})\) and \(Y_2 = f_2(X_{n_1+1}, \dots, X_n)\) are independent.

Proof (Proof of Corollary 33.3). Let \(n_2 = n - n_1\), so the two blocks are \(\{1, \ldots, n_1\}\) and \(\{n_1+1, \ldots, n\}\).

Step 1: Block \(\sigma\)-algebras are independent. Since \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) are independent, the generated \(\sigma\)-algebras \(\sigma(X_1), \ldots, \sigma(X_n)\) are independent. By Lemma 33.2 with \(k = 2\) and block sizes \(n_1\) and \(n_2\), the block-generated \(\sigma\)-algebras \[ \mathcal{G}_1 \triangleq \sigma\!\bigl(\sigma(X_1) \cup \cdots \cup \sigma(X_{n_1})\bigr), \qquad \mathcal{G}_2 \triangleq \sigma\!\bigl(\sigma(X_{n_1+1}) \cup \cdots \cup \sigma(X_n)\bigr) \] are independent.

Step 2: Each \(Y_j\) is \(\mathcal{G}_j\)-measurable. Every \(X_i\) with \(i \le n_1\) is \(\sigma(X_i)\)-measurable, and \(\sigma(X_i) \subseteq \mathcal{G}_1\), so \((X_1, \ldots, X_{n_1})\) is \(\mathcal{G}_1\)-measurable as a map into \(\prod_{i=1}^{n_1} S_i\) (by Lemma 30.3 applied on \(\Omega\)). Since \(f_1\) is measurable, \(Y_1 = f_1(X_1, \ldots, X_{n_1})\) is \(\mathcal{G}_1\)-measurable, i.e., \(\sigma(Y_1) \subseteq \mathcal{G}_1\). The same argument gives \(\sigma(Y_2) \subseteq \mathcal{G}_2\).

Conclusion. For any \(A \in \sigma(Y_1) \subseteq \mathcal{G}_1\) and \(B \in \sigma(Y_2) \subseteq \mathcal{G}_2\), independence of \(\mathcal{G}_1\) and \(\mathcal{G}_2\) gives \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)\). Hence \(Y_1\) and \(Y_2\) are independent. The same argument applied with \(k \ge 2\) blocks yields independence of \(k\) such block functions.

  • Example: If \(X_1, X_2, X_3, X_4, X_5\) are independent real-valued random variables, then \(X_1 + X_2\), \(X_3 X_4\), and \(e^{X_5}\) are mutually independent.

33.7 Method of Moments for Bounded Random Variables

Proposition 33.2 Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be real-valued random variables such that \(|X_i| \le M\) a.s. for all \(i\) and some \(M\in[0,\infty)\). Then \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are independent if and only if for all non-negative integers \(k_1, \dots, k_n \in \mathbb{N}_0\): \[ \mathbb{E}\left[ X_1^{k_1} X_2^{k_2} \dots X_n^{k_n} \right] = \prod_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}\left[ X_i^{k_i} \right] \tag{33.4}\]

Proof (Proof of Proposition 33.2). \((\Rightarrow)\) Since \(|X_i| \le M\) a.s., define \(\tilde{f}_i(x) \triangleq x^{k_i}\mathbf{1}_{|x| \le M}\), which is bounded (by \(M^{k_i}\)) and measurable, and satisfies \(\tilde{f}_i(X_i) = X_i^{k_i}\) a.s. Applying Equation 33.2 to \(\tilde{f}_1,\ldots,\tilde{f}_n\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[\prod_i X_i^{k_i}] = \mathbb{E}[\prod_i \tilde{f}_i(X_i)] = \prod_i \mathbb{E}[\tilde{f}_i(X_i)] = \prod_i \mathbb{E}[X_i^{k_i}]\).

\((\Leftarrow)\)

Step 1: Polynomial factorization. For polynomials \(p_i(x_i) = \sum_{k_i} a_{k_i}^{(i)} x_i^{k_i}\), expanding by multi-linearity of \(\mathbb{E}\) over the finite sum and then applying the hypothesis Equation 33.4: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\prod_i p_i(X_i)\Bigr] &= \sum_{k_1,\ldots,k_n}\Bigl(\prod_i a_{k_i}^{(i)}\Bigr) \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\prod_i X_i^{k_i}\Bigr] \\ &= \sum_{k_1,\ldots,k_n}\Bigl(\prod_i a_{k_i}^{(i)}\Bigr)\prod_i \mathbb{E}\bigl[X_i^{k_i}\bigr] \\ &= \prod_i \Bigl(\sum_{k_i} a_{k_i}^{(i)} \mathbb{E}\bigl[X_i^{k_i}\bigr]\Bigr) = \prod_i \mathbb{E}[p_i(X_i)]. \end{aligned} \] The last refactoring separates the sum over all multi-indices \((k_1,\ldots,k_n)\) into a product of single-index sums.

Step 2: Extension to \(C_c(\mathbb{R})\) by Stone-Weierstrass. For general \(f_i \in C_c(\mathbb{R})\), by the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem choose polynomials \(p_m^{(i)}\) such that \(\|p_m^{(i)} - f_i\|_{[-M,M],\infty} \to 0\). Since convergent sequences of real numbers are bounded, \(C_i \triangleq \sup_{m \ge 1} \|p_m^{(i)}\|_{[-M,M],\infty} < \infty\). Because \(|X_i| \le M\) a.s., this gives the uniform bound \(\bigl|\prod_i p_m^{(i)}(X_i)\bigr| \le \prod_i C_i < \infty\) for all \(m\).

Since uniform convergence on \([-M,M]\) and \(|X_i| \le M\) a.s. imply \(p_m^{(i)}(X_i) \to f_i(X_i)\) a.s., the Bounded Convergence Theorem gives: \[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_i p_m^{(i)}(X_i)\right] \xrightarrow{m\to\infty} \mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_i f_i(X_i)\right]. \] Applying BCT to each factor separately gives \(\mathbb{E}[p_m^{(i)}(X_i)] \to \mathbb{E}[f_i(X_i)]\), and hence \(\prod_i \mathbb{E}[p_m^{(i)}(X_i)] \to \prod_i \mathbb{E}[f_i(X_i)]\) by continuity of finite products.

Since \(\mathbb{E}[\prod_i p_m^{(i)}(X_i)] = \prod_i \mathbb{E}[p_m^{(i)}(X_i)]\) for every \(m\) (Step 1), equality holds in the limit: \(\mathbb{E}[\prod_i f_i(X_i)] = \prod_i \mathbb{E}[f_i(X_i)]\).

Conclusion. This establishes Condition 4 of Theorem 33.2 for all \(f_i \in C_c(\mathbb{R})\), from which independence of \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) follows.

33.8 Takeaways

  1. Independence is independence of information. A family \(\{X_i\}_{i\in I}\) is independent precisely when the generated \(\sigma\)-algebras \(\{\sigma(X_i)\}_{i\in I}\) are independent. Equivalently, the probability factorization must hold for every finite collection of distinct indices and measurable sets in their respective state spaces.

  2. It suffices to test generating classes. To prove independence, one may verify the factorization on a \(\pi\)-system generating each state-space \(\sigma\)-algebra, provided the test is made for every finite subfamily. For real-valued random variables, this yields the CDF criterion: independence is equivalent to factorization of the joint CDF into the marginal CDFs.

  3. Independence is product factorization of the joint law. For a finite family, \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) are independent if and only if \[ \mu_{(X_1,\ldots,X_n)}=\mu_{X_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{X_n}. \] Consequently, independence is also equivalent to factorization of expectations of products of bounded measurable functions.

  4. Products factor when integrability is available. If independent real-valued random variables \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) belong to \(L^1\), then their product is in \(L^1\) and \[ \mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^n X_i\right]=\prod_{i=1}^n\mathbb{E}[X_i]. \] In particular, independent \(L^2\) random variables are uncorrelated, but the converse fails.

  5. Independence survives measurable transformations on disjoint blocks. Independent \(\sigma\)-algebras remain independent after grouping them into disjoint blocks. Therefore, measurable functions of disjoint blocks of independent random variables are themselves independent.

  6. For bounded real-valued variables, all mixed moments characterize independence. If \(|X_i|\le M\) a.s. for a common finite \(M\), then independence is equivalent to factorization of every mixed moment. The converse uses polynomial approximation on \([-M,M]\), bounded convergence, and the bounded continuous-function criterion.
