34.1 Construction of Finite Independent Families

Definition 34.1 Let \((S, \mathcal{B})\) be a measurable space and let \(X_n : (\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P) \to (S, \mathcal{B})\), \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), be measurable maps defined on a common probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\), with laws (pushforward measures) \(\mu_{X_n} \triangleq P \circ X_n^{-1}\). The sequence \(\{X_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) is independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) if:

  1. Independence. \(\{X_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) is independent in the sense of Definition 33.1; explicitly, for every finite subfamily \(X_{n_1}, \dots, X_{n_k}\) with \(n_1 < \dots < n_k\) and all \(B_1, \dots, B_k \in \mathcal{B}\), \[ P\Bigl( \bigcap_{i=1}^k \{ X_{n_i} \in B_i \} \Bigr) = \prod_{i=1}^k P(X_{n_i} \in B_i). \]
  2. Identical distribution. There is a single probability measure \(\mu\) on \((S, \mathcal{B})\) with \(\mu_{X_n} = \mu\) for all \(n \in \mathbb{N}\).

Independence of an infinite family is thus a condition on finite subfamilies only. Unless stated otherwise we take \((S, \mathcal{B}) = (\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\); nothing in the definition requires this choice.

To construct finite collections of independent random variables with prescribed probability laws \(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_N\), we use product probability spaces. Since Corollary 30.1 constructs only the two-factor product, we first record the \(N\)-fold construction, which also requires identifying the \(N\)-fold product \(\sigma\)-algebra of Definition 30.1 (defined directly by \(N\)-fold rectangles) with an iterated two-factor product.

Proposition 34.1 (Existence and Uniqueness of Finite Product Measures) Let \((S_1, \mathcal{B}_1, \mu_1), \dots, (S_N, \mathcal{B}_N, \mu_N)\) be \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces with \(N \ge 2\), and set \(S \triangleq \prod_{n=1}^N S_n\). Then, under the canonical bijection \(S \cong \bigl( \prod_{n=1}^{N-1} S_n \bigr) \times S_N\), \[ \bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mathcal{B}_n = \Bigl( \bigotimes_{n=1}^{N-1} \mathcal{B}_n \Bigr) \otimes \mathcal{B}_N , \tag{34.1}\] and there exists a unique measure \(\bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mu_n\) on \(\bigl( S, \bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mathcal{B}_n \bigr)\) satisfying \[ \Bigl( \bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mu_n \Bigr)(B_1 \times \dots \times B_N) = \prod_{n=1}^N \mu_n(B_n) \quad \forall\, B_n \in \mathcal{B}_n . \tag{34.2}\] This measure is \(\sigma\)-finite, and it is a probability measure whenever every \(\mu_n\) is.

Proof (Proof of Proposition 34.1). Associativity of the product \(\sigma\)-algebra (Equation 34.1). Write \(\mathcal{C} \triangleq \bigl( \bigotimes_{n=1}^{N-1} \mathcal{B}_n \bigr) \otimes \mathcal{B}_N\).

(\(\subseteq\)) Every \(N\)-fold rectangle satisfies \(B_1 \times \dots \times B_N = (B_1 \times \dots \times B_{N-1}) \times B_N\), a two-factor rectangle whose first side lies in \(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{N-1} \mathcal{B}_n\). Hence all \(N\)-fold rectangles lie in \(\mathcal{C}\), and since \(\bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mathcal{B}_n\) is generated by these rectangles (Definition 30.1), \(\bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mathcal{B}_n \subseteq \mathcal{C}\).

(\(\supseteq\)) By Lemma 30.1 each coordinate projection \(\pi_n : S \to S_n\) is \(\bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mathcal{B}_n\)-measurable, so by Lemma 30.3 the map \(\pi_{1:N-1} \triangleq (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_{N-1}) : S \to \prod_{n=1}^{N-1} S_n\) is \(\bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mathcal{B}_n \,/\, \bigotimes_{n=1}^{N-1} \mathcal{B}_n\)-measurable. Under the canonical bijection \(S \cong \bigl( \prod_{n=1}^{N-1} S_n \bigr) \times S_N\) the two coordinate projections of the two-factor product are exactly \(\pi_{1:N-1}\) and \(\pi_N\), so Lemma 30.2 applied with \(d = 2\) gives \[ \mathcal{C} = \sigma\bigl( \{ \pi_{1:N-1}^{-1}(A) \mid A \in \textstyle\bigotimes_{n=1}^{N-1} \mathcal{B}_n \} \cup \{ \pi_N^{-1}(B_N) \mid B_N \in \mathcal{B}_N \} \bigr). \] Each generator lies in \(\bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mathcal{B}_n\): the sets \(\pi_{1:N-1}^{-1}(A)\) by the measurability of \(\pi_{1:N-1}\) just established, and the sets \(\pi_N^{-1}(B_N)\) by Lemma 30.1. Hence \(\mathcal{C} \subseteq \bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mathcal{B}_n\).

Existence. Induct on \(N\). For \(N = 2\) the assertion is Corollary 30.1, which also shows that the product of two \(\sigma\)-finite measures is \(\sigma\)-finite. For \(N > 2\), let \(\nu \triangleq \bigotimes_{n=1}^{N-1} \mu_n\) be the (\(\sigma\)-finite) measure supplied by the induction hypothesis and define \[ \bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mu_n \triangleq \nu \otimes \mu_N , \] which is a measure on \(\bigl( \bigotimes_{n=1}^{N-1} \mathcal{B}_n \bigr) \otimes \mathcal{B}_N = \bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mathcal{B}_n\) by Equation 34.1, and is \(\sigma\)-finite by Corollary 30.1. On rectangles, \[ (\nu \otimes \mu_N)(B_1 \times \dots \times B_N) = \nu(B_1 \times \dots \times B_{N-1}) \, \mu_N(B_N) = \prod_{n=1}^N \mu_n(B_n), \] using the induction hypothesis. If every \(\mu_n\) is a probability measure, then taking \(B_n = S_n\) gives \(\bigl( \bigotimes_n \mu_n \bigr)(S) = \prod_n \mu_n(S_n) = 1\).

Uniqueness. The collection \(\mathcal{R} \triangleq \bigl\{ \prod_{n=1}^N B_n \mid B_n \in \mathcal{B}_n \bigr\}\) is a \(\pi\)-system, since \(\prod_n B_n \cap \prod_n B_n' = \prod_n (B_n \cap B_n')\), and \(\sigma(\mathcal{R}) = \bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mathcal{B}_n\) by Definition 30.1. By \(\sigma\)-finiteness choose, for each \(n\), an increasing sequence \(A^{(n)}_m \nearrow S_n\) with \(\mu_n(A^{(n)}_m) < \infty\) (replace a given countable cover by the unions of its initial segments, which still have finite measure by finite subadditivity). Put \[ R_m \triangleq \prod_{n=1}^N A^{(n)}_m \in \mathcal{R} . \] Then \(R_m \nearrow S\) and any measure \(\kappa\) on \(\bigotimes_{n=1}^N\mathcal{B}_n\) satisfying Equation 34.2 has \(\kappa(R_m) = \prod_n \mu_n(A^{(n)}_m) < \infty\). Thus \(\mathcal{R}\) contains an increasing sequence of sets exhausting \(S\) that has finite measure under every such \(\kappa\). Consequently, if \(\kappa\) and \(\kappa'\) both satisfy Equation 34.2 then they agree on \(\mathcal{R}\) and, exactly as in the uniqueness step of Corollary 30.1 (Dynkin’s \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) theorem applied to the \(\lambda\)-system \(\{E \mid \kappa(E \cap R_m) = \kappa'(E \cap R_m)\}\) for each fixed \(m\), followed by continuity from below as \(m \to \infty\)), \(\kappa = \kappa'\) on \(\sigma(\mathcal{R}) = \bigotimes_{n=1}^N\mathcal{B}_n\). Taking \(\kappa = \bigotimes_n \mu_n\) also shows this measure is \(\sigma\)-finite. In the probability case, where \(S \in \mathcal{R}\) has measure \(1\), the conclusion is also immediate from Lemma 32.1.

Suppose you want random variables \(X_1,\ldots,X_N\) such that

  • \(X_n\) has a particular distribution \(\mu_n\), and
  • \(X_1,\ldots,X_N\) are independent.

The following lemma says that there is a completely canonical way to do this.

Lemma 34.1 Let \((S_1, \mathcal{B}_1), \dots, (S_N, \mathcal{B}_N)\) be measurable spaces and \(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_N\) be probability measures on them. Define the product space: \[ \Omega \triangleq \prod_{n=1}^N S_n, \quad \mathcal{F} \triangleq \bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mathcal{B}_n, \quad P \triangleq \bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mu_n \tag{34.3}\] where \(P\) is the product measure of Proposition 34.1. Then \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\) is a probability space, and the canonical projections \[ X_n(\omega) \triangleq \pi_n(\omega) = x_n, \qquad \omega = (x_1, \dots, x_N) \in \Omega, \] are independent random variables with marginal laws \(\mu_{X_n} = \mu_n\) for \(n = 1, \dots, N\).

Proof (Proof of Lemma 34.1). Probability space. By Proposition 34.1 the product measure \(P\) exists, is unique, and satisfies \(P(\Omega) = \prod_{n=1}^N \mu_n(S_n) = 1\), so \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\) is a probability space and the maps \(X_n\) below are genuine random variables.

Measurability. Each projection \(X_n = \pi_n : \Omega \to S_n\) is \((\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{B}_n)\)-measurable by Lemma 30.1 applied to the product \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F} = \bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mathcal{B}_n\).

Joint distribution on rectangles. For any \(B_n \in \mathcal{B}_n\), the simultaneous event is the product rectangle: \[ \{X_1 \in B_1, \dots, X_N \in B_N\} = \bigcap_{n=1}^N \pi_n^{-1}(B_n) = B_1 \times \dots \times B_N. \] Applying \(P = \bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mu_n\) and using the defining property Equation 34.2 of the product measure on rectangles: \[ \begin{aligned} P(X_1 \in B_1, \dots, X_N \in B_N) &= \left(\bigotimes_{n=1}^N \mu_n\right)\!(B_1 \times \dots \times B_N) \\ &= \prod_{n=1}^N \mu_n(B_n). \end{aligned} \tag{34.4}\]

Marginal laws. Fix \(n \in \{1,\dots,N\}\) and \(B_n \in \mathcal{B}_n\). Setting \(B_j = S_j\) for all \(j \neq n\) in Equation 34.4, and using \(\mu_j(S_j) = 1\) (since each \(\mu_j\) is a probability measure): \[ P(X_n \in B_n) = \mu_n(B_n) \cdot \prod_{j \neq n} \mu_j(S_j) = \mu_n(B_n) \cdot \prod_{j \neq n} 1 = \mu_n(B_n). \] Hence \(\mu_{X_n} = \mu_n\).

Independence. Substituting \(P(X_n \in B_n) = \mu_n(B_n)\) into Equation 34.4 gives: \[ P(X_1 \in B_1, \dots, X_N \in B_N) = \prod_{n=1}^N P(X_n \in B_n) \quad \forall\, B_n \in \mathcal{B}_n. \] For any sub-family \(J \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}\) and \(B_j \in \mathcal{B}_j\) (\(j \in J\)), set \(B_j = S_j\) for \(j \notin J\); then \(\prod_{j \notin J} P(X_j \in S_j) = 1\), giving \(P\bigl(\bigcap_{j \in J}\{X_j \in B_j\}\bigr) = \prod_{j \in J} P(X_j \in B_j)\). This reduction is needed because Definition 33.1 demands the product rule for every finite subfamily, not merely for the full family. Since it holds for all non-empty \(J\) and all \(B_j \in \mathcal{B}_j\), the random variables \(X_1, \dots, X_N\) are independent by Definition 33.1.

Example 34.1 (Repeated coin tosses) Suppose we want to construct three independent coin tosses, where the probability of heads is \(p\). For each toss, let \[ S_n=\{H,T\}, \qquad \mathcal B_n=2^{S_n}, \] and define the probability measure \(\mu_n(\{H\})=p\) and \(\mu_n(\{T\})=1-p\). The lemma tells us to construct \[ \Omega = \{H,T\}\times\{H,T\}\times\{H,T\}. \] Thus, an outcome is something like \(\omega=(H,T,H)\). Define the coordinate random variables \[ X_1(\omega)=\omega_1,\qquad X_2(\omega)=\omega_2,\qquad X_3(\omega)=\omega_3. \] So, for example, \(X_1(H,T,H)=H\), \(X_2(H,T,H)=T\), and \(X_3(H,T,H)=H\). The probability measure is \(P=\mu_1\otimes\mu_2\otimes\mu_3\). Now consider the outcome \(\{X_1=H,X_2=T,X_3=H\}\). This is exactly the singleton \(\{(H,T,H)\}\). Therefore, \[ P(X_1=H,X_2=T,X_3=H) = p(1-p)p. \] In other words, \(P(H,T,H)=p^2(1-p)\). The product measure automatically gives us the multiplication rule for independent experiments.

Example 34.2 (Example with different distributions) Suppose we want three independent random variables: \(X_1\sim N(0,1)\), \(X_2\sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)\), and \(X_3\sim \operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)\). Take \[ S_1=\mathbb R,\qquad S_2=\{0,1\},\qquad S_3=[0,1], \] and \[ \Omega=\mathbb R\times\{0,1\}\times[0,1]. \] Then define \(X_1(x_1,x_2,x_3)=x_1\), \(X_2(x_1,x_2,x_3)=x_2\), and \(X_3(x_1,x_2,x_3)=x_3\). The product measure \[ P = N(0,1) \otimes \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p) \otimes \operatorname{Uniform}(0,1) \] guarantees that \(X_1\), \(X_2\), and \(X_3\) are independent and have exactly the desired marginal distributions. Thus, the lemma is not merely about coin tosses. It provides a general construction principle:

Choose the marginal distributions\(\implies\)take their product measure\(\implies\)the coordinate variables are independent.

Example 34.3 (Construction of Standard Normal Vectors) To construct \(d\) i.i.d. \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) random variables on \(\mathbb{R}^d\), let \(\gamma(x) = (2\pi)^{-1/2} e^{-x^2/2}\) be the standard Gaussian density with respect to Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\) on \((\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))\), and define \(d\mu = \gamma \, d\lambda\). The Gaussian integral \(\int_{\mathbb{R}} \gamma \, d\lambda = 1\) is what makes \(\mu\) a probability measure, which is required below.

Since \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) = \bigotimes_{j=1}^d \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) (the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra identity of Lecture 14.2), the space \((\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\) carries the product measure \(P = \mu^{\otimes d}\) of Proposition 34.1. By Lemma 34.1 the coordinate projections \(X_n(x_1, \dots, x_d) = x_n\) are i.i.d. with common law \(\mu = \mathcal{N}(0,1)\); no computation of a joint density is needed for this conclusion.

The joint density is a consequence, not the reason. Define \[ \begin{aligned} f(x_1, \dots, x_d) &\triangleq \prod_{j=1}^d \gamma(x_j) \\ &= (2\pi)^{-d/2} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^d x_j^2 \right) \\ &= (2\pi)^{-d/2} e^{-\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^2}, \end{aligned} \tag{34.5}\] a non-negative Borel function on \(\mathbb{R}^d\).

The two candidate reference measures agree. Let \(\lambda^{\otimes d}\) be the \(d\)-fold product of one-dimensional Lebesgue measure on \(\bigotimes_{j=1}^d \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\), which exists by Proposition 34.1 since \(\lambda\) is \(\sigma\)-finite, and let \(\lambda^d\) denote Lebesgue measure on \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\). The two \(\sigma\)-algebras coincide by the Borel identity. Let \[ \mathcal{R}_{\square} \triangleq \Bigl\{ \textstyle\prod_{j=1}^d (a_j, b_j] \;\Big|\; -\infty \le a_j \le b_j \le +\infty \Bigr\}, \] with the convention \((a, +\infty] \triangleq (a, +\infty)\), and where \(a_j = b_j\) is allowed so that \(\emptyset \in \mathcal{R}_{\square}\). Then \(\mathcal{R}_{\square}\) is a \(\pi\)-system, because \(\prod_j(a_j,b_j] \cap \prod_j(a_j',b_j'] = \prod_j (a_j \vee a_j', \, b_j \wedge b_j']\) (interpreting an interval with left endpoint \(\ge\) right endpoint as empty), and \(\sigma(\mathcal{R}_{\square}) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\). Both \(\lambda^{\otimes d}\) and \(\lambda^d\) assign the value \(\prod_{j=1}^d (b_j - a_j)\) to such a box — for \(\lambda^{\otimes d}\) by Equation 34.2, for \(\lambda^d\) by its defining property. Since \(R_m \triangleq (-m, m]^d \in \mathcal{R}_{\square}\) increases to \(\mathbb{R}^d\) with \(\lambda^{\otimes d}(R_m) = (2m)^d < \infty\), the uniqueness argument of Corollary 30.1 (an increasing sequence of finite-measure sets inside the \(\pi\)-system) gives \[ \lambda^{\otimes d} = \lambda^d \quad\text{on } \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d). \]

Computing the law. For any box \(R = \prod_{j=1}^d (a_j, b_j] \in \mathcal{R}_{\square}\) we have the tensor factorization \(f \cdot \mathbf{1}_R = \bigotimes_{j=1}^d \bigl( \gamma \cdot \mathbf{1}_{(a_j,b_j]} \bigr)\). Applying Theorem 31.1 to the two-factor splitting \(\lambda^{\otimes d} = \lambda^{\otimes (d-1)} \otimes \lambda\) supplied by Equation 34.1 — legitimate because \(f \mathbf{1}_R \ge 0\) is Borel and all factors are \(\sigma\)-finite — and iterating \(d - 1\) times, \[ \int_R f \, d\lambda^{\otimes d} = \prod_{j=1}^d \int_{(a_j, b_j]} \gamma \, d\lambda = \prod_{j=1}^d \mu\bigl((a_j, b_j]\bigr) = \mu^{\otimes d}(R), \] the last step by Equation 34.2. Taking \(R = \mathbb{R}^d\) shows \(\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f \, d\lambda^{\otimes d} = 1\), so \(\nu(E) \triangleq \int_E f \, d\lambda^{\otimes d}\) defines a probability measure on \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\). Since \(\nu\) and \(\mu^{\otimes d}\) are probability measures agreeing on the \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal{R}_{\square}\) with \(\sigma(\mathcal{R}_{\square}) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\), Lemma 32.1 yields \[ d\mu^{\otimes d} = f \, d\lambda^{\otimes d} = f \, d\lambda^d , \] i.e. the law of \((X_1, \dots, X_d)\) is the standard multivariate normal distribution \(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\).

34.2 Compact Infinite Product Spaces

To construct infinite sequences of random variables, we extend finite product measure spaces to infinite products. Let \(Q \triangleq [0, 1]^\mathbb{N} =\prod_{n=1}^\infty [0, 1] = \{ (x_n)_{n=1}^\infty \mid x_n \in [0, 1] \}\) and let \(p_n : Q \to [0,1]\), \(p_n(x) = x_n\), denote the coordinate maps. We equip \(Q\) with the product topology, i.e. the initial (coarsest) topology making every \(p_n\) continuous; a base is given by the finite cylinders \[ \bigcap_{i=1}^k p_{n_i}^{-1}(U_i), \qquad k \in \mathbb{N},\ n_1 < \dots < n_k,\ U_i \subseteq [0,1] \text{ open}. \tag{34.6}\]

The product topology is not defined by a convergence criterion; the following lemma shows that on \(Q\) it is nevertheless metrizable, so that sequences do determine the topology. This is used twice below: to pass from sequential compactness to compactness in Theorem 34.1, and to identify \(\mathcal{B}(Q)\) in Lemma 34.3.

Lemma 34.2 (Metrizability of \(Q\)) The product topology on \(Q = [0,1]^\mathbb{N}\) is induced by the metric \[ \rho(x, y) \triangleq \sum_{n=1}^\infty 2^{-n} |x_n - y_n| , \tag{34.7}\] and \(\rho\)-convergence is exactly coordinatewise (pointwise) convergence: \[ \begin{aligned} &x^m = (x_n^m)_{n=1}^\infty \to x = (x_n)_{n=1}^\infty \text{ in } Q \\ &\iff x_n^m \to x_n \text{ in } [0, 1] \quad \forall n \in \mathbb{N}. \end{aligned} \tag{34.8}\] In particular \(Q\) is a separable metric space, and compactness, sequential compactness, closedness and sequential closedness in \(Q\) agree.

Proof (Proof of Lemma 34.2). The series in Equation 34.7 converges (terms bounded by \(2^{-n}\)), and \(\rho\) is clearly a metric: symmetry and the triangle inequality are inherited termwise, and \(\rho(x,y) = 0\) forces \(x_n = y_n\) for every \(n\).

\(\rho\)-convergence \(\Rightarrow\) coordinatewise. Fix \(n\); then \(|x_n^m - x_n| \le 2^n \rho(x^m, x)\).

Coordinatewise \(\Rightarrow\) \(\rho\)-convergence. Given \(\epsilon > 0\), pick \(N\) with \(\sum_{n > N} 2^{-n} < \epsilon/2\); the remaining finitely many terms satisfy \(\sum_{n \le N} 2^{-n}|x_n^m - x_n| < \epsilon/2\) for \(m\) large. (This is where \(\sum_n 2^{-n} < \infty\) is essential: the tail is uniformly small, which is why no analogue holds for uncountable products.)

\(\rho\) induces the product topology. Each \(p_n\) is \(1\)-Lipschitz up to the factor \(2^n\), hence \(\rho\)-continuous, so the product topology is coarser than the \(\rho\)-topology. Conversely, given \(x \in Q\) and \(\epsilon > 0\), choose \(N\) with \(\sum_{n > N} 2^{-n} < \epsilon/2\); the basic cylinder \(\bigcap_{n \le N} p_n^{-1}\bigl( (x_n - \epsilon/4, x_n + \epsilon/4) \cap [0,1] \bigr)\) is a product-open neighbourhood of \(x\) contained in the \(\rho\)-ball \(B_\rho(x, \epsilon)\). Hence the two topologies coincide.

The final assertions are the standard facts that in a metric space compactness and sequential compactness are equivalent, and that closed sets are exactly the sequentially closed sets. Separability follows since finitely supported rational sequences are \(\rho\)-dense.

Theorem 34.1 (Sequential Compactness of \([0, 1]^\mathbb{N}\)) Every sequence \(x^{(1)},x^{(2)},\ldots\in Q\) has a subsequence \(x^{(m_k)}\) and some \(x\in Q\) such that \(x^{(m_k)}\to x\) coordinatewise, i.e., \[ x_n^{(m_k)}\to x_n \qquad\text{for every }n\in\mathbb N. \] That is, \(Q = [0,1]^\mathbb{N}\) is sequentially compact; by Lemma 34.2 it is therefore also compact.

Proof (Proof of Theorem 34.1). (Cantor’s Diagonal Argument)

Let \((x^{(m)})_{m \ge 1}\) be an arbitrary sequence in \(Q\).

Step 1: Construct nested convergent subsequences. Apply the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem (every bounded sequence in \(\mathbb{R}\) has a convergent subsequence) inductively:

  • Base case (\(j = 1\)): \((x_1^{(m)})_{m \ge 1} \subseteq [0,1]\) is bounded. By Bolzano-Weierstrass, extract a subsequence \(m^{(1)}(k)\) such that \(x_1^{(m^{(1)}(k))} \to x_1 \in [0,1]\) as \(k \to \infty\).
  • Inductive step (\(j \ge 2\)): Given the subsequence \(m^{(j-1)}\), apply Bolzano-Weierstrass to \((x_j^{(m^{(j-1)}(k))})_{k \ge 1} \subseteq [0,1]\) to extract a further subsequence \(m^{(j)}(k)\) of \(m^{(j-1)}(k)\) such that \(x_j^{(m^{(j)}(k))} \to x_j \in [0,1]\) as \(k \to \infty\).

The limits lie in \([0,1]\) because \([0,1]\) is closed. (This recursive extraction uses the axiom of dependent choice; we shall not comment on such uses again.)

This yields nested subsequences \(m^{(1)}, m^{(2)}, \ldots\) — each \(m^{(j)}\) a subsequence of \(m^{(j-1)}\) — satisfying: \[ x_j^{(m^{(j)}(k))} \to x_j \in [0,1] \quad \text{as } k \to \infty, \quad \forall j \ge 1. \]

Step 2: Define the diagonal sequence. Set \(d_k \triangleq m^{(k)}(k)\) — the \(k\)-th element of the \(k\)-th subsequence. This is strictly increasing: since \(m^{(k+1)}\) is a subsequence of \(m^{(k)}\), write \(m^{(k+1)}(j) = m^{(k)}(\sigma_k(j))\) for a strictly increasing function \(\sigma_k\) satisfying \(\sigma_k(j) \ge j\). Then: \[ d_{k+1} = m^{(k+1)}(k+1) = m^{(k)}\!\left(\sigma_k(k+1)\right) \ge m^{(k)}(k+1) > m^{(k)}(k) = d_k. \]

Step 3: Coordinatewise convergence. Fix any \(n \in \mathbb{N}\). For every \(k \ge n\), \(m^{(k)}\) is a subsequence of \(m^{(n)}\) (by transitivity of nesting: \(m^{(k)} \subset m^{(k-1)} \subset \cdots \subset m^{(n)}\)). Hence \(d_k = m^{(k)}(k) = m^{(n)}(l_k)\) for a unique index \(l_k\), since \(m^{(n)}\) is strictly increasing, hence injective. Moreover \(l_k\) is strictly increasing in \(k \ge n\): if \(l_{k+1} \le l_k\) then \(d_{k+1} = m^{(n)}(l_{k+1}) \le m^{(n)}(l_k) = d_k\), contradicting Step 2. Therefore \(l_k \to \infty\), and \((x_n^{(d_k)})_{k \ge n}\) is a subsequence of the convergent sequence \((x_n^{(m^{(n)}(l))})_{l \ge 1}\): \[ x_n^{(d_k)} = x_n^{(m^{(n)}(l_k))} \xrightarrow{\;k \to \infty\;} x_n. \] The finitely many terms with \(k < n\) do not affect the limit. Since \(n \in \mathbb{N}\) was arbitrary, \(x^{(d_k)} \to x = (x_n)_{n \ge 1} \in Q\) coordinatewise.

Corollary 34.1 (Countable Finite Intersection Property) If \(\{K_m\}_{m=1}^\infty\) is a sequence of closed subsets of \(Q\) such that \(\bigcap_{i=1}^m K_i \neq \emptyset\) for all \(m \in \mathbb{N}\), then \(\bigcap_{i=1}^\infty K_i \neq \emptyset\).

Proof (Proof of Corollary 34.1). (Each \(K_m\) is automatically non-empty, by the hypothesis with \(m\) in place of the index.) For each \(m \in \mathbb{N}\), choose \(x^m \in \bigcap_{i=1}^m K_i\). By Theorem 34.1 there are indices \(m_1 < m_2 < \cdots\) and \(x \in Q\) with \(x^{m_l} \to x\) in \(Q\).

Fix \(i \in \mathbb{N}\). Since \(m_l \to \infty\), choose \(l_0\) with \(m_{l_0} \ge i\). For every \(l \ge l_0\) we have \(m_l \ge m_{l_0} \ge i\), hence \[ x^{m_l} \in \bigcap_{j=1}^{m_l} K_j \subseteq K_i . \] Thus \(x\) is the limit of a sequence lying in \(K_i\), and \(K_i\) is closed, hence sequentially closed by Lemma 34.2; therefore \(x \in K_i\). As \(i \in \mathbb{N}\) was arbitrary, \(x \in \bigcap_{i=1}^\infty K_i\).

34.3 Kolmogorov Extension Theorem on \([0, 1]^\mathbb{N}\)

The Kolmogorov Extension Theorem provides a method to construct infinite sequences of random variables with prescribed finite-dimensional distributions.

NoteNotation from here on

From this point onward, and throughout Lecture 16.2, \(p_n : Q \to [0,1]\) denotes the \(n\)-th coordinate map, \(p_n(x) = x_n\), and \[ \pi_n : Q \to [0,1]^n, \qquad \pi_n(x) = (x_1, \dots, x_n), \] denotes the projection onto the first \(n\) coordinates. This supersedes the earlier use of \(\pi_n\) in this lecture for the \(n\)-th coordinate projection of a finite product \(\prod_{k=1}^N S_k\).

Before stating the theorem we must know that the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra of the topological space \(Q\) coincides with the \(\sigma\)-algebra generated by the cylinder sets, on which the theorem prescribes \(P\). This is exactly what makes the uniqueness assertion meaningful; it uses that the product is countable and each factor is second countable, and it fails for uncountable products, where the cylinder \(\sigma\)-algebra is strictly smaller than the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra.

Lemma 34.3 (Borel Sets of \(Q\) are the Cylinder Sets) For every \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), \(\mathcal{B}([0,1]^n) = \bigotimes_{k=1}^n \mathcal{B}([0,1])\); in particular \(B \times [0,1] \in \mathcal{B}([0,1]^{n+1})\) whenever \(B \in \mathcal{B}([0,1]^n)\). Moreover \[ \mathcal{B}(Q) = \sigma\bigl( p_n : n \in \mathbb{N} \bigr) = \sigma\Bigl( \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \pi_n^{-1}\bigl( \mathcal{B}([0,1]^n) \bigr) \Bigr) , \tag{34.9}\] and the collection \(\mathcal{A} \triangleq \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \pi_n^{-1}\bigl( \mathcal{B}([0,1]^n) \bigr)\) is an algebra, hence in particular a \(\pi\)-system, generating \(\mathcal{B}(Q)\).

Proof (Proof of Lemma 34.3). Finite-dimensional identity. Open boxes \(\prod_{k=1}^n I_k\) with \(I_k \subseteq [0,1]\) relatively open with rational endpoints form a countable base of the (second-countable, metric) space \([0,1]^n\). Every open \(G \subseteq [0,1]^n\) is therefore a countable union of such boxes, each of which lies in \(\bigotimes_{k=1}^n \mathcal{B}([0,1])\); hence \(\mathcal{B}([0,1]^n) \subseteq \bigotimes_{k=1}^n \mathcal{B}([0,1])\). Conversely each coordinate projection \(\operatorname{pr}_k : [0,1]^n \to [0,1]\) is continuous, hence Borel, so \(\operatorname{pr}_k^{-1}(\mathcal{B}([0,1])) \subseteq \mathcal{B}([0,1]^n)\) for every \(k\); since \(\bigotimes_{k=1}^n \mathcal{B}([0,1]) = \sigma(\operatorname{pr}_1, \dots, \operatorname{pr}_n)\) by Lemma 30.2, the reverse inclusion follows. Combining this with the associativity identity Equation 34.1 gives \(\mathcal{B}([0,1]^{n+1}) = \mathcal{B}([0,1]^n) \otimes \mathcal{B}([0,1])\), so \(B \times [0,1] \in \mathcal{B}([0,1]^{n+1})\) for \(B \in \mathcal{B}([0,1]^n)\).

\(\supseteq\) in Equation 34.9. Each \(p_n\) is continuous for the product topology, hence Borel measurable, so \(\sigma(p_n : n \in \mathbb{N}) \subseteq \mathcal{B}(Q)\). Furthermore \(\pi_n = (p_1, \dots, p_n)\) has all components \(\sigma(p_1, \dots, p_n)\)-measurable, so by Lemma 30.3 it is \(\sigma(p_1, \dots, p_n) \,/\, \bigotimes_{k=1}^n \mathcal{B}([0,1])\)-measurable; by the finite-dimensional identity this means \(\pi_n^{-1}(\mathcal{B}([0,1]^n)) \subseteq \sigma(p_1, \dots, p_n)\). Hence the second and third \(\sigma\)-algebras in Equation 34.9 coincide, and both are contained in \(\mathcal{B}(Q)\).

\(\subseteq\) in Equation 34.9. By Lemma 34.2 the metric \(\rho\) of Equation 34.7 induces the product topology. Let \(\mathcal{U}\) be the countable collection of finite cylinders \(\bigcap_{k \le N} p_k^{-1}(I_k)\) with \(N \in \mathbb{N}\) and each \(I_k \subseteq [0,1]\) relatively open with rational endpoints. The argument in the proof of Lemma 34.2 shows that for every \(x \in Q\) and \(\epsilon > 0\) there is a member of \(\mathcal{U}\) containing \(x\) and contained in \(B_\rho(x, \epsilon)\) (choose \(N\) with \(\sum_{n > N} 2^{-n} < \epsilon/2\) and rational-endpoint intervals of length \(< \epsilon/2\) around \(x_1, \dots, x_N\)). Hence \(\mathcal{U}\) is a countable base, so every open \(G \subseteq Q\) is a countable union of members of \(\mathcal{U}\), and each member of \(\mathcal{U}\) lies in \(\sigma(p_n : n \in \mathbb{N})\). Therefore \(G \in \sigma(p_n : n \in \mathbb{N})\) and \(\mathcal{B}(Q) \subseteq \sigma(p_n : n \in \mathbb{N})\). (Countability of \(\mathcal{U}\) is exactly what fails for uncountable products.)

\(\mathcal{A}\) is an algebra. \(Q = \pi_1^{-1}([0,1]) \in \mathcal{A}\); complements are handled within a fixed level since \(Q \setminus \pi_n^{-1}(B) = \pi_n^{-1}([0,1]^n \setminus B)\); and two cylinders of orders \(m \le n\) can be brought to the common order \(n\) via \(\pi_m^{-1}(B) = \pi_n^{-1}(B \times [0,1]^{n-m})\), after which unions are taken inside \(\mathcal{B}([0,1]^n)\). Finally \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}) = \sigma(p_n : n \in \mathbb{N}) = \mathcal{B}(Q)\).

Theorem 34.2 (Kolmogorov Extension Theorem (Compact Base Space)) For each \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), let \(\nu_n\) be a probability measure on \(([0, 1]^n, \mathcal{B}([0, 1]^n))\). Suppose the sequence \(\{\nu_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) satisfies the projective consistency condition: \[ \nu_{n+1}(B \times [0, 1]) = \nu_n(B) \quad \forall B \in \mathcal{B}([0, 1]^n), \, \forall n \in \mathbb{N} \tag{34.10}\] which is meaningful by Lemma 34.3. Then there exists a unique probability measure \(P\) on \((Q, \mathcal{B}(Q))\) such that \[ P\bigl( \pi_n^{-1}(B) \bigr) = \nu_n(B) \quad \forall B \in \mathcal{B}([0, 1]^n), \, \forall n \in \mathbb{N}, \tag{34.11}\] equivalently \(P \circ \pi_n^{-1} = \nu_n\) for every \(n\); that is, \(\{\nu_n\}\) is exactly the family of finite-dimensional distributions of \(P\).

Three comments on the statement.

  • Notation. In Equation 34.11 the cylinder \(\pi_n^{-1}(B)\) is often written \(B \times Q\), using the canonical homeomorphism \(Q \cong [0,1]^n \times [0,1]^{\{n+1, n+2, \dots\}}\). Strictly, \(B \times Q\) is not a subset of \(Q\), and we use \(\pi_n^{-1}(B)\) throughout.

  • Uniqueness. Uniqueness is immediate from Lemma 34.3 and Lemma 32.1: Equation 34.11 determines \(P\) on the \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal{A}\), and \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}) = \mathcal{B}(Q)\). Existence is the substantive assertion.

  • Scope of the consistency condition. Equation 34.10 only requires consistency under deletion of the last coordinate. For a family indexed by the initial segments \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) this is enough: iterating it gives \(\nu_m\bigl( B \times [0,1]^{m-n} \bigr) = \nu_n(B)\) for all \(m > n\). For families indexed by arbitrary finite subsets \(F\) of a general index set \(T\), the condition must be strengthened to include permutation consistency, \(\nu_{\sigma F} = \nu_F \circ \sigma^{-1}\), in addition to marginal consistency.

Finally, the hypothesis that the base space is the compact interval \([0,1]\) is not a technical convenience: the theorem is false for general measurable state spaces. The proof converts finite additivity on the cylinder algebra \(\mathcal{A}\) into countable additivity by an inner-regularity argument, using Theorem 35.1 (in the form Corollary 35.1, which applies to the cubes \([0,1]^n\)) together with Corollary 34.1. The proof is given in Lecture 16.2; the case of \(\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}\) is then obtained by transporting the problem to \((0,1)^\mathbb{N} \subseteq Q\) along a homeomorphism \(\mathbb{R} \cong (0,1)\).

Example 34.4 Let \(\lambda\) denote the uniform probability measure on \([0,1]\). For each \(n\), define \(\nu_n=\lambda^{\otimes n}\) the \(n\)-fold product measure on \(([0,1]^n,\mathcal B([0,1]^n))\). Thus, \(\nu_n\) is the joint distribution of \(n\) independent \(U[0,1]\) random variables. We want to check that \[ \nu_{n+1}(B\times[0,1])=\nu_n(B) \] for every \(B\in\mathcal B([0,1]^n)\).

Suppose \(B=B_1\times\cdots\times B_n\), where each \(B_j\in\mathcal B([0,1])\). Then \[ B\times[0,1] = B_1\times\cdots\times B_n\times[0,1]. \] Because \(\nu_{n+1}=\lambda^{\otimes(n+1)}\), we have \[ \begin{aligned} \nu_{n+1}(B\times[0,1]) &= \lambda(B_1)\cdots\lambda(B_n)\lambda([0,1])\\ &= \lambda(B_1)\cdots\lambda(B_n), \end{aligned} \] because \(\lambda([0,1])=1\). But \(\nu_n(B) = \lambda(B_1)\cdots\lambda(B_n)\). Therefore, \[ \nu_{n+1}(B\times[0,1]) = \nu_n(B). \]

Suppose \(\nu_2=\lambda\otimes\lambda\) describes the joint distribution of \((X_1,X_2)\) and \(\nu_3=\lambda\otimes\lambda\otimes\lambda\) describes the joint distribution of \((X_1,X_2,X_3)\). Take, for example, \(B=[0,0.3]\times[0.5,0.8]\). Then \[ B\times[0,1] = [0,0.3]\times[0.5,0.8]\times[0,1]. \] The consistency condition says \[ \nu_3\left( [0,0.3]\times[0.5,0.8]\times[0,1] \right) = \nu_2\left( [0,0.3]\times[0.5,0.8] \right). \] Indeed, \((0.3)(0.3)(1)=0.09\) while \((0.3)(0.3)=0.09\). The third coordinate contributes nothing because we allow it to take any value in \([0,1]\).

Thus the family \(\nu_1=\lambda\), \(\nu_2=\lambda^{\otimes2}\), \(\nu_3=\lambda^{\otimes3}\),\(\ldots\) is projectively consistent. Kolmogorov’s theorem then gives a unique probability measure \(P\) on \(Q=[0,1]^{\mathbb N}\) such that \[ P\circ\pi_n^{-1}=\lambda^{\otimes n} \qquad\forall n. \] In other words, the theorem constructs the probability law of an infinite sequence \((X_1,X_2,X_3,\ldots)\) of independent \(U[0,1]\) random variables from its finite-dimensional distributions.

So the conceptual picture is: \[ \underbrace{\nu_1,\nu_2,\nu_3,\ldots}_{\text{finite-dimensional distributions}} \quad\xrightarrow{\text{Kolmogorov Extension}}\quad \underbrace{P}_{\text{law of the infinite sequence}} \]
