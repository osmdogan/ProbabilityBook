35.1 Radon Measures on \(\mathbb{R}^d\)

Let \((X, \tau)\) be a topological space equipped with its Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{B}(X)\); recall from Definition 11.1 that a Borel measure on \(X\) is a measure defined on \(\mathcal{B}(X)\). The notions below are best behaved when \(X\) is locally compact and Hausdorff, which is the case for the only space we use, \(X = \mathbb{R}^d\).

Definition 35.1 A Borel measure \(\mu\) on \((X, \mathcal{B}(X))\) is called:

  • Outer-regular: \(\mu(B) = \inf \{ \mu(V) \mid B \subseteq V, \, V \text{ open} \}\) for all \(B \in \mathcal{B}(X)\).
  • Inner-regular: \(\mu(B) = \sup \{ \mu(K) \mid K \subseteq B, \, K \text{ compact} \}\) for all \(B \in \mathcal{B}(X)\).
  • Radon measure: \(\mu\) is finite on compact sets (\(\mu(K) < \infty\) for all compact \(K \subseteq X\); on a locally compact Hausdorff space this is equivalent to being locally finite) and both inner- and outer-regular.

Remark 35.1 (Two Conventions for “Radon”). Two cautionary remarks about the word Radon.

  1. Relation to Definition 11.1. In Lecture 5.1 a Radon measure on \(\mathbb{R}\) was defined by the purely local condition \(\mu((a,b]) < \infty\) for all bounded intervals, with no regularity requirement. On \(\mathbb{R}^d\) the two notions in fact coincide, but we do not prove this in general: Theorem 35.1 below establishes it for finite Borel measures, which is the only case used in this lecture. Whenever regularity is invoked below, it is Theorem 35.1 (or its Corollary 35.1) that is being used, never Definition 11.1.
  2. Strong versus weak inner regularity. Many texts require inner regularity only on open sets (together with outer regularity on all Borel sets). The definition above, demanding inner regularity on all Borel sets, is the stronger “tight” form. The two are not equivalent on a general locally compact Hausdorff space, but they agree for finite Borel measures on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) by Theorem 35.1, so no ambiguity arises here.

Theorem 35.1 Every finite Borel measure \(\mu\) on \((\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\) (in particular, any probability measure) is a Radon measure.

Proof (Proof of Theorem 35.1). Finiteness on compact sets is immediate: \(\mu(K) \le \mu(\mathbb{R}^d) < \infty\) for every compact \(K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d\). It remains to prove inner- and outer-regularity.

Write \(d(x,C) \triangleq \inf_{y \in C} |x - y|\) for the Euclidean distance from a point to a set (with \(d(x,\emptyset) \triangleq +\infty\)), and define the family of sets: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{F} \triangleq \bigl\{ B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) \;\big|\;& \forall \epsilon > 0,\ \exists\text{ open }V\text{ and closed }C\text{ such that}\\ & C \subseteq B \subseteq V \text{ and } \mu(V\setminus C)<\epsilon \bigr\}. \end{aligned} \]

  1. Closed Sets are in \(\mathcal{F}\): Let \(C\) be closed. For \(k \in \mathbb{N}\), set \(V_k \triangleq \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^d \mid d(x, C) < 1/k \}\) (so \(V_k = \emptyset\) if \(C = \emptyset\)). Then each \(V_k\) is open, \(C \subseteq V_k\), the sequence \((V_k)\) is decreasing, and \(\bigcap_{k} V_k = \overline{C} = C\) because \(C\) is closed. Since \(\mu(V_1) \le \mu(\mathbb{R}^d) < \infty\), continuity from above gives \(\mu(V_k \setminus C) = \mu(V_k) - \mu(C) \to 0\). Given \(\epsilon > 0\), choose \(k\) with \(\mu(V_k \setminus C) < \epsilon\); the pair consisting of the closed set \(C\) itself and the open set \(V_k\) then witnesses \(C \in \mathcal{F}\).

  2. Algebra Properties:

    • \(\emptyset \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(\mathbb{R}^d \in \mathcal{F}\), both being closed (item 1).
    • Let \(A \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(\epsilon > 0\). Choose \(C\) closed and \(V\) open with \(C \subseteq A \subseteq V\) and \(\mu(V \setminus C) < \epsilon\). Then \(V^c \subseteq A^c \subseteq C^c\) with \(V^c\) closed and \(C^c\) open. Since \(C^c \setminus V^c = C^c \cap V = V \setminus C\), we get \(\mu(C^c \setminus V^c) < \epsilon\). As \(\epsilon > 0\) was arbitrary, \(A^c \in \mathcal{F}\).
    • Let \(A_1, A_2 \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(\epsilon > 0\). Choose \(C_i \subseteq A_i \subseteq V_i\) with \(C_i\) closed, \(V_i\) open and \(\mu(V_i \setminus C_i) < \epsilon/2\). Then \(C_1 \cup C_2\) is closed, \(V_1 \cup V_2\) is open, \(C_1 \cup C_2 \subseteq A_1 \cup A_2 \subseteq V_1 \cup V_2\), and \((V_1 \cup V_2) \setminus (C_1 \cup C_2) \subseteq (V_1 \setminus C_1) \cup (V_2 \setminus C_2)\), so \(\mu((V_1 \cup V_2) \setminus (C_1 \cup C_2)) \le \mu(V_1 \setminus C_1) + \mu(V_2 \setminus C_2) < \epsilon\). As \(\epsilon > 0\) was arbitrary, \(A_1 \cup A_2 \in \mathcal{F}\).

    Hence \(\mathcal{F}\) is an algebra (closure under finite intersections follows from complements and unions).

  3. Closure under countable disjoint unions: Let \(A_n \in \mathcal{F}\) be pairwise disjoint and fix \(\epsilon > 0\). Choose \(C_n \subseteq A_n \subseteq V_n\) with \(\mu(V_n \setminus C_n) < \epsilon/2^{n+1}\). Set \(V = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty V_n\) (open) and \(D_N = \bigcup_{n=1}^N C_n\) (closed), so that \(D_N \subseteq A \triangleq \bigcup_n A_n \subseteq V\). Observe that: \[ V \setminus D_N \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^N (V_n \setminus C_n) \cup \bigcup_{n=N+1}^\infty V_n. \] For \(n > N\), since \(C_n \subseteq A_n\) we have \(\mu(V_n) \le \mu(A_n) + \mu(V_n \setminus C_n)\). By subadditivity: \[ \mu(V \setminus D_N) \le \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(V_n \setminus C_n) + \sum_{n=N+1}^\infty \mu(A_n) < \frac{\epsilon}{2} + \sum_{n=N+1}^\infty \mu(A_n). \] Since \(\mu\) is finite and the \(A_n\) are disjoint, \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n) = \mu(A) \le \mu(\mathbb{R}^d) < \infty\), so \(\sum_{n=N+1}^\infty \mu(A_n) < \epsilon/2\) for \(N\) sufficiently large. Hence \(\mu(V \setminus D_N) < \epsilon\) for such \(N\), and since \(\epsilon > 0\) was arbitrary, \(A = \bigcup_n A_n \in \mathcal{F}\).

    \(\mathcal{F}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra: Given any (not necessarily disjoint) \(A_n \in \mathcal{F}\), apply disjointification: set \(B_1 = A_1\) and \(B_n = A_n \setminus \bigcup_{k=1}^{n-1} A_k\) for \(n \ge 2\). Since \(\mathcal{F}\) is an algebra (Step 2), each \(B_n \in \mathcal{F}\). The \(B_n\) are pairwise disjoint and \(\bigcup_n A_n = \bigsqcup_n B_n\), so \(\bigcup_n A_n \in \mathcal{F}\) by the disjoint case above. Together with \(\mathbb{R}^d \in \mathcal{F}\) and closure under complements, \(\mathcal{F}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra.

    Since \(\mathcal{F}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra containing all closed sets, \(\mathcal{F} \supseteq \sigma(\text{closed sets}) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\). Combined with \(\mathcal{F} \subseteq \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\) by definition, \(\mathcal{F} = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\).

For any \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) = \mathcal{F}\), the definition of \(\mathcal{F}\) provides, for each \(n\), a closed set \(C_n \subseteq B\) with \(\mu(B \setminus C_n) < 1/n\), establishing inner-regularity with closed sets. Setting \(K_n = C_n \cap \bar{B}^d(0, n)\) gives compact \(K_n \subseteq B\) with \[ \mu(B \setminus K_n) \le \mu(B \setminus C_n) + \mu(C_n \setminus \bar{B}^d(0,n)) < \frac{1}{n} + \mu(B \setminus \bar{B}^d(0,n)) \to 0 \] as \(n \to \infty\) (using \(B \setminus \bar{B}^d(0,n) \downarrow \emptyset\) and continuity from above for the finite measure \(\mu\)). Hence \(\mu(K_n) \to \mu(B)\), establishing full inner-regularity. For outer-regularity, given \(\epsilon > 0\) take \(C \subseteq B \subseteq V\) with \(\mu(V \setminus C) < \epsilon\) as in the definition of \(\mathcal{F}\); then \(V \setminus B \subseteq V \setminus C\), so \(\mu(V) = \mu(B) + \mu(V \setminus B) \le \mu(B) + \epsilon\), and since \(\epsilon > 0\) was arbitrary the infimum over open \(V \supseteq B\) equals \(\mu(B)\).

Note where the two hypotheses enter. Everything up to the previous paragraph uses only that \(\mathbb{R}^d\) is a metric space and that \(\mu\) is finite, and yields inner regularity by closed sets; this part is valid in any metric space. The upgrade from closed to compact sets uses the Heine–Borel theorem (a closed bounded subset of \(\mathbb{R}^d\) is compact) together with \(\sigma\)-compactness of \(\mathbb{R}^d\), and does fail in general metric spaces.

The Kolmogorov Extension Theorem is applied below to probability measures on the cube \([0,1]^n\), not on \(\mathbb{R}^n\). The following corollary transfers Theorem 35.1 to that setting.

Corollary 35.1 (Regularity on Borel Subsets of \(\mathbb{R}^d\)) Let \(E \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\), give \(E\) the subspace topology, and let \(\mathcal{B}(E)\) be its Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra. Let \(\nu\) be a finite Borel measure on \((E, \mathcal{B}(E))\). Then \(\nu\) is inner-regular by compact sets: \[ \nu(B) = \sup\{ \nu(K) \mid K \subseteq B, \ K \text{ compact} \} \qquad \forall B \in \mathcal{B}(E), \] where “compact” may equivalently be read in \(E\) or in \(\mathbb{R}^d\), compactness being intrinsic to a subset. In particular every probability measure \(\nu_n\) on \(([0,1]^n, \mathcal{B}([0,1]^n))\) is inner-regular by compact sets.

Proof (Proof of Corollary 35.1). Step 0: identification of \(\mathcal{B}(E)\). The collection \(\mathcal{D} \triangleq \{ A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d \mid A \cap E \in \mathcal{B}(E) \}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra (preimages under the inclusion \(E \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^d\) commute with complements and countable unions) and contains every open \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d\), since \(A \cap E\) is open in \(E\). Hence \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) \subseteq \mathcal{D}\), i.e. \(\{A \cap E \mid A \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\} \subseteq \mathcal{B}(E)\). Conversely \(\{A \cap E \mid A \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(E\) containing all sets open in \(E\), so it contains \(\mathcal{B}(E)\). Therefore \[ \mathcal{B}(E) = \{ A \cap E \mid A \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) \}, \] and because \(E \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\) every such \(A \cap E\) is itself Borel in \(\mathbb{R}^d\), giving \[ \mathcal{B}(E) = \{ A \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) \mid A \subseteq E \} \subseteq \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d). \]

Step 1: transfer. Define \(\hat{\nu}(A) \triangleq \nu(A \cap E)\) for \(A \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\); this is well defined by Step 0, is countably additive because \(\nu\) is and \(A \mapsto A \cap E\) preserves disjointness and countable unions, and is finite with \(\hat\nu(\mathbb{R}^d) = \nu(E) < \infty\). Hence \(\hat\nu\) is Radon by Theorem 35.1. For \(B \in \mathcal{B}(E)\) we have \(B \subseteq E\), so \(\hat\nu(B) = \nu(B)\) and \[ \nu(B) = \hat{\nu}(B) = \sup\{ \hat{\nu}(K) \mid K \subseteq B, \ K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d \text{ compact} \}. \] Every such \(K\) satisfies \(K \subseteq B \subseteq E\), so \(K\) is compact in the subspace topology of \(E\) as well (compactness of a set depends only on its own subspace topology), and \(\hat{\nu}(K) = \nu(K)\). The final claim follows with \(E = [0,1]^n\), which is closed, hence Borel, in \(\mathbb{R}^n\).

35.2 Proof of the Kolmogorov Extension Theorem

Let \(Q = [0, 1]^\mathbb{N}\) equipped with the product topology and its Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{B}(Q)\). We prove Theorem 34.2, whose hypotheses (a projectively consistent family \(\{\nu_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) of probability measures on \(([0,1]^n, \mathcal{B}([0,1]^n))\)) and conclusion (existence and uniqueness of \(P\) on \((Q, \mathcal{B}(Q))\) with \(P \circ \pi_n^{-1} = \nu_n\)) are stated there.

Proof (Proof of Theorem 34.2).

  1. Cylinder Algebra: For \(j \in \mathbb{N}\), let \(p_j:Q\to[0,1]\) be the \(j\)-th coordinate projection, \(p_j((x_k)_{k=1}^\infty)=x_j\). Let \(\pi_n:Q\to[0,1]^n\) be the projection onto the first \(n\) coordinates: \[ \pi_n(x)=(p_1(x),\ldots,p_n(x))=(x_1,\ldots,x_n), \] and write \(Q_{>n} \triangleq [0,1]^{\{n+1,n+2,\ldots\}}\) for the remaining coordinates. Under the canonical bijection \(Q \cong [0,1]^n \times Q_{>n}\) we have \(\pi_n^{-1}(B) = B \times Q_{>n}\); we use the unambiguous form \(\pi_n^{-1}(B)\) from now on. Define the \(\sigma\)-algebra of cylinder sets determined by the first \(n\) coordinates by \[ \mathcal{C}_n \triangleq \{\pi_n^{-1}(B)\mid B\in\mathcal{B}([0,1]^n)\} =\sigma(\pi_n)=\sigma(p_1,\ldots,p_n). \tag{35.1}\] The inverse images of Borel sets form the \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\sigma(\pi_n)\). Moreover, each \(p_j\) with \(j\le n\) is \(\sigma(\pi_n)\)-measurable because \(p_j=\operatorname{pr}_j\circ\pi_n\) with \(\operatorname{pr}_j\) continuous, while \(\pi_n=(p_1,\ldots,p_n)\) is \(\sigma(p_1,\ldots,p_n)/\bigotimes_{k\le n}\mathcal{B}([0,1])\)-measurable by Lemma 30.3, and \(\bigotimes_{k \le n}\mathcal{B}([0,1]) = \mathcal{B}([0,1]^n)\) by Lemma 34.3. These two inclusions prove \(\sigma(\pi_n)=\sigma(p_1,\ldots,p_n)\).

    The collection \(\mathcal{A} \triangleq \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \mathcal{C}_n\) is exactly the algebra \(\mathcal{A}\) of Lemma 34.3, and by that lemma \[ \sigma(\mathcal{A})=\sigma(p_j:j\in\mathbb{N})=\mathcal{B}(Q). \] (This last identity is where countability of the index set and second countability of \([0,1]\) are used; for an uncountable product the inclusion \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}) \subseteq \mathcal{B}(Q)\) can be strict.) Note also that \(\mathcal{C}_m \subseteq \mathcal{C}_n\) for \(m \le n\), since \(\pi_m^{-1}(B) = \pi_n^{-1}(B \times [0,1]^{n-m})\) and \(B \times [0,1]^{n-m} \in \mathcal{B}([0,1]^n)\) by Lemma 34.3; hence any finite collection of elements of \(\mathcal{A}\) lies in a common \(\mathcal{C}_n\), which is used repeatedly below.

  2. Finitely Additive Set Function: Define \(P_0: \mathcal{A} \to [0, 1]\) by \(P_0(\pi_n^{-1}(B)) \triangleq \nu_n(B)\). This is well-defined: if \(\pi_m^{-1}(B) = \pi_n^{-1}(B')\) with \(m \le n\), then applying the surjection \(\pi_n\) gives \(B' = B \times [0,1]^{n-m}\), so iterating the consistency condition \(n-m\) times gives \(\nu_n(B') = \nu_m(B)\). Finite additivity holds because two disjoint elements of \(\mathcal{A}\) may be written at a common order \(n\) as \(\pi_n^{-1}(B_1)\), \(\pi_n^{-1}(B_2)\); disjointness and surjectivity of \(\pi_n\) force \(B_1 \cap B_2 = \emptyset\), whence \[ P_0\bigl(\pi_n^{-1}(B_1) \cup \pi_n^{-1}(B_2)\bigr) = \nu_n(B_1 \cup B_2) = \nu_n(B_1) + \nu_n(B_2). \] In particular, \(P_0(Q) = \nu_1([0,1]) = 1\), so \(P_0\) is a finitely additive probability set function on the algebra \(\mathcal{A}\). Being non-negative and finitely additive on an algebra, \(P_0\) is also monotone and finitely subadditive there; both facts are used below.

  3. Countable Additivity via Compactness: We show that \(P_0\) is a premeasure on \(\mathcal{A}\). Since \(P_0\) is finitely additive with \(P_0(Q) = 1 < \infty\), Proposition 11.1 (equivalence of items 1 and 4) reduces this to: \[ B_n \in \mathcal{A},\ B_n \downarrow \emptyset \ \Longrightarrow \ P_0(B_n) \to 0 . \] Since \((B_n)\) is decreasing and \(P_0\) is monotone, \(P_0(B_n)\) is non-increasing and bounded, so the limit exists; suppose towards a contradiction that \[ \lim_{n\to\infty} P_0(B_n) = \inf_{n} P_0(B_n) = \epsilon > 0 . \]

    • Cylinder representation. Write \(B_n = \pi_{m_n}^{-1}(C_n)\) with \(m_n \in \mathbb{N}\) and \(C_n \in \mathcal{B}([0,1]^{m_n})\), so that \(P_0(B_n) = \nu_{m_n}(C_n)\) by Step 2. (No re-indexing to a common or monotone order is needed: the argument below applies Corollary 34.1 directly to closed sets, rather than building a point of \(\bigcap_n B_n\) coordinate by coordinate.)
    • Compact inner approximation. Each \(\nu_{m_n}\) is a probability measure on \([0,1]^{m_n}\), hence inner-regular by compact sets by Corollary 35.1. Since \(\nu_{m_n}(C_n) < \infty\), we may choose a compact \(K_n' \subseteq C_n\) with \[ \nu_{m_n}(C_n \setminus K_n') < \frac{\epsilon}{2^{n+1}} . \]
    • Cylinders over the compacta. Put \(K_n \triangleq \pi_{m_n}^{-1}(K_n') \subseteq B_n\). Since \(\pi_{m_n} : Q \to [0,1]^{m_n}\) is continuous (its components \(p_1, \dots, p_{m_n}\) are) and \(K_n'\) is compact, hence closed in \([0,1]^{m_n}\), the set \(K_n\) is closed in \(Q\). Also \(K_n \in \mathcal{A}\), and \(B_n \setminus K_n = \pi_{m_n}^{-1}(C_n \setminus K_n')\), so \[ P_0(B_n \setminus K_n) = \nu_{m_n}(C_n \setminus K_n') < \frac{\epsilon}{2^{n+1}} . \]
    • Set \(H_n \triangleq \bigcap_{i=1}^n K_i\). Then \(H_n \in \mathcal{A}\) (a finite intersection of cylinders), \(H_n\) is closed in \(Q\), and \(H_n \subseteq K_n \subseteq B_n\).
    • Estimate the difference. Since \(H_n \subseteq B_n\) we have \(B_n \setminus H_n = \bigcup_{i=1}^n (B_n \setminus K_i)\), and \(B_n \subseteq B_i\) for \(i \le n\) (because \((B_n)\) is decreasing), so \(B_n \setminus K_i \subseteq B_i \setminus K_i\). Using finite subadditivity and monotonicity of \(P_0\) from Step 2: \[ \begin{aligned} P_0(B_n \setminus H_n) &= P_0\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^n (B_n \setminus K_i) \right) \\ &\le \sum_{i=1}^n P_0(B_n \setminus K_i) \\ &\le \sum_{i=1}^n P_0(B_i \setminus K_i) \\ &< \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\epsilon}{2^{i+1}} < \frac{\epsilon}{2} \end{aligned} \tag{35.2}\]
    • Since \(H_n \subseteq B_n\) and \(P_0\) is finitely additive, \(P_0(B_n) = P_0(H_n) + P_0(B_n \setminus H_n)\), whence, using \(P_0(B_n) \ge \epsilon\), \[ P_0(H_n) = P_0(B_n) - P_0(B_n \setminus H_n) > \epsilon - \frac{\epsilon}{2} = \frac{\epsilon}{2} > 0 . \]
    • In particular \(H_n = \bigcap_{i=1}^n K_i \neq \emptyset\) for every \(n \in \mathbb{N}\). The sets \(K_i\) are closed in \(Q\) and all their initial finite intersections are non-empty, so Corollary 34.1 applies and gives, using \(H_n \subseteq B_n\), \[ \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty B_n \supseteq \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty H_n = \bigcap_{i=1}^\infty K_i \neq \emptyset \tag{35.3}\]
    • This contradicts \(B_n \downarrow \emptyset\). Hence \(P_0(B_n) \to 0\) whenever \(B_n \downarrow \emptyset\), so \(P_0\) is a premeasure on the algebra \(\mathcal{A}\).

  4. Extension and uniqueness. \((Q, \mathcal{A}, P_0)\) is a finite premeasure space, so by the measure extension theorem Theorem 9.2, \(P_0\) extends to a measure \(P\) on a \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}) = \mathcal{B}(Q)\); restricting to \(\mathcal{B}(Q)\) gives a measure with \(P(Q) = P_0(Q) = 1\) and \[ P(\pi_n^{-1}(B)) = P_0(\pi_n^{-1}(B)) = \nu_n(B) \qquad \forall n \in \mathbb{N},\ \forall B \in \mathcal{B}([0,1]^n), \] which is Equation 34.11. Uniqueness holds because \(\mathcal{A}\) is an algebra, hence a \(\pi\)-system, generating \(\mathcal{B}(Q)\), and Equation 34.11 determines \(P\) on \(\mathcal{A}\); apply Lemma 32.1. (This is the same argument already recorded after the statement of Theorem 34.2 in Lecture 16.1.)

35.3 Extensions to \(\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}\) and Infinite Independent Sequences

Throughout this section \(\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}\) carries the product topology, \(p_n : \mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}\) is the \(n\)-th coordinate map and \(\pi_n : \mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}^n\), \(\pi_n(x) = (x_1,\dots,x_n)\), is the projection onto the first \(n\) coordinates. The function \((a,b) \mapsto |a-b| \wedge 1\) is a metric on \(\mathbb{R}\) inducing its usual topology, so \[ \rho_\mathbb{R}(x,y) \triangleq \sum_{n=1}^\infty 2^{-n}\bigl( |x_n - y_n| \wedge 1 \bigr) \] is a well-defined metric on \(\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}\) (the truncation \(\wedge\,1\) is what makes the series converge), and the argument of Lemma 34.2 shows verbatim that \(\rho_\mathbb{R}\) induces the product topology, that \(\rho_\mathbb{R}\)-convergence is coordinatewise convergence, and that the countable family of finite cylinders \(\bigcap_{k \le N} p_k^{-1}(I_k)\) with \(I_k \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) an open interval with rational endpoints is a base. In particular \(\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}\) is a separable metrizable space; it is in fact Polish, since \(\rho_\mathbb{R}\) is complete. Repeating the argument of Lemma 34.3 mutatis mutandis — it used only countability of the index set and second countability of the factor, not compactness — gives \[ \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}) = \sigma(p_n : n \in \mathbb{N}) = \bigotimes_{n=1}^\infty \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}) = \sigma\Bigl( \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \pi_n^{-1}\bigl( \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n) \bigr) \Bigr), \tag{35.4}\] where the second equality is Definition 30.3 (the product \(\sigma\)-algebra is by definition generated by the coordinate maps) and the third uses the Borel identity \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n) = \bigotimes_{k=1}^n \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) of Lecture 14.2. Moreover \(\bigcup_{n} \pi_n^{-1}(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n))\) is an algebra, hence a \(\pi\)-system, generating \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N})\).

Corollary 35.2 (Kolmogorov Extension Theorem on \(\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}\)) Let \(\nu_n\) be probability measures on \((\mathbb{R}^n, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n))\), \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), satisfying projective consistency \[ \nu_{n+1}(B \times \mathbb{R}) = \nu_n(B) \qquad \forall B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n),\ \forall n \in \mathbb{N} . \tag{35.5}\] Then there exists a unique probability measure \(P\) on \((\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}))\) such that \[ P\bigl( \pi_n^{-1}(B) \bigr) = \nu_n(B) \qquad \forall B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n),\ \forall n \in \mathbb{N}, \tag{35.6}\] equivalently \(P \circ \pi_n^{-1} = \nu_n\) for every \(n\).

Proof (Proof of Corollary 35.2). Uniqueness. By Equation 35.4 the sets \(\pi_n^{-1}(B)\) form a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N})\) on which Equation 35.6 prescribes \(P\); since \(P\) is a probability measure, Lemma 32.1 gives uniqueness. It remains to prove existence.

Transport to the cube. Fix a homeomorphism \(\alpha: \mathbb{R} \to (0, 1)\) (e.g. \(\alpha(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x \frac{1}{\pi(1+t^2)} \, dt\), the Cauchy CDF), and let \(\beta \triangleq \alpha^{-1} : (0,1) \to \mathbb{R}\). Extend \(\beta\) to a Borel map \(\bar\beta : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}\) by setting \(\bar\beta(0) = \bar\beta(1) \triangleq 0\). Both \(\alpha\) and \(\bar\beta\) are Borel measurable, so the coordinatewise maps \[ \alpha^n : \mathbb{R}^n \to (0,1)^n, \qquad \bar\beta^{\,n} : [0,1]^n \to \mathbb{R}^n, \qquad \bar\beta^{\,\infty} : Q \to \mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N} \] are Borel measurable — for \(\bar\beta^{\,\infty}\) this is Lemma 30.3 applied to the components \(\bar\beta \circ p_k\), together with Equation 35.4. Note \(\bar\beta^{\,n} \circ \alpha^n = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^n}\) and \(\alpha^n \circ \bar\beta^{\,n} = \mathrm{id}\) on \((0,1)^n\).

Define \(\tilde\nu_n\) on \(([0,1]^n, \mathcal{B}([0,1]^n))\) as the pushforward of \(\nu_n\) under \(\alpha^n\): \[ \tilde{\nu}_n(A) \triangleq \nu_n\bigl((\alpha^n)^{-1}(A \cap (0, 1)^n)\bigr), \qquad A \in \mathcal{B}([0,1]^n) . \] Each \(\tilde\nu_n\) is a probability measure with \(\tilde\nu_n\bigl((0,1)^n\bigr) = \nu_n(\mathbb{R}^n) = 1\).

Consistency of \(\{\tilde{\nu}_n\}\). Let \(A \in \mathcal{B}([0,1]^n)\) and abbreviate \(S \triangleq A \cap (0,1)^n\). Two set identities are used. First, \[ (A \times [0,1]) \cap (0,1)^{n+1} = S \times (0,1), \] and second, since \(\alpha^{n+1}\) acts coordinatewise and \(\alpha^{-1}\bigl((0,1)\bigr) = \mathbb{R}\), \[ (\alpha^{n+1})^{-1}\bigl( S \times (0,1) \bigr) = (\alpha^{n})^{-1}(S) \times \alpha^{-1}\bigl((0,1)\bigr) = (\alpha^{n})^{-1}(S) \times \mathbb{R} . \] Therefore \[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{\nu}_{n+1}(A \times [0,1]) &= \nu_{n+1}\Bigl( (\alpha^{n+1})^{-1}\bigl( (A \times [0,1]) \cap (0,1)^{n+1} \bigr) \Bigr) \\ &= \nu_{n+1}\bigl( (\alpha^n)^{-1}(S) \times \mathbb{R} \bigr) \\ &= \nu_n\bigl( (\alpha^n)^{-1}(S) \bigr) = \tilde{\nu}_n(A), \end{aligned} \] the third equality being Equation 35.5. Hence \(\{\tilde{\nu}_n\}\) is projectively consistent in the sense of Equation 34.10.

Existence. By Theorem 34.2 there is a probability measure \(\tilde{P}\) on \((Q, \mathcal{B}(Q))\) with \(\tilde{P} \circ \pi_n^{-1} = \tilde\nu_n\) for every \(n\). Define \[ P \triangleq \tilde{P} \circ \bigl( \bar\beta^{\,\infty} \bigr)^{-1}, \] a probability measure on \((\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}))\) since \(\bar\beta^{\,\infty}\) is measurable. (The values assigned to \(\bar\beta\) at the endpoints \(0,1\) are irrelevant: \((0,1)^\mathbb{N} = \bigcap_n \pi_n^{-1}\bigl((0,1)^n\bigr) \in \mathcal{B}(Q)\) and \(\tilde{P}\bigl(\pi_n^{-1}((0,1)^n)\bigr) = \tilde\nu_n((0,1)^n) = 1\) for every \(n\), so \(\tilde{P}\bigl((0,1)^\mathbb{N}\bigr) = 1\) and \(\bar\beta^{\,\infty}\) agrees \(\tilde P\)-a.s. with the inverse of the coordinatewise map induced by \(\alpha\).)

Verification of Equation 35.6. Fix \(n\) and \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)\). Since \(\pi_n \circ \bar\beta^{\,\infty} = \bar\beta^{\,n} \circ \pi_n\), \[ P\bigl(\pi_n^{-1}(B)\bigr) = \tilde{P}\Bigl( \bigl(\bar\beta^{\,\infty}\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(\pi_n^{-1}(B)\bigr) \Bigr) = \tilde{P}\Bigl( \pi_n^{-1}\bigl( (\bar\beta^{\,n})^{-1}(B) \bigr) \Bigr) = \tilde{\nu}_n\bigl( (\bar\beta^{\,n})^{-1}(B) \bigr). \] Writing \(A \triangleq (\bar\beta^{\,n})^{-1}(B)\) we have \(A \cap (0,1)^n = \{ y \in (0,1)^n \mid \beta^n(y) \in B \} = \alpha^n(B)\), hence \((\alpha^n)^{-1}\bigl(A \cap (0,1)^n\bigr) = B\) and therefore \(\tilde\nu_n(A) = \nu_n(B)\). Thus \(P(\pi_n^{-1}(B)) = \nu_n(B)\).

Remark 35.2 (What the Transport Argument Actually Uses). The specific choice of the Cauchy CDF is irrelevant. The only properties of \(\mathbb{R}\) used above are:

  1. \(\mathbb{R}\) is homeomorphic to a Borel subset \((0,1)\) of the compact metrizable space \([0,1]\), so that the finite-dimensional laws transport to \([0,1]^n\) and the transported laws charge \((0,1)^n\) fully;
  2. the inverse homeomorphism extends to a Borel map \([0,1] \to \mathbb{R}\) (any extension will do, since the added set is \(\tilde P\)-null).

By the Borel isomorphism theorem every uncountable Polish space is Borel isomorphic to \([0,1]\), so the same argument yields the Kolmogorov extension theorem with an arbitrary Polish state space in place of \(\mathbb{R}\). Some such hypothesis is genuinely needed: the theorem is false for arbitrary measurable state spaces.

35.4 Existence of Infinite Independent Sequences

Theorem 35.2 (Existence of Infinite Independent Sequences) Let \(\{\mu_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) be an arbitrary sequence of Borel probability measures on \((\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))\). There exists a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\) and a sequence of independent random variables \(X_n: (\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P) \to (\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))\) such that \(\mu_{X_n} = \mu_n\) for all \(n \in \mathbb{N}\). In particular, taking \(\mu_n = \mu\) for every \(n\) produces an i.i.d. sequence with common law \(\mu\), so i.i.d. sequences with an arbitrary prescribed marginal law exist.

Proof (Proof of Theorem 35.2). Set \(\Omega \triangleq \mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}\) and \(\mathcal{F} \triangleq \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}) = \sigma(p_n : n \in \mathbb{N})\) by Equation 35.4. All the random variables constructed below are defined on this single space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\), as Definition 33.1 requires.

  1. Define the finite product measures \(\nu_n \triangleq \bigotimes_{k=1}^n \mu_k\) on \(\bigl(\mathbb{R}^n, \bigotimes_{k=1}^n \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\bigr) = (\mathbb{R}^n, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n))\), the identification of the two \(\sigma\)-algebras being the Borel identity of Lecture 14.2; these exist and are unique probability measures by Proposition 34.1 (for \(n = 1\) read \(\nu_1 = \mu_1\)).
  2. Projective consistency. The map \(B \mapsto \nu_{n+1}(B \times \mathbb{R})\) is the pushforward of \(\nu_{n+1}\) under the projection \(\operatorname{pr}_{1:n} : \mathbb{R}^{n+1} \to \mathbb{R}^n\) (measurable by Lemma 30.3), hence a probability measure on \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)\); so is \(B \mapsto \nu_n(B)\). On a rectangle \(B = B_1 \times \dots \times B_n\) they agree, since by Equation 34.2 \[ \begin{aligned} \nu_{n+1}(B_1 \times \dots \times B_n \times \mathbb{R}) &= \Bigl( \prod_{k=1}^n \mu_k(B_k) \Bigr) \mu_{n+1}(\mathbb{R})\\ & = \prod_{k=1}^n \mu_k(B_k) = \nu_n(B). \end{aligned} \] Rectangles form a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)\) by Definition 30.1 together with \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n) = \bigotimes_{k=1}^n \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\), so Lemma 32.1 gives \(\nu_{n+1}(B \times \mathbb{R}) = \nu_n(B)\) for all \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)\), i.e. Equation 35.5 holds.
  3. By Corollary 35.2 there is a unique probability measure \(P\) on \((\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}))\) with \(P(\pi_n^{-1}(B)) = \nu_n(B)\) for all \(n\) and all \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)\).
  4. Set \(X_n \triangleq p_n\), the \(n\)-th coordinate map, which is \(\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\)-measurable by Lemma 30.1. For any \(n\) and any \(B_1, \dots, B_n \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) we have \(\bigcap_{k=1}^n \{X_k \in B_k\} = \pi_n^{-1}(B_1 \times \dots \times B_n)\), so \[ \begin{aligned} P(X_1 \in B_1, \dots, X_n \in B_n) &= P\bigl( \pi_n^{-1}(B_1 \times \dots \times B_n) \bigr) \\ &= \nu_n(B_1 \times \dots \times B_n) \\ &= \prod_{k=1}^n \mu_k(B_k) . \end{aligned} \tag{35.7}\]
  5. Marginals. Fixing \(k \le n\) and setting \(B_j = \mathbb{R}\) for \(j \ne k\) in Equation 35.7 gives \(P(X_k \in B_k) = \mu_k(B_k)\), i.e. \(\mu_{X_k} = \mu_k\).
  6. Independence. Let \(J \subseteq \mathbb{N}\) be finite and non-empty, \(n \triangleq \max J\), and \(B_j \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) for \(j \in J\). Setting \(B_j = \mathbb{R}\) for \(j \in \{1,\dots,n\} \setminus J\) in Equation 35.7 and using step 5, \[ P\Bigl( \bigcap_{j \in J} \{X_j \in B_j\} \Bigr) = \prod_{j \in J} \mu_j(B_j) = \prod_{j \in J} P(X_j \in B_j) . \] Since this holds for every finite \(J\), the family \(\{X_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) is independent in the sense of Definition 33.1.

Example 35.1 Suppose we want random variables \(X_1,X_2,X_3,\ldots\) such that \[ X_n= \begin{cases} 1,&\text{with probability }p_n,\\ 0,&\text{with probability }1-p_n, \end{cases} \] where the probabilities can be different for every \(n\). For example, let \(p_n=\frac{1}{n+1}\). Thus, \[ X_1\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}\left(\frac12\right), \, X_2\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}\left(\frac13\right), \, X_3\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}\left(\frac14\right), \,\ldots \] and we want all these random variables to be independent. The theorem says that such an infinite sequence exists. Let’s construct it explicitly. For each \(n\), define \[ \mu_n = \frac{n}{n+1}\delta_0 + \frac{1}{n+1}\delta_1, \] where \(\delta_x\) denotes the point mass at \(x\). So \(\mu_n(\{1\})=\frac{1}{n+1}\) and \(\mu_n(\{0\})=\frac{n}{n+1}\). Our goal is therefore to construct independent \(X_n\) satisfying \(X_n \sim \mu_n\) for \(n=1,2,3,\ldots\).

Let \(\Omega=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}\). An element of \(\Omega\) is an entire infinite sequence: \[ \omega=(\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3,\ldots), \, \omega_n\in\{0,1\}. \] For example, \(\omega=(0,1,0,0,1,0,1,\ldots)\) is one possible outcome. Think of \(\omega\) as describing the outcomes of all infinitely many coin tosses. We equip \(\Omega\) with the product \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal F = \bigotimes_{n=1}^\infty 2^{\{0,1\}}\), where \(2^{\{0,1\}}\) is the power set of \(\{0,1\}\). For each coordinate, define the probability measure \[ \mu_n = \frac{n}{n+1}\delta_0+\frac{1}{n+1}\delta_1. \] The infinite product probability measure is \[ P=\bigotimes_{n=1}^\infty\mu_n. \] This is the probability measure that makes the coordinates behave according to their specified distributions and makes them independent. Schematically, \[ (\Omega,\mathcal F,P) = \left( \{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}, \bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{\{0,1\}}, \bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_n \right). \] Now define the \(n\)th coordinate map: \(X_n(\omega)=\omega_n\). For example, if \(\omega=(0,1,0,0,1,0,\ldots)\), then \[ X_1(\omega)=0,\, X_2(\omega)=1,\, X_3(\omega)=0,\, X_4(\omega)=0,\,\ldots. \] So each \(X_n\) simply looks at the \(n\)th coordinate. The event \(\{X_n=1\}\) consists of all sequences whose \(n\)th coordinate equals \(1\): \(\{X_n=1\} = \{\omega\in\Omega:\omega_n=1\}\). Therefore, \(P(X_n=1) = \mu_n(\{1\}) = \frac{1}{n+1}\). Similarly, \(P(X_n=0) = \mu_n(\{0\}) = \frac{n}{n+1}\). Hence, \(X_n\) has the desired Bernoulli distribution: \(X_n \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}\left(\frac{1}{n+1}\right)\). Thus, \(\mu_{X_n} = \mu_n\).

The entire sample space can be visualized as

\[ \begin{array}{cccccc} \omega &=& (\omega_1,&\omega_2,&\omega_3,&\omega_4,\ldots)\\ &&\downarrow&\downarrow&\downarrow&\downarrow\\ &&X_1(\omega)&X_2(\omega)&X_3(\omega)&X_4(\omega) \end{array} \] For example, \(\omega=(0,1,0,0,1,0,0,\ldots)\) produces \[ (X_1(\omega),X_2(\omega),X_3(\omega),X_4(\omega),X_5(\omega),\ldots) = (0,1,0,0,1,\ldots). \] So an element \(\omega\) is itself an infinite realization, while \(X_n\) extracts its \(n\)th component. This is the central idea behind the product-space construction.

Example 35.2 (Infinitely many independent Uniform\((0,1)\) random variables) Suppose we want \(X_1,X_2,X_3,\ldots\) to satisfy \(X_n\sim \operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)\) for every \(n\). The theorem tells us that such a sequence exists. Let’s construct it.

Let \(\lambda\) denote Lebesgue measure on \([0,1]\). The Uniform\((0,1)\) probability measure is \(\mu(A)=\lambda(A)\) for all \(A\in\mathcal B([0,1])\), because \(\lambda([0,1])=1\). Thus, \(([0,1],\mathcal B([0,1]),\lambda)\) is the desired probability space for a single Uniform\((0,1)\) random variable.

Take \(\Omega=[0,1]^{\mathbb N}\). An element of \(\Omega\) is an infinite sequence \[ \omega=(\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3,\ldots), \qquad \omega_n\in[0,1]. \] For example, \(\omega=(0.374,0.821,0.156,\ldots)\) is one possible outcome. The sample space therefore contains every possible infinite sequence of numbers in \([0,1]\). Equip it with the product \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal F = \bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty} \mathcal B([0,1])=\mathcal B([0,1])^{\otimes\mathbb N}\). For every coordinate, we want the Uniform\((0,1)\) distribution. Therefore define \(P=\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda=\lambda^{\otimes\mathbb N}\). Thus, the probability space can be written as \[ (\Omega, \mathcal F, P)=([0,1]^{\mathbb N}, \bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty} \mathcal B([0,1]), \bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda). \]

Define the coordinate maps \[ X_n: \underbrace{([0,1]^{\mathbb N},\mathcal B([0,1])^{\otimes\mathbb N},\lambda^{\otimes\mathbb N})}_{\text{probability space}} \longrightarrow \underbrace{([0,1],\mathcal B([0,1]))}_{\text{measurable codomain space}}, \] with \(X_n(\omega)=\omega_n\). Why is \(X_n\) Uniform\((0,1)\)? Consider any Borel set \(A\subseteq[0,1]\). We have \[ \{X_n\in A\} = \{\omega:\omega_n\in A\}. \] This is the cylinder set \[ [0,1]\times\cdots\times[0,1] \times A \times[0,1]\times\cdots, \] where \(A\) occurs in the \(n\)th position. By the definition of the product measure, \[ \begin{aligned} P(X_n\in A) &= P([0,1]\times\cdots\times A\times\cdots)\\ &= \prod_{j\neq n}\lambda([0,1])\cdot\lambda(A)\\ &=\lambda(A). \end{aligned} \] Since \(\lambda(A)\) is precisely the Uniform\((0,1)\) probability of \(A\), we conclude that \(X_n \sim \operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)\).
