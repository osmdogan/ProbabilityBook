37  Lecture 17.2: Convolution

37.1 Notation and Standing Conventions

Throughout this lecture, \(d \in \mathbb{N}\) is fixed:

  • \(\lambda\) denotes Lebesgue measure on \((\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))\) (Definition 12.1), and \(d\)-dimensional Lebesgue measure is defined as the \(d\)-fold product \[ \lambda^d \triangleq \lambda^{\otimes d} \quad \text{on} \quad \textstyle\bigotimes_{j=1}^d \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d), \tag{37.1}\] which exists, is unique, and is \(\sigma\)-finite by Corollary 30.1; the identification of the product \(\sigma\)-algebra with \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\) is the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra identity of Lecture 14.2. By associativity of the product construction, \(\lambda^{d} \otimes \lambda^{d} = \lambda^{2d}\) and \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) \otimes \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})\).
  • \(s: \mathbb{R}^{2d} \to \mathbb{R}^d\) denotes the addition map \(s(x, y) \triangleq x + y\). It is continuous, hence Borel measurable.
  • For \(B \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d\) and \(y \in \mathbb{R}^d\) we write \(B - y \triangleq \{x \in \mathbb{R}^d \mid x + y \in B\}\) for the translate of \(B\) by \(-y\). This is not a set difference. If \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\) then \(B - y = \tau_y^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\), where \(\tau_y(x) \triangleq x + y\) is a homeomorphism.

37.2 Sums of Independent Random Variables and Convolutions

Let \(X, Y\) be random variables defined on a common probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\) taking values in \((\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\). Since \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) \otimes \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\), the pair \((X, Y): \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^{2d}\) is \(\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})\)-measurable, so its joint law \(\mu_{X, Y} \triangleq (X, Y)_* P\) is a Borel probability measure on \(\mathbb{R}^{2d}\).

In general, the law of \(X+Y\) is not determined solely by the marginal laws \(\mu_X\) and \(\mu_Y\), but requires knowledge of \(\mu_{X, Y}\). For instance, in dimension \(d=1\), let \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\). Taking \(Y = X\) gives \(X + Y = 2X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 4)\), whereas taking \(Y = -X\) gives \(X + Y = 0\) everywhere, so \(\mu_{X+Y} = \delta_0\). In both cases \(\mu_Y = \mathcal{N}(0,1) = \mu_X\) — for the second by symmetry of the standard normal density — so the marginals are identical, yet the two laws of \(X+Y\) differ. (In neither case are \(X\) and \(Y\) independent; independence is exactly the hypothesis that removes this ambiguity.)

Proposition 37.1 For any Borel set \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\), the law of \(X+Y\) is determined by the joint law \(\mu_{X, Y}\) via \[ \mu_{X+Y}(B) = P(X+Y \in B) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}} \mathbf{1}_B(x+y) \, \mu_{X, Y}(dx, dy). \tag{37.2}\]

Proof (Proof of Proposition 37.1). Write \(X + Y = s \circ (X, Y)\), a composition of the \(\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})\)-measurable map \((X,Y)\) with the Borel map \(s\); hence \(X+Y\) is a random vector and \(\mu_{X+Y}\) is well defined. Pushforward is functorial: for \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\), \[ \mu_{X+Y}(B) = P\bigl((s \circ (X,Y))^{-1}(B)\bigr) = P\bigl((X,Y)^{-1}(s^{-1}(B))\bigr) = \mu_{X,Y}\bigl(s^{-1}(B)\bigr), \] that is, \(\mu_{X+Y} = s_* \mu_{X, Y}\). Since \(s^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})\) and \(\mathbf{1}_{s^{-1}(B)} = \mathbf{1}_B \circ s\), integrating the indicator gives \[ \begin{aligned} \mu_{X+Y}(B) &= \mu_{X,Y}\bigl(s^{-1}(B)\bigr) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}} \mathbf{1}_{s^{-1}(B)} \, d\mu_{X, Y} \\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}} (\mathbf{1}_B \circ s)(x, y) \, \mu_{X, Y}(dx, dy) \\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}} \mathbf{1}_B(x+y) \, \mu_{X, Y}(dx, dy). \end{aligned} \tag{37.3}\] Equivalently, one may apply Corollary 24.1 to the map \((X,Y)\) with non-negative integrand \(\mathbf{1}_B \circ s\), obtaining \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_B(X+Y)] = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}} \mathbf{1}_B(x+y)\,\mu_{X,Y}(dx,dy)\).

37.3 Definition and Properties of Measure Convolution

When \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, their joint law factorizes as \(\mu_{X, Y} = \mu_X \otimes \mu_Y\) (Theorem 33.1). Convolution is the probability-theoretic rule for adding independent random variables.

Definition 37.1 Let \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) be Borel probability measures on \((\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\). Their convolution, denoted \(\mu * \nu\), is defined by \[ \begin{aligned} (\mu * \nu)(B) &\triangleq \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}} \mathbf{1}_B(x+y) \, (\mu \otimes \nu)(dx, dy) \\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mu(B-y) \, \nu(dy) \\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \nu(B-x) \, \mu(dx), \end{aligned} \tag{37.4}\] for \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\), where \(B-y\) is the translate introduced in the conventions above.

  • \(\mu * \nu\) is a Borel probability measure. Since \(\mathbf{1}_B(x+y) = \mathbf{1}_{s^{-1}(B)}(x,y)\), the defining expression reads \((\mu * \nu)(B) = (\mu \otimes \nu)(s^{-1}(B))\), i.e. \[ \mu * \nu = s_*(\mu \otimes \nu). \tag{37.5}\] The product measure \(\mu \otimes \nu\) exists and is a probability measure by Corollary 30.1 (both factors are probability measures, hence \(\sigma\)-finite), and \(s\) is Borel measurable; a pushforward of a probability measure under a measurable map is again a probability measure. In particular \(\mu * \nu\) is countably additive with \((\mu*\nu)(\mathbb{R}^d) = (\mu\otimes\nu)(\mathbb{R}^{2d}) = 1\).

  • Derivation of the two iterated forms. The integrand \(\mathbf{1}_B \circ s\) is non-negative and \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\)-measurable, and \(\mu, \nu\) are \(\sigma\)-finite, so Theorem 31.1 applies and gives both iterated integrals. Using \(x+y \in B \iff x \in B-y\), i.e. \(\mathbf{1}_B(x+y) = \mathbf{1}_{B-y}(x)\): \[ \begin{aligned} \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}} \mathbf{1}_B(x+y) \, (\mu \otimes \nu)(dx, dy)&= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{1}_B(x+y) \, \mu(dx) \right) \nu(dy)\\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{1}_{B-y}(x) \, \mu(dx) \right) \nu(dy)\\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mu(B-y) \, \nu(dy). \end{aligned} \] By the measurability clause of Theorem 31.1 applied to the non-negative product-measurable integrand \((x,y) \mapsto \mathbf{1}_B(x+y)\), the map \[ y \longmapsto \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{1}_B(x+y)\,\mu(dx) = \mu(B-y) \] is Borel measurable, so the outer integral is meaningful. Applying instead the reversed iterated order in Theorem 31.1, and using \(x + y \in B \iff y \in B - x\), gives in the same way \[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}} \mathbf{1}_B(x+y) \, (\mu \otimes \nu)(dx, dy) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \nu(B-x) \, \mu(dx), \] with \(x \mapsto \nu(B-x)\) Borel measurable. This proves the third line of Equation 37.4.

  • Intuition: Convolution represents the distribution of the sum of two independent random variables. Precisely, if \(X \sim \mu\) and \(Y \sim \nu\) are independent, then Theorem 33.1 gives \(\mu_{X,Y} = \mu \otimes \nu\), and Proposition 37.1 yields \(\mu_{X+Y} = \mu * \nu\).

Proposition 37.2 (Commutativity and Associativity) Let \(\mu, \nu, \rho\) be Borel probability measures on \((\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\). Then \[ \mu * \nu = \nu * \mu \qquad \text{and} \qquad (\mu * \nu) * \rho = \mu * (\nu * \rho). \tag{37.6}\] Consequently the \(n\)-fold convolution \(\mu_1 * \cdots * \mu_n\) is unambiguous and independent of the order of the factors.

Proof (Proof of Proposition 37.2). Commutativity. Fix \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\). Both iterated forms of Theorem 31.1 are available for the non-negative product-measurable integrand \(\mathbf{1}_B \circ s\), so \[ \begin{aligned} (\mu*\nu)(B) &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d}\!\! \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{1}_B(x+y)\, \mu(dx)\, \nu(dy) \\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d}\!\! \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{1}_B(y+x)\, \nu(dy)\, \mu(dx) \\ &= (\nu*\mu)(B), \end{aligned} \] where the middle equality is the interchange of the two iterated orders together with the commutativity \(x + y = y + x\) of addition in \(\mathbb{R}^d\). (No auxiliary random variables need be constructed.)

Associativity. Let \(\theta \triangleq \mu \otimes \nu \otimes \rho\) be the product measure on \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{3d})\) furnished by Corollary 30.1, and let \(s_3(x,y,z) \triangleq x+y+z\), which is continuous hence Borel. Note first that \[ (\mu \otimes \nu) \otimes \rho = \theta = \mu \otimes (\nu \otimes \rho), \tag{37.7}\] since all three are measures on \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{3d})\) assigning the value \(\mu(E_1)\nu(E_2)\rho(E_3)\) to each rectangle \(E_1\times E_2\times E_3\), and such a measure is unique by Corollary 30.1 (all factors being probability measures, hence \(\sigma\)-finite).

Fix \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\). By Definition 37.1 together with Theorem 31.1 applied to the pair \((\mu*\nu, \rho)\), \[ \begin{aligned} \bigl((\mu * \nu) * \rho\bigr)(B) &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{1}_B(w + z) \, (\mu*\nu)(dw) \right) \rho(dz) \\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}} \mathbf{1}_B\bigl((x+y) + z\bigr) \, (\mu \otimes \nu)(dx, dy) \right) \rho(dz) \\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^{3d}} \mathbf{1}_B(x+y+z) \, \theta(dx, dy, dz), \end{aligned} \] where the second equality is Corollary 24.1 applied, for each fixed \(z\), to the map \(s\) with \(\mu*\nu = s_*(\mu \otimes \nu)\) and non-negative integrand \(w \mapsto \mathbf{1}_B(w+z)\), and the third is Theorem 31.1 for the non-negative Borel integrand \(\mathbf{1}_B \circ s_3\) on the \(\sigma\)-finite product \(\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times\mathbb{R}^d\), together with the first half of Equation 37.7. The identical computation performed in the other grouping, using the second half of Equation 37.7, gives \[ \bigl(\mu * (\nu * \rho)\bigr)(B) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{3d}} \mathbf{1}_B\bigl(x + (y+z)\bigr) \, \theta(dx, dy, dz). \] Since addition in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) is associative, the two integrands coincide pointwise, so the two measures agree on every \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\).

The final assertion follows by induction from these two identities.

37.4 Absolutely Continuous and Discrete Special Cases

The convolution operation has special forms when the measures involved are either absolutely continuous or purely discrete.

Proposition 37.3 (Absolutely Continuous Measures (Density Functions)) Let \(\mu, \nu\) be Borel probability measures on \((\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\) with \(d\mu = f \, d\lambda^d\) and \(d\nu = g \, d\lambda^d\), where \(f, g: \mathbb{R}^d \to [0, \infty)\) are Borel measurable with \(\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f \, d\lambda^d = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} g \, d\lambda^d = 1\). Define the convolution of the densities \[ (f * g)(u) \triangleq \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(x) g(u-x) \, \lambda^d(dx) \in [0, \infty]. \tag{37.8}\] Then:

  1. \(f * g\) is a Borel measurable function of \(u\), and \((f*g)(u) < \infty\) for \(\lambda^d\)-a.e. \(u\);
  2. \(\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} (f*g) \, d\lambda^d = 1\), so \(f * g \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^d, \lambda^d)\);
  3. \(\mu * \nu \ll \lambda^d\), with Radon-Nikodym derivative \[ \frac{d(\mu * \nu)}{d\lambda^d} = f * g \qquad \lambda^d\text{-a.e.} \tag{37.9}\]

Proof (Proof of Proposition 37.3). Throughout, \(\lambda^d\) is \(\sigma\)-finite and \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) \otimes \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})\) by Equation 37.1, so a function on \(\mathbb{R}^{2d}\) is product-measurable if and only if it is Borel measurable. All integrands below are non-negative, so every integral is well defined in \([0, \infty]\) and Theorem 31.1 applies without integrability hypotheses.

37.4.1 Step 1: Measures with a density, and integration against them

Claim. Let \((E, \mathcal{E}, m)\) be a measure space and let \(w: E \to [0, \infty)\) be \(\mathcal{E}\)-measurable. Then \[ \kappa(A) \triangleq \int_A w \, dm, \qquad A \in \mathcal{E}, \] defines a measure on \(\mathcal{E}\), and for every \(\mathcal{E}\)-measurable \(h: E \to [0, \infty]\), \[ \int_E h \, d\kappa = \int_E h w \, dm. \tag{37.10}\]

Proof of claim. First, \(\kappa\) is a measure: \(\kappa(\emptyset) = \int \mathbf{0} \, dm = 0\), and if \(A = \biguplus_{k \ge 1} A_k\) with \(A_k \in \mathcal{E}\) pairwise disjoint, then \(\mathbf{1}_A w = \sum_{k \ge 1} \mathbf{1}_{A_k} w\) pointwise with non-negative terms, so the partial sums increase to \(\mathbf{1}_A w\) and Theorem 18.1 (with finite additivity of the integral at each stage) gives \(\kappa(A) = \sum_{k\ge1} \kappa(A_k)\).

Next, Equation 37.10. For \(h = \mathbf{1}_A\) both sides equal \(\int_A w \, dm\), by definition of \(\kappa\). By linearity of the integral on non-negative functions, Equation 37.10 extends to non-negative simple \(h\). For general \(\mathcal{E}\)-measurable \(h: E \to [0,\infty]\) choose simple functions \(0 \le h_n \uparrow h\) pointwise; then \(h_n w \uparrow h w\) pointwise as well, and applying Theorem 18.1 to both sides of the simple-function identity gives Equation 37.10. \(\square\)

37.4.2 Step 2: The product measure \(\mu \otimes \nu\) has density \(f(x) g(y)\)

Let \(w(x, y) \triangleq f(x) g(y)\). Since \(w = (f \circ \pi_1) \cdot (g \circ \pi_2)\) with \(\pi_1, \pi_2\) the coordinate projections (which are product-measurable by Lemma 30.2), \(w\) is \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) \otimes \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\)-measurable, and \(w \ge 0\) is finite-valued. By Step 1 applied on \((\mathbb{R}^{2d}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}), \lambda^d \otimes \lambda^d)\), \[ \kappa(E) \triangleq \int_E w \, d(\lambda^d \otimes \lambda^d), \qquad E \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) \otimes \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d), \] is a measure. For a measurable rectangle \(E_1 \times E_2\) we have \(\mathbf{1}_{E_1 \times E_2}(x,y) = \mathbf{1}_{E_1}(x)\mathbf{1}_{E_2}(y)\), so Theorem 31.1 gives \[ \begin{aligned} \kappa(E_1 \times E_2) &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d}\!\! \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^d}\!\! \mathbf{1}_{E_1}(x) f(x) \mathbf{1}_{E_2}(y) g(y) \, \lambda^d(dy) \right) \lambda^d(dx) \\ &= \mu(E_1) \, \nu(E_2), \end{aligned} \] the last step using \(\int \mathbf{1}_{E_2} g \, d\lambda^d = \nu(E_2)\) and \(\int \mathbf{1}_{E_1} f \, d\lambda^d = \mu(E_1)\), both finite, so no \(0 \cdot \infty\) convention is invoked. By the uniqueness assertion of Corollary 30.1 (applicable since \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are probability measures, hence \(\sigma\)-finite), \(\kappa\) is the product measure: \(\kappa = \mu \otimes \nu\), that is, \[ d(\mu \otimes \nu) = f(x) g(y) \, d(\lambda^d \otimes \lambda^d). \tag{37.11}\]

37.4.3 Step 3: Unfolding the definition of \(\mu * \nu\)

Fix \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\). The addition map \(s\) is Borel, so \(\mathbf{1}_B \circ s\) is a non-negative product-measurable function. Applying Equation 37.4, then Equation 37.10 with Equation 37.11, then Theorem 31.1: \[ \begin{aligned} (\mu * \nu)(B) &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}} \mathbf{1}_B(x+y) \, (\mu \otimes \nu)(dx, dy) \\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}} \mathbf{1}_B(x+y) f(x) g(y) \, (\lambda^d \otimes \lambda^d)(dx, dy) \\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(x) \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{1}_B(x+y) g(y) \, \lambda^d(dy) \right) \lambda^d(dx). \end{aligned} \tag{37.12}\]

37.4.4 Step 4: Translation invariance of the Lebesgue integral

Claim. For every fixed \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and every Borel \(h: \mathbb{R}^d \to [0, \infty]\), \[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} h(y) \, \lambda^d(dy) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} h(u - x) \, \lambda^d(du). \tag{37.13}\]

Proof of claim. We first record translation invariance of \(\lambda^d\): for every \(\tau \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and \(A \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\), \[ \lambda^d(A + \tau) = \lambda^d(A). \tag{37.14}\] Indeed, in dimension one \(\lambda(A_j + \tau_j) = \lambda(A_j)\) for all \(A_j \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\), as established in the Translation Invariance section of Lecture 5.2. Hence the Borel measure \(A \mapsto \lambda^d(A + \tau)\) agrees with \(\lambda^d = \lambda^{\otimes d}\) on every measurable rectangle \(A_1 \times \cdots \times A_d\), because \((A_1 \times \cdots \times A_d) + \tau = (A_1+\tau_1)\times\cdots\times(A_d+\tau_d)\). Both are \(\sigma\)-finite measures, so the uniqueness assertion of Corollary 30.1 forces them to coincide on \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\), giving Equation 37.14.

Now let \(T_x(u) \triangleq u - x\), a continuous (hence Borel) bijection of \(\mathbb{R}^d\). For \(A \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\) we have \(T_x^{-1}(A) = \{u : u - x \in A\} = A + x\), so by Equation 37.14 \[ \bigl((T_x)_* \lambda^d\bigr)(A) = \lambda^d\bigl(T_x^{-1}(A)\bigr) = \lambda^d(A + x) = \lambda^d(A). \] Hence \((T_x)_* \lambda^d = \lambda^d\), and Corollary 24.1 applied to \(T_x\) with the non-negative integrand \(h\) yields \(\int h \, d\lambda^d = \int h \circ T_x \, d\lambda^d\), which is Equation 37.13. \(\square\)

Apply Equation 37.13 to the inner integral in Equation 37.12 with \(h(y) = \mathbf{1}_B(x+y) g(y)\), noting \(h(u - x) = \mathbf{1}_B\bigl(x + (u-x)\bigr) g(u-x) = \mathbf{1}_B(u) g(u-x)\): \[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{1}_B(x+y) g(y) \, \lambda^d(dy) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{1}_B(u) g(u-x) \, \lambda^d(du). \]

37.4.5 Step 5: Interchanging the order of integration

The map \(\Phi(x, u) \triangleq (x, u - x)\) is continuous, hence Borel on \(\mathbb{R}^{2d}\), so \[ (x, u) \longmapsto f(x) g(u - x) = w\bigl(\Phi(x, u)\bigr) \] is product-measurable and non-negative; multiplying by the product-measurable \(\mathbf{1}_B(u)\) preserves this. Note also that the constant \(f(x) \ge 0\) may be moved inside the inner \(\lambda^d(du)\)-integral without invoking the convention \(0 \cdot \infty = 0\), since that inner integral is bounded by \(\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} g \, d\lambda^d = 1 < \infty\). Therefore Theorem 31.1 permits the interchange \[ \begin{aligned} (\mu * \nu)(B) &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{1}_B(u) f(x) g(u-x) \, \lambda^d(du) \right) \lambda^d(dx) \\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{1}_B(u) \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(x) g(u-x) \, \lambda^d(dx) \right) \lambda^d(du) \\ &= \int_B (f * g)(u) \, \lambda^d(du). \end{aligned} \tag{37.15}\] The measurability clause of Theorem 31.1 also gives that \(u \mapsto (f*g)(u)\) is Borel measurable, proving the first half of claim 1.

37.4.6 Step 6: Integrability and identification of the density

Taking \(B = \mathbb{R}^d\) in Equation 37.15 and using that \(\mu * \nu\) is a probability measure (Definition 37.1), \[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} (f * g) \, d\lambda^d = (\mu * \nu)(\mathbb{R}^d) = 1 < \infty, \] which is claim 2. A non-negative measurable function with finite integral is finite a.e., so \((f*g)(u) < \infty\) for \(\lambda^d\)-a.e. \(u\); this completes claim 1. Replacing \(f * g\) by the finite-valued version \((f*g) \mathbf{1}_{\{f * g < \infty\}}\) changes no integral.

Finally, Equation 37.15 holds for every \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\). In particular \(\lambda^d(B) = 0\) implies \((\mu * \nu)(B) = \int_B (f*g) \, d\lambda^d = 0\), so \(\mu * \nu \ll \lambda^d\) by Definition 23.1. Since Equation 37.15 exhibits \(f * g\) as a density of \(\mu * \nu\) with respect to \(\lambda^d\), the a.e.-uniqueness clause of Theorem 23.1 identifies it as the Radon-Nikodym derivative, giving Equation 37.9.

Proposition 37.4 (Point Masses of a Convolution) Let \(\mu, \nu\) be Borel probability measures on \((\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\), and let \[ A_\mu \triangleq \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^d \mid \mu(\{x\}) > 0 \} \] denote the (at most countable) set of atoms of \(\mu\). Then for every \(u \in \mathbb{R}^d\), \[ (\mu * \nu)(\{u\}) = \sum_{x \in A_\mu} \mu(\{x\}) \, \nu(\{u-x\}), \tag{37.16}\] the series having non-negative terms and hence an unambiguous value in \([0, 1]\). No discreteness assumption on \(\mu\) or \(\nu\) is required.

Proof (Proof of Proposition 37.4). Step 1: \(A_\mu\) is at most countable. For each \(n \in \mathbb{N}\) the set \(A_\mu^n \triangleq \{x : \mu(\{x\}) > 1/n\}\) has at most \(n\) elements: any \(n+1\) distinct points of \(A_\mu^n\) would be disjoint singletons of total \(\mu\)-measure \(> 1\), contradicting \(\mu(\mathbb{R}^d) = 1\). Hence \(A_\mu = \bigcup_{n \ge 1} A_\mu^n\) is a countable union of finite sets.

Step 2: Reduction to a sum. Note \(\{u\} \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\) and \(\{u\} - y = \{u - y\}\). Taking \(B = \{u\}\) in the second line of Equation 37.4, \[ (\mu * \nu)(\{u\}) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mu(\{u\} - y) \, \nu(dy) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \varphi(y) \, \nu(dy), \qquad \varphi(y) \triangleq \mu(\{u-y\}), \] where \(\varphi \ge 0\) is Borel measurable by the measurability clause of Theorem 31.1 recorded after Equation 37.4.

By definition of \(A_\mu\), we have \(\varphi(y) > 0\) if and only if \(u - y \in A_\mu\), i.e. if and only if \(y \in u - A_\mu\). By Step 1 this set is at most countable; enumerate it as \(u - A_\mu = \{y_1, y_2, \dots\}\) (a finite or countably infinite list of distinct points), so that \[ \varphi = \sum_{i} \varphi(y_i) \, \mathbf{1}_{\{y_i\}} \quad \text{pointwise on } \mathbb{R}^d . \] All terms are non-negative, so Corollary 18.2 (integration of series in \(L^+\), itself a consequence of Theorem 18.1) gives \[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \varphi \, d\nu = \sum_i \varphi(y_i) \, \nu(\{y_i\}) = \sum_i \mu(\{u - y_i\}) \, \nu(\{y_i\}). \]

Step 3: Reindexing. Substitute \(x \triangleq u - y_i\). As \(y_i\) runs over \(u - A_\mu\), the point \(x\) runs bijectively over \(A_\mu\), and \(y_i = u - x\). Therefore \[ (\mu * \nu)(\{u\}) = \sum_{x \in A_\mu} \mu(\{x\}) \, \nu(\{u - x\}), \] which is Equation 37.16. Since the terms are non-negative, the sum is independent of the enumeration chosen. Finally, adjoining any further terms with \(x \notin A_\mu\) would change nothing, because such terms have \(\mu(\{x\}) = 0\). Thus one may use the common shorthand \(\sum_{x \in \mathbb{R}^d} \mu(\{x\})\nu(\{u-x\})\), interpreting it as the non-negative sum whose only nonzero terms occur at the at most countable set \(A_\mu\).

The discrete case is the one in which these point masses determine the whole measure.

Corollary 37.1 (Convolution of Purely Atomic Measures) Let \(\mu, \nu\) be Borel probability measures on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) that are purely atomic, i.e. there exist at most countable sets \(S_\mu, S_\nu \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) with \(\mu(S_\mu) = \nu(S_\nu) = 1\). Then \(\mu * \nu\) is purely atomic and concentrated on the at most countable set \[ S_\mu + S_\nu \triangleq \{ a + b \mid a \in S_\mu,\ b \in S_\nu \}, \] its point masses being given by Equation 37.16. Consequently \(\mu * \nu\) is uniquely determined by the numbers \(\bigl((\mu*\nu)(\{u\})\bigr)_{u \in S_\mu + S_\nu}\).

Proof (Proof of Corollary 37.1). Set \(C \triangleq S_\mu + S_\nu\), a countable union of countable sets, hence at most countable, and Borel (being countable). By Equation 37.5, \((\mu*\nu)(C) = (\mu \otimes \nu)(s^{-1}(C))\), and \(S_\mu \times S_\nu \subseteq s^{-1}(C)\) by construction, so \[ (\mu * \nu)(C) \ge (\mu \otimes \nu)(S_\mu \times S_\nu) = \mu(S_\mu)\,\nu(S_\nu) = 1, \] using Corollary 30.1 for the value on the rectangle. Hence \((\mu*\nu)(C) = 1\), so \(\mu * \nu\) is concentrated on the countable set \(C\) and is therefore purely atomic. For any \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\), countable additivity then gives \[ (\mu*\nu)(B) = (\mu*\nu)(B \cap C) = \sum_{u \in B \cap C} (\mu*\nu)(\{u\}), \] so the measure is determined by its point masses on \(C\), each of which is computed by Equation 37.16.

37.5 Canonical Examples

Example 37.1 (Sum of Independent Poisson RVs) Take \(d = 1\) and let \(\alpha, \beta \in (0, \infty)\). Let \(\mu = \text{Poisson}(\alpha)\) and \(\nu = \text{Poisson}(\beta)\), i.e. the purely atomic probability measures on \(\mathbb{R}\) with \(\mu(\{j\}) = e^{-\alpha}\alpha^j/j!\) and \(\nu(\{j\}) = e^{-\beta}\beta^j/j!\) for \(j \in \mathbb{N}_0\), and no mass elsewhere; thus \(A_\mu = S_\mu = S_\nu = \mathbb{N}_0\).

Fix \(k \in \mathbb{N}_0\). In Equation 37.16 the sum runs over \(x \in A_\mu = \mathbb{N}_0\); writing \(x = j\), the factor \(\nu(\{k - j\})\) vanishes unless \(k - j \in \mathbb{N}_0\), i.e. unless \(j \le k\). Hence the sum truncates at \(j = k\) and \[ \begin{aligned} (\mu * \nu)(\{k\}) &= \sum_{j=0}^k \left( e^{-\alpha} \frac{\alpha^j}{j!} \right) \left( e^{-\beta} \frac{\beta^{k-j}}{(k-j)!} \right) \\ &= \frac{e^{-(\alpha+\beta)}}{k!} \sum_{j=0}^k \frac{k!}{j!\,(k-j)!} \alpha^j \beta^{k-j}\\ & = \frac{e^{-(\alpha+\beta)}}{k!} \sum_{j=0}^k \binom{k}{j} \alpha^j \beta^{k-j} \\ &= e^{-(\alpha+\beta)} \frac{(\alpha+\beta)^k}{k!}, \end{aligned} \tag{37.17}\] the last step by the binomial theorem.

By Corollary 37.1, \(\mu * \nu\) is purely atomic and concentrated on \(S_\mu + S_\nu = \mathbb{N}_0 + \mathbb{N}_0 = \mathbb{N}_0\), and is therefore determined by the point masses just computed. Since these coincide with those of \(\text{Poisson}(\alpha+\beta)\) — which is likewise concentrated on \(\mathbb{N}_0\) — we conclude \[ \text{Poisson}(\alpha) * \text{Poisson}(\beta) = \text{Poisson}(\alpha + \beta). \]

Example 37.2 (Sum of Independent Gaussian RVs) Take \(d = 1\). For \(m \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(\sigma \in (0,\infty)\) write \(\varphi_{m,\sigma^2}(x) \triangleq \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma} e^{-(x-m)^2/(2\sigma^2)}\) for the \(\mathcal{N}(m,\sigma^2)\) density with respect to \(\lambda\). These are strictly positive continuous functions, hence Borel measurable with values in \((0,\infty)\), and they integrate to \(1\); so Proposition 37.3 applies to any pair of them.

The standard case. Let \(\mu = \nu = \mathcal{N}(0,1)\), so \(f = g = \varphi_{0,1}\). Completing the square, \[ -\frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{(u-x)^2}{2} = -x^2 + ux - \frac{u^2}{2} = -\left(x - \frac{u}{2}\right)^2 - \frac{u^2}{4}, \] so that \[ \begin{aligned} (\varphi_{0,1} * \varphi_{0,1})(u) &= \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-x^2/2} e^{-(u-x)^2/2} \, \lambda(dx) \\ &= \frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-u^2/4} \int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-(x - u/2)^2} \, \lambda(dx) \\ &= \frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-u^2/4} \int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-x^2} \, \lambda(dx)\\ &= \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\pi} e^{-u^2/4} \\ &= \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}} e^{-u^2/4}, \end{aligned} \tag{37.18}\] where the third equality is the translation invariance Equation 37.13 (with \(x\) there taken to be \(u/2\)) and the fourth is the Gaussian integral \(\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-x^2}\,dx = \sqrt{\pi}\). Since \[ \varphi_{0,2}(u) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \cdot 2}} e^{-u^2/(2 \cdot 2)} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}} e^{-u^2/4}, \] the computed density is exactly \(\varphi_{0,2}\), and Proposition 37.3 gives \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1) * \mathcal{N}(0, 1) = \mathcal{N}(0, 2)\).

The general case. The preceding computation does not by itself yield the general formula, since a single rescaling cannot reduce a pair with \(s \ne t\) to the standard pair. We therefore redo the completion of the square in general.

Proposition 37.5 (Convolution of Gaussian Laws) For all \(\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(s, t \in (0, \infty)\), \[ \mathcal{N}(\alpha, s^2) * \mathcal{N}(\beta, t^2) = \mathcal{N}(\alpha + \beta,\, s^2 + t^2). \tag{37.19}\]

Proof (Proof of Proposition 37.5). Set \(v \triangleq s^2 + t^2 \in (0,\infty)\). By Proposition 37.3 it suffices to show that \(\varphi_{\alpha, s^2} * \varphi_{\beta, t^2} = \varphi_{\alpha+\beta,\, v}\) pointwise on \(\mathbb{R}\), since both sides are then densities of the two measures in Equation 37.19 with respect to \(\lambda\).

Fix \(u \in \mathbb{R}\). Abbreviate \(a \triangleq x - \alpha\) and \(b \triangleq (u - x) - \beta\), and put \(\bar u \triangleq u - \alpha - \beta\), so that \(a + b = \bar u\). The exponent in the convolution integrand is \(-\tfrac12\bigl(\tfrac{a^2}{s^2} + \tfrac{b^2}{t^2}\bigr)\). Substituting \(b = \bar u - a\) and collecting powers of \(a\), \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{a^2}{s^2} + \frac{(\bar u - a)^2}{t^2} &= \frac{v}{s^2 t^2}\, a^2 - \frac{2\bar u}{t^2}\, a + \frac{\bar u^2}{t^2}\\ &= \frac{v}{s^2 t^2}\left( a - \frac{s^2 \bar u}{v} \right)^{2} + \frac{\bar u^{2}}{t^{2}} - \frac{s^{2}\bar u^{2}}{v\, t^{2}} . \end{aligned} \] Since \(\dfrac{1}{t^2} - \dfrac{s^2}{v t^2} = \dfrac{v - s^2}{v t^2} = \dfrac{t^2}{v t^2} = \dfrac{1}{v}\), the last two terms combine to \(\bar u^2 / v\), giving the identity \[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{(x-\alpha)^2}{s^2} + \frac{(u - x - \beta)^2}{t^2} \\ &= \frac{v}{s^2 t^2}\left( x - \alpha - \frac{s^2 \bar u}{v} \right)^{2} + \frac{(u - \alpha - \beta)^2}{v}. \end{aligned} \tag{37.20}\] Let \(c \triangleq \alpha + s^2 \bar u / v\) and \(\sigma_*^2 \triangleq s^2 t^2 / v\), so the first term on the right of Equation 37.20 equals \((x - c)^2/\sigma_*^2\). Therefore \[ \begin{aligned} (\varphi_{\alpha,s^2} * \varphi_{\beta,t^2})(u) &= \frac{1}{2\pi s t} \int_{\mathbb{R}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2}\left[ \frac{(x-\alpha)^2}{s^2} + \frac{(u-x-\beta)^2}{t^2} \right] \right) \lambda(dx) \\ &= \frac{1}{2\pi s t}\, e^{-(u-\alpha-\beta)^2/(2v)} \int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-(x-c)^2/(2\sigma_*^2)} \, \lambda(dx) \\ &= \frac{1}{2\pi s t}\, e^{-(u-\alpha-\beta)^2/(2v)} \cdot \sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_* \\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\frac{\sigma_*}{st}\, e^{-(u-\alpha-\beta)^2/(2v)}\\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi v}}\, e^{-(u-\alpha-\beta)^2/(2v)}\\ &= \varphi_{\alpha+\beta,\,v}(u), \end{aligned} \] where the third equality uses \(\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-(x-c)^2/(2\sigma_*^2)}\,\lambda(dx) = \sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_*\) (translation invariance Equation 37.13 followed by the scaling substitution \(x = \sigma_* z\), legitimate by Theorem 12.2, and the Gaussian integral), and the fifth uses \(\sigma_*/(st) = \sqrt{s^2t^2/v}/(st) = 1/\sqrt{v}\). This holds for every \(u \in \mathbb{R}\).

Since \(\varphi_{\alpha+\beta,v}\) is a version of \(d(\mu*\nu)/d\lambda\) by Proposition 37.3, and it is also the density of \(\mathcal{N}(\alpha+\beta, v)\), the two measures assign the same value \(\int_B \varphi_{\alpha+\beta,v}\,d\lambda\) to every \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\), i.e. they are equal.

37.6 Partial Sum Laws and Scaling Behavior

Let \(\{X_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) be i.i.d. random vectors on \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\) with values in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) and common law \(\mu\), and let \(S_n \triangleq \sum_{k=1}^n X_k\) (so \(S_0 \triangleq 0\)).

Proposition 37.6 (Law of the Partial Sum) For every \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), \[ \mu_{S_n} = \mu_{S_{n-1}} * \mu_{X_n} = \mu^{*n} \triangleq \underbrace{\mu * \mu * \dots * \mu}_{n \text{ times}} . \tag{37.21}\]

Proof (Proof of Proposition 37.6). We argue by induction on \(n\). For \(n = 1\), \(\mu_{S_1} = \mu_{X_1} = \mu = \mu^{*1}\).

Assume \(\mu_{S_{n-1}} = \mu^{*(n-1)}\) for some \(n \ge 2\). The map \(f_1(x_1,\dots,x_{n-1}) = x_1 + \cdots + x_{n-1}\) is continuous, hence Borel, and \(f_2(x_n) = x_n\) is the identity; so by Corollary 33.3 (applied to the independent family \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) with blocks \(\{1,\dots,n-1\}\) and \(\{n\}\)), the random vectors \[ S_{n-1} = f_1(X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}) \qquad \text{and} \qquad X_n \] are independent. This is the step that requires genuine mutual independence of the family and does not follow from pairwise independence alone. Equivalently, this is the block-independence principle: measurable functions of disjoint blocks of an independent family are independent.

By Theorem 33.1 the joint law therefore factorizes, \(\mu_{(S_{n-1}, X_n)} = \mu_{S_{n-1}} \otimes \mu_{X_n}\). Applying Proposition 37.1 to the pair \((S_{n-1}, X_n)\) and then Definition 37.1, \[ \mu_{S_n}(B) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}} \mathbf{1}_B(x+y)\, \bigl(\mu_{S_{n-1}} \otimes \mu_{X_n}\bigr)(dx,dy) = \bigl(\mu_{S_{n-1}} * \mu_{X_n}\bigr)(B) \] for every \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\). Since \(X_n \sim \mu\), the induction hypothesis gives \(\mu_{S_n} = \mu^{*(n-1)} * \mu\), which equals \(\mu^{*n}\) unambiguously by the associativity established in Proposition 37.2.

37.6.1 Normalizing sequences

We now specialize to \(d = 1\) and ask how to choose a positive normalizing sequence \(b_n \to \infty\) so that \(S_n / b_n\) has a non-degenerate limiting behavior. Note that the two computations below require different moment hypotheses. Stabilization requires convergence of a ratio, which in particular implies a corresponding \(\Theta\) bound; a \(\Theta\) bound by itself is not sufficient.

  • Mean stabilization. Assume \(X_1 \in L^1(P)\) with \(\mathbb{E}[X_1] \ne 0\). By linearity of expectation, \[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \frac{S_n}{b_n} \right] = \frac{n}{b_n} \, \mathbb{E}[X_1]. \] This converges to a finite nonzero limit if and only if \(n/b_n\) converges to a finite nonzero limit. Equivalently, \(b_n/n\) converges to a finite positive limit. This condition implies \(b_n = \Theta(n)\), but the converse is false: for example, \(b_n = n(1 + \tfrac12(-1)^n)\) is positive and satisfies \(b_n = \Theta(n)\), while \(n/b_n\) does not converge. The weaker condition \(b_n = \mathcal{O}(n)\) would not suffice: taking \(b_n = \log(n+1)\) gives \(b_n \to \infty\) and \(b_n = \mathcal{O}(n)\), yet \(n/b_n \to \infty\), so the mean diverges. Conversely \(b_n = n^2\) gives \(n/b_n \to 0\) and the mean degenerates to \(0\).

  • Variance stabilization. Assume \(X_1 \in L^2(P)\) with \(\operatorname{Var}(X_1) \in (0,\infty)\). Since \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are independent and in \(L^2\), they are pairwise uncorrelated by Corollary 33.2, so the Bienaymé identity Equation 25.7 gives \(\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = n \operatorname{Var}(X_1)\) and hence \[ \operatorname{Var}\!\left( \frac{S_n}{b_n} \right) = \frac{n}{b_n^2} \, \operatorname{Var}(X_1). \] This converges to a finite nonzero limit if and only if \(n/b_n^2\) converges to a finite nonzero limit. Equivalently, \(b_n/\sqrt{n}\) converges to a finite positive limit. This condition implies \(b_n = \Theta(\sqrt{n})\), but the converse is false for the same oscillation reason. Again \(b_n = \mathcal{O}(\sqrt n)\) is insufficient: \(b_n = n^{1/4}\) satisfies \(b_n \to \infty\) and this upper bound, while \(\operatorname{Var}(S_n/b_n) = n^{1/2}\operatorname{Var}(X_1) \to \infty\).

The requirements \(b_n/n \to c_1 \in (0,\infty)\) and \(b_n/\sqrt n \to c_2 \in (0,\infty)\) are mutually incompatible, so they cannot both be met when \(\mathbb{E}[X_1] \ne 0\). This is precisely why one centers first: replacing \(S_n\) by \(S_n - n\mathbb{E}[X_1]\) makes the mean identically zero for every \(b_n\). Under the finite, positive variance hypothesis, the canonical choice \(b_n = \sqrt{n}\,\sigma\) with \(\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X_1)\) is then a scale at which the variance is exactly constant. The Gaussian case shows that at this scale the entire law, not merely its first two moments, can be exactly stationary in \(n\).

Theorem 37.1 (Exact Scaling Theorem for Gaussian i.i.d. Sequences) Let \(\{X_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) be i.i.d. \(\mathcal{N}(\alpha, s^2)\) random variables with \(\alpha \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(s \in (0, \infty)\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[S_n] = n\alpha\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = n s^2 \in (0,\infty)\), and for every \(n \in \mathbb{N}\): \[ \text{Law}\left( \frac{S_n - n\alpha}{\sqrt{n}\,s} \right) = \text{Law}\left( \frac{S_n - \mathbb{E}[S_n]}{\sqrt{\text{Var}(S_n)}} \right) = \mathcal{N}(0, 1). \tag{37.22}\]

Proof (Proof of Theorem 37.1). Step 1: Moments of \(S_n\). Gaussian random variables with finite variance lie in \(L^2(P)\), so the \(X_k\) are independent \(L^2\) random variables. By linearity, \(\mathbb{E}[S_n] = n\alpha\). They are pairwise uncorrelated by Corollary 33.2, so Equation 25.7 gives \(\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = n s^2\), which lies in \((0,\infty)\) because \(s > 0\). Hence \(\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(S_n)} = \sqrt{n}\,s \ne 0\) and both normalizations in Equation 37.22 are well defined and identical. This proves the first equality.

Step 2: Law of \(S_n\). By Proposition 37.6, \(\mu_{S_n} = \mu^{*n}\) with \(\mu = \mathcal{N}(\alpha, s^2)\). Applying Proposition 37.5 and induction on \(n\): the case \(n=1\) is trivial, and if \(\mu^{*(n-1)} = \mathcal{N}((n-1)\alpha, (n-1)s^2)\) then \[ \mu^{*n} = \mathcal{N}\bigl((n-1)\alpha, (n-1)s^2\bigr) * \mathcal{N}(\alpha, s^2) = \mathcal{N}(n\alpha, n s^2), \] the hypotheses of Proposition 37.5 being met since \((n-1)s^2 > 0\) for \(n \ge 2\) and \(s^2 > 0\). Hence \(\mu_{S_n} = \mathcal{N}(n\alpha, ns^2)\).

Step 3: The affine normalization. Write \(T_n \triangleq (S_n - n\alpha)/(\sqrt n\, s)\) and fix \(t \in \mathbb{R}\). Since \(\sqrt n\,s > 0\), the event \(\{T_n \le t\}\) equals \(\{S_n \le n\alpha + \sqrt n\, s\, t\}\), so by Step 2 \[ P(T_n \le t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\,n\alpha + \sqrt n\, s\, t} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n s^2}}\, e^{-(x - n\alpha)^2/(2 n s^2)} \, \lambda(dx). \] Apply the affine substitution \(x = n\alpha + \sqrt n\, s\, u\). This is the pushforward of \(\lambda\) under an invertible affine map, and combining translation invariance Equation 37.14 with the scaling identity Equation 12.6 (with \(\gamma = \sqrt n\, s\)) gives \(\lambda(dx) = \sqrt{n}\,s\,\lambda(du)\) in the sense of Corollary 24.1. The exponent becomes \(-u^2/2\), the prefactor \(\sqrt n\, s / \sqrt{2\pi n s^2} = 1/\sqrt{2\pi}\), and the upper limit becomes \(t\). Therefore \[ P(T_n \le t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-u^2/2} \, \lambda(du) = \Phi(t), \qquad t \in \mathbb{R}. \]

Step 4: From the CDF to the law. The two Borel probability measures \(\mu_{T_n}\) and \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) agree on the collection \(\{(-\infty, t] : t \in \mathbb{R}\}\), which is a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\). By Lemma 32.1 they agree on all of \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\), i.e. \(\text{Law}(T_n) = \mathcal{N}(0,1)\).
