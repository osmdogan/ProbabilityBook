42  Lecture 20.1: Cramer’s LDP-Part I

42.1 Introduction: Rare Events and SLLN

Let \(\{X_n\}_{n \ge 1}\) be an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sequence of random variables in \(L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\), and define the partial sums: \[S_n = \sum_{k=1}^n X_k\]

By the Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN), the sample average converges almost surely to the expected value: \[\frac{S_n}{n} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \mathbb{E}[X_1] \quad \text{as } n \to \infty\]

If we further assume \(X_1 \in L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) with \(\operatorname{Var}(X_1) = \sigma^2 < \infty\), then, because the \(X_k\) are independent and identically distributed, variances add: \[\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{S_n}{n}\right) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{k=1}^n \operatorname{Var}(X_k) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}.\] Since \(\mathbb{E}[S_n/n] = \mathbb{E}[X_1]\), Chebyshev’s inequality gives, for every fixed \(\epsilon > 0\), \[\mathbb{P}\left(\left| \frac{S_n}{n} - \mathbb{E}[X_1] \right| > \epsilon \right) \le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(S_n/n)}{\epsilon^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n \epsilon^2} = O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\]

Thus, the event \(\left\{ \left| \frac{S_n}{n} - \mathbb{E}[X_1] \right| > \epsilon \right\}\) is a rare event as \(n \to \infty\).

To understand the exact decay rate of such rare events without pathological behavior, we impose a stronger regularity condition on \(X_n\).

Definition 42.1 (Exponential Integrability) A random variable \(X\) is called exponentially integrable if \[ \mathbb{E}\left[e^{t|X|}\right]<\infty \quad\text{for every }t>0. \]

Remark 42.1. Exponential integrability implies the existence of moments of all orders. Indeed, fix an integer \(p \ge 1\) and any \(t > 0\). Since every term of the series \(e^{s} = \sum_{j \ge 0} s^j/j!\) is nonnegative for \(s = t|X| \ge 0\), retaining only the \(j = p\) term gives the pointwise bound \[ e^{t|X|} \geq \frac{(t|X|)^p}{p!}. \] Both sides are nonnegative, so monotonicity of the expectation applies and yields \[ \mathbb{E}[|X|^p] \leq \frac{p!}{t^p}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t|X|}\right] <\infty. \] Therefore \(X \in L^p(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) for every integer \(p \ge 1\), and hence for every real \(p \ge 1\) by \(|X|^p \le 1 + |X|^{\lceil p \rceil}\).

42.2 Moments and Cumulant Generating Functions

When \(X\) is exponentially integrable, its moment-generating properties are exceptionally well-behaved.

Definition 42.2 (MGF and CGF) Let \(X\) be an exponentially integrable random variable.

  1. The Moment Generating Function (MGF) of \(X\) is defined as: \[M_X(t) := \mathbb{E}\left[e^{tX}\right], \quad t \in \mathbb{R}\]

  2. The Cumulant Generating Function (CGF) of \(X\) is defined as: \[\Psi_X(t) := \log M_X(t) = \log \mathbb{E}\left[e^{tX}\right], \quad t \in \mathbb{R}\] The number \(C_k(X) := \Psi_X^{(k)}(0)\) is called the \(k\)-th cumulant of \(X\).

Proposition 42.1 (Regularity of the MGF and CGF) Let \(X\) be exponentially integrable. Then:

  1. \(M_X(t) \in (0, \infty)\) for every \(t \in \mathbb{R}\), and \(M_X\) is entire (real-analytic on all of \(\mathbb{R}\)) with the globally convergent power series \[M_X(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{E}[X^k] \frac{t^k}{k!} = 1 + \mathbb{E}[X]t + \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[X^2]t^2 + \dots\]
  2. \(\Psi_X = \log M_X\) is real-analytic on all of \(\mathbb{R}\), and it admits a Taylor expansion \[\Psi_X(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} C_k(X) \frac{t^k}{k!}\] valid on some interval \((-r, r)\) with \(r > 0\). (The radius \(r\) need not be infinite: the Taylor series of \(\log M_X\) at \(0\) is limited by the complex zeros of \(M_X\), even though \(\Psi_X\) itself is analytic on all of \(\mathbb{R}\).)

Proof. (1) Positivity is clear since \(e^{tX} > 0\) pointwise. Finiteness: \(0 < e^{tX} \le e^{|t||X|}\) pointwise, so \(M_X(t) \le \mathbb{E}[e^{|t||X|}] < \infty\) for \(t \ne 0\) by Definition 42.1, and \(M_X(0) = 1\). For the series, apply Tonelli’s theorem to the nonnegative terms \(|X|^k|t|^k/k!\): \[\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{|t|^k}{k!}\mathbb{E}[|X|^k] = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(|t||X|)^k}{k!}\right] = \mathbb{E}\left[e^{|t||X|}\right] < \infty .\] This absolute convergence licenses Fubini’s theorem for the interchange of \(\mathbb{E}\) and \(\sum\) applied to \(e^{tX} = \sum_k (tX)^k/k!\), giving the stated series for every \(t \in \mathbb{R}\). A power series convergent on all of \(\mathbb{R}\) defines a real-analytic function there.

(2) \(\log\) is analytic on \((0,\infty)\) and \(M_X > 0\) by part (1), so the composition \(\Psi_X = \log \circ M_X\) is real-analytic on \(\mathbb{R}\); in particular it is \(C^\infty\), so all cumulants \(C_k(X) = \Psi_X^{(k)}(0)\) exist, and analyticity at \(0\) gives convergence of the Taylor series on some interval \((-r,r)\), \(r > 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

42.3 Explicit Expressions for the First Two Cumulants

We work on the interval \((-r, r)\) of Proposition 42.1, where all expansions below converge. Using the standard logarithmic Taylor series \(\log(1 + u) = u - \frac{u^2}{2} + O(u^3)\) as \(u \to 0\) (legitimate because \(u := M_X(t) - 1 \to 0\) as \(t \to 0\)):

\[\begin{aligned} \Psi_X(t) &= \log \left( 1 + \mathbb{E}[X]t + \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[X^2]t^2 + O(t^3) \right) \\ &= \left( \mathbb{E}[X]t + \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[X^2]t^2 \right) - \frac{1}{2}\left( \mathbb{E}[X]t \right)^2 + O(t^3) \\ &= \mathbb{E}[X]t + \frac{1}{2}\left( \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 \right)t^2 + O(t^3) \end{aligned}\]

Comparing coefficients or differentiating directly gives:

  1. First Cumulant (Mean): \[C_1(X) = \Psi_X'(0) = \frac{M_X'(0)}{M_X(0)} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{1} = \mathbb{E}[X]\]

  2. Second Cumulant (Variance): \[C_2(X) = \Psi_X''(0) = \frac{M_X''(0)M_X(0) - (M_X'(0))^2}{(M_X(0))^2} = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X)\]

42.4 Chernoff’s Bound for Upper Deviations

Throughout this section, \(\{X_n\}_{n\ge1}\) is i.i.d. and \(X_1\) is exponentially integrable, and \(\epsilon > 0\) is fixed. How rare is it for \(\frac{S_n}{n} - \mathbb{E}[X_1] > \epsilon\)?

Define the centered random variables: \[\dot{X}_k := X_k - \mathbb{E}[X_1], \quad \text{so that } \mathbb{E}[\dot{X}_k] = 0\] \[\dot{S}_n := \sum_{k=1}^n \dot{X}_k = S_n - n\mathbb{E}[X_1]\]

Two facts we will use repeatedly, both immediate but worth stating:

  • The \(\dot X_k\) are i.i.d. Each \(\dot X_k = f(X_k)\) for the same Borel function \(f(x) = x - \mathbb{E}[X_1]\), and measurable functions of independent random variables are independent; identical distribution is preserved likewise.
  • \(\dot X_1\) is exponentially integrable. Writing \(m := \mathbb{E}[X_1]\), the triangle inequality gives \(|\dot X_1| \le |X_1| + |m|\), hence for every \(t > 0\) \[\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t|\dot X_1|}\right] \le e^{t|m|}\,\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t|X_1|}\right] < \infty .\] So Proposition 42.1 applies to \(\dot X_1\), and \(M_{\dot X_1}, \Psi_{\dot X_1}\) are finite on all of \(\mathbb{R}\).

The event of interest becomes: \[\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{S_n}{n} - \mathbb{E}[X_1] > \epsilon\right) = \mathbb{P}(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon)\]

Fix \(t > 0\). Since \(s \mapsto e^{ts}\) is strictly increasing for \(t > 0\), the events \(\{\dot S_n > n\epsilon\}\) and \(\{e^{t\dot S_n} > e^{tn\epsilon}\}\) coincide. Applying Markov’s inequality to the nonnegative random variable \(e^{t\dot S_n}\) (which is integrable by exponential integrability of \(\dot X_1\)):

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon) &= \mathbb{P}\left(e^{t\dot{S}_n} > e^{tn\epsilon}\right) \quad \text{(valid because } t > 0) \\ &\le e^{-tn\epsilon} \, \mathbb{E}\left[e^{t\dot{S}_n}\right] \quad \text{(by Markov's inequality)} \end{aligned}\]

By independence of \(\dot{X}_1, \dots, \dot{X}_n\) (the factors \(e^{t\dot X_k}\) are independent, nonnegative and integrable, so the expectation of the product is the product of the expectations), and by their identical distribution: \[\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t\dot{S}_n}\right] = \mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^n e^{t\dot{X}_k}\right] = \prod_{k=1}^n \mathbb{E}\left[e^{t\dot{X}_k}\right] = \left(M_{\dot{X}_1}(t)\right)^n\]

Substituting \(M_{\dot{X}_1}(t) = e^{\Psi_{\dot{X}_1}(t)}\): \[\mathbb{P}(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon) \le e^{-tn\epsilon} \left(e^{\Psi_{\dot{X}_1}(t)}\right)^n = \exp\left(-n \left( t\epsilon - \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}(t) \right)\right)\]

Since this inequality holds for every \(t > 0\), we may take the infimum of the right-hand side over \(t > 0\): \[\mathbb{P}(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon) \le \inf_{t>0} \exp\left(-n(t\epsilon - \Psi_{\dot X_1}(t))\right) = \exp\left(-n \sup_{t > 0} \{ t\epsilon - \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}(t) \} \right)\] where the last equality uses that \(u \mapsto e^{-nu}\) is decreasing.

42.5 The Legendre Transform and Properties of \(\Psi_{\dot{X}}\)

Definition 42.3 (Legendre Transform (Convex Conjugate)) Let \(\psi: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) be a convex function. Its Legendre transform (convex conjugate) is the function \(\psi^*: \mathbb{R} \to (-\infty, +\infty]\) defined by \[\psi^*(\eta) := \sup_{t \in \mathbb{R}} \{ \eta t - \psi(t) \}\] The value \(+\infty\) is genuinely allowed (take \(\psi \equiv 0\)), so the codomain must be the extended half-line. The value \(-\infty\) never occurs, since \(\psi^*(\eta) \ge \eta\cdot 0 - \psi(0) = -\psi(0) > -\infty\). As a pointwise supremum of the affine functions \(\eta \mapsto \eta t - \psi(t)\), \(\psi^*\) is automatically convex and lower semicontinuous.

Example 42.1 (Example: Gaussian Distribution) Let \(\sigma^2 > 0\) and \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\). Then \(Z\) is exponentially integrable and \(\Psi_Z(t) = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t^2\). Fix \(\eta \in \mathbb{R}\) and set \[h(t) := \eta t - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t^2 .\] Since \(h''(t) = -\sigma^2 < 0\), \(h\) is strictly concave, and \(h(t) \to -\infty\) as \(|t| \to \infty\); hence the supremum is attained at the unique stationary point. Solving \(h'(t) = \eta - \sigma^2 t = 0\) gives \(t^* = \eta/\sigma^2\), and substituting back: \[\Psi_Z^*(\eta) = h(t^*) = \eta\left(\frac{\eta}{\sigma^2}\right) - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\left(\frac{\eta}{\sigma^2}\right)^2 = \frac{\eta^2}{2\sigma^2}\] In particular the supremum in Definition 42.3 is a maximum here, and it is finite for every \(\eta\).

Theorem 42.1 (Convexity of the Cumulant Generating Function) For any exponentially integrable random variable \(X\), its CGF \(\Psi_X(t)\) is convex on \(\mathbb{R}\). If additionally \(\operatorname{Var}(X) > 0\) (i.e., \(X\) is non-degenerate), then \(\Psi_X(t)\) is strictly convex.

Proof. Step 1 (differentiation under the expectation). Fix \(t_0 \in \mathbb{R}\) and let \(\delta := 1\). For \(|t - t_0| \le \delta\) we have the domination \[\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial t} e^{tX}\right| = |X| e^{tX} \le |X| e^{(|t_0|+1)|X|} \le e^{(|t_0|+2)|X|},\] using \(|x| \le e^{|x|}\), and the right-hand side is integrable by Definition 42.1. The same bound with \(X^2\) in place of \(|X|\) (and \(|t_0|+3\) in the exponent) handles the second derivative. Hence \(M_X\) is twice differentiable with \[M_X'(t) = \mathbb{E}\left[X e^{tX}\right], \qquad M_X''(t) = \mathbb{E}\left[X^2 e^{tX}\right],\] both finite for every \(t \in \mathbb{R}\). (This also follows from Proposition 42.1.)

Step 2 (the second derivative of the CGF). Since \(M_X(t) > 0\), the quotient rule applied to \(\Psi_X'(t) = M_X'(t)/M_X(t)\) gives, for an arbitrary \(t \in \mathbb{R}\), \[\Psi_X''(t) = \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[X^2 e^{tX}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tX}\right] - \left(\mathbb{E}\left[X e^{tX}\right]\right)^2}{\left(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tX}\right]\right)^2} = \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[X^2 e^{tX}\right]}{M_X(t)} - \left( \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[X e^{tX}\right]}{M_X(t)} \right)^2\]

Step 3 (the tilted measure). Introduce the exponentially tilted probability measure \(\mathbb{P}_t\) via the Radon-Nikodym derivative: \[\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_t := \frac{e^{tX}}{M_X(t)} \, \mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\]

The density is nonnegative and \(\mathbb{P}\)-integrable, and \(\mathbb{P}_t(\Omega) = \int d\mathbb{P}_t = M_X(t)/M_X(t) = 1\), so \(\mathbb{P}_t\) is indeed a probability measure on \((\Omega,\mathcal{F})\). By the change-of-measure formula, and since \(X, X^2\) are \(\mathbb{P}_t\)-integrable by Step 1, \[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_t}[X] = \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[X e^{tX}\right]}{M_X(t)}, \qquad \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_t}[X^2] = \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[X^2 e^{tX}\right]}{M_X(t)}\]

Hence \(\Psi_X''(t) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_t}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_t}[X])^2 = \operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb{P}_t}(X) \ge 0\) for every \(t \in \mathbb{R}\). A twice-differentiable function with nonnegative second derivative on \(\mathbb{R}\) is convex, establishing convexity.

Step 4 (strict convexity). Since \(d\mathbb{P}_t/d\mathbb{P} = e^{tX}/M_X(t) > 0\) everywhere, \(\mathbb{P}_t\) and \(\mathbb{P}\) are mutually absolutely continuous (\(\mathbb{P}_t \sim \mathbb{P}\)). They therefore share the same null sets, so \(\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb{P}_t}(X) = 0 \iff X\) is \(\mathbb{P}_t\)-a.s. constant \(\iff X\) is \(\mathbb{P}\)-a.s. constant \(\iff \operatorname{Var}(X) = 0\). When \(\operatorname{Var}(X) > 0\), we conclude \(\Psi_X''(t) = \operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb{P}_t}(X) > 0\) for every \(t \in \mathbb{R}\); a positive second derivative on all of \(\mathbb{R}\) gives strict convexity. \(\blacksquare\)

42.6 Analysis of the Supremum over \(t > 0\)

Let \(\dot{X}_1\) be centered and exponentially integrable, and fix \(\epsilon > 0\). Define \[g(t) := \epsilon t - \Psi_{\dot X_1}(t), \qquad t \in \mathbb{R}.\] By Proposition 42.1, \(g\) is finite and differentiable on all of \(\mathbb{R}\), and by Theorem 42.1 it is concave (an affine function minus a convex function). Its values at the origin are:

  1. \(g(0) = -\Psi_{\dot{X}_1}(0) = -\log M_{\dot X_1}(0) = -\log(1) = 0\).
  2. \(g'(0) = \epsilon - \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}'(0) = \epsilon - \mathbb{E}[\dot{X}_1] = \epsilon > 0\), using \(C_1(\dot X_1) = \mathbb{E}[\dot X_1] = 0\).

From these two facts:

  • On \(t \le 0\) the supremum is \(0\). A differentiable concave function lies below each of its tangent lines, so for every \(t \in \mathbb{R}\), \[g(t) \le g(0) + g'(0)(t - 0) = \epsilon t .\] For \(t < 0\) this gives \(g(t) \le \epsilon t < 0 = g(0)\). Combined with \(g(0)=0\), we get \(\sup_{t \le 0} g(t) = g(0) = 0\).
  • On \(t > 0\) the supremum is strictly positive. Since \(g(0)=0\) and \(g'(0) = \epsilon > 0\), the definition of the derivative gives \(g(t)/t \to \epsilon > 0\) as \(t \downarrow 0\), so \(g(t) > 0\) for all sufficiently small \(t > 0\). Hence \(\sup_{t > 0} g(t) > 0\).
  • Consequently the two suprema agree. Because \(\sup_{t>0} g > 0 = \sup_{t \le 0} g\), \[\sup_{t > 0} \{ \epsilon t - \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}(t) \} = \sup_{t \in \mathbb{R}} \{ \epsilon t - \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}(t) \} = \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}^*(\epsilon) \in (0, +\infty]\]

Thus, our bound simplifies directly to: \[\mathbb{P}(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon) \le e^{-n \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}^*(\epsilon)}\] with the convention \(e^{-\infty} = 0\) if \(\Psi_{\dot X_1}^*(\epsilon) = +\infty\).

Taking logarithms (monotone), dividing by \(n > 0\), and passing to the limit superior gives the upper bound: \[\limsup_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \log \mathbb{P}(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon) \le -\Psi_{\dot{X}_1}^*(\epsilon)\] valid in \([-\infty, \infty)\), with \(\log 0 := -\infty\).

42.7 Main Result: Cramér’s Large Deviation Theorem

Theorem 42.2 (Cramér’s Large Deviation Theorem (Upper Bound & Exact Rate)) Let \(\{X_n\}_{n \ge 1}\) be an i.i.d. sequence of exponentially integrable random variables which are non-degenerate, i.e. \(\operatorname{Var}(X_1) > 0\). Let \(\dot{X}_n := X_n - \mathbb{E}[X_1]\), \(\dot{S}_n := \sum_{k=1}^n \dot{X}_k\), and set \[b := \operatorname*{ess\,sup} \dot X_1 \in (0, +\infty] .\] (Non-degeneracy together with \(\mathbb{E}[\dot X_1] = 0\) forces \(b > 0\).) Then for every \(\epsilon\) with \(0 < \epsilon < b\): \[\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \log \mathbb{P}\left( \frac{\dot{S}_n}{n} > \epsilon \right) = -\Psi_{\dot{X}_1}^*(\epsilon)\] with the conventions \(\log 0 := -\infty\) and \(-(+\infty) := -\infty\).

Remark 42.2. The restriction \(\epsilon < b\) is needed for the strict inequality \(\dot S_n/n > \epsilon\) used here. If \(b < \infty\) and \(\mathbb{P}(\dot X_1 = b) > 0\), then at \(\epsilon = b\) the left-hand side equals \(-\infty\) (the event is empty), whereas \(\Psi_{\dot X_1}^*(b) = -\log \mathbb{P}(\dot X_1 = b)\) is finite, so the identity fails at that single point. For \(\epsilon > b\) both sides equal \(-\infty\), so the statement remains true there. Only the upper bound \[\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\log\mathbb{P}(\dot S_n > n\epsilon) \le -\Psi^*_{\dot X_1}(\epsilon)\] is proved above; it requires no assumption beyond exponential integrability and holds for every \(\epsilon > 0\). The matching lower bound is proved in Part 2.

42.8 Significance and Modern Terminology

This result formalizes how rare the tail event \(\left\{ \frac{S_n}{n} - \mathbb{E}[X_1] > \epsilon \right\} = \{\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon\}\) is:

  • The probability decays exponentially fast in \(n\).
  • The exact exponential rate of decay is governed by the Legendre transform value \(-\Psi_{\dot{X}_1}^*(\epsilon)\).

Historically, Harald Cramér referred to such rare tail events as large deviations.

In modern terminology, writing \(\mu_n\) for the law of \(\dot{S}_n / n\) on \((\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))\), one says that the sequence \(\{\mu_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) satisfies a Large Deviations Principle (LDP) with rate function \(I(x) = \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}^*(x)\), \(x \in \mathbb{R}\), and speed \(n\). The computation above is exactly the upper-bound half of that principle for the half-lines \((\epsilon, \infty)\).

42.9 Takeaways

  1. From polynomial to exponential decay. The SLLN (\(X_1 \in L^1\)) gives \(S_n/n \to \mathbb{E}[X_1]\) a.s., and Chebyshev (\(X_1 \in L^2\)) gives polynomial decay \(\mathbb{P}(|S_n/n - \mathbb{E}[X_1]| > \epsilon) = O(1/n)\). Exponential integrability enables Chernoff’s method, which yields the far sharper exponential bound \(\mathbb{P}(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon) \le e^{-n\Psi_{\dot{X}_1}^*(\epsilon)}\).

  2. Chernoff’s bound: Markov \(+\) i.i.d. factorization \(+\) optimization. For any \(t > 0\), strict monotonicity of \(s \mapsto e^{ts}\) lets us rewrite the event, and Markov’s inequality applied to \(e^{t\dot{S}_n}\) gives \(e^{-tn\epsilon}\mathbb{E}[e^{t\dot{S}_n}]\). Independence and identical distribution factor the expectation into \(M_{\dot{X}_1}(t)^n = e^{n\Psi_{\dot{X}_1}(t)}\), yielding \(e^{-n(t\epsilon - \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}(t))}\). Taking the infimum over all \(t > 0\) produces the rate \(\sup_{t>0}\{t\epsilon - \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}(t)\}\).

  3. The supremum over \(t > 0\) equals the full Legendre transform. Centering gives \(\Psi_{\dot{X}_1}'(0) = 0\), so \(g(t) = \epsilon t - \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}(t)\) satisfies \(g(0) = 0\) and \(g'(0) = \epsilon > 0\). The tangent upper bound for the concave function \(g\) gives \(g(t) \le \epsilon t < 0\) for \(t < 0\), so \(\sup_{t \le 0} g = 0\); and \(g'(0) > 0\) forces \(g > 0\) just to the right of \(0\), so \(\sup_{t > 0} g > 0\). Hence the restricted supremum over \(t > 0\) equals the full Legendre transform \(\Psi_{\dot{X}_1}^*(\epsilon) \in (0,+\infty]\).

  4. Convexity of the CGF via the tilted measure. \(\Psi_X''(t) = \operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb{P}_t}(X) \ge 0\) for every \(t \in \mathbb{R}\), where \(d\mathbb{P}_t = (e^{tX}/M_X(t))\,d\mathbb{P}\) is the exponentially tilted measure; differentiation under the expectation is justified by exponential integrability. Since \(\mathbb{P}_t \sim \mathbb{P}\) (mutually absolutely continuous), strict convexity holds whenever \(\operatorname{Var}(X) > 0\): the tilted measure cannot degenerate a non-constant \(X\).

  5. Cramér’s theorem gives the exact exponential rate. The Chernoff bound establishes the upper bound \(\limsup_{n} \frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon) \le -\Psi_{\dot{X}_1}^*(\epsilon)\) for every \(\epsilon > 0\). The matching lower bound, proved in Part 2 for \(0 < \epsilon < \operatorname{ess\,sup}\dot X_1\) using the tilted measure \(\mathbb{P}_{t_\epsilon}\) (where \(t_\epsilon\) is the Legendre maximizer), yields the exact limit \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon) = -\Psi_{\dot{X}_1}^*(\epsilon)\).
