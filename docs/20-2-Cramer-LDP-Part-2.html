43  Lecture 20.2: Cramer’s LDP-Part II

43.1 Theorem Statement & Context

Let \(\{X_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\) be a sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), exponentially integrable random variables defined on a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\). We assume throughout that \(X_1\) is non-degenerate, i.e. \(\operatorname{Var}(X_1) > 0\).

Define the centered random variables and their sample sum: \[ \dot{X}_n := X_n - \mathbb{E}[X_1], \quad \text{and} \quad \dot{S}_n := \sum_{i=1}^n \dot{X}_i . \] As shown in Part I, each \(\dot X_n\) is again exponentially integrable, the \(\dot X_n\) are i.i.d., and \(\mathbb{E}[\dot X_1] = 0\).

Also recall that the moment generating function (MGF) and cumulant generating function (CGF) of \(\dot{X}_1\) are defined as: \[ M_{\dot{X}_1}(t) := \mathbb{E}\left[e^{t\dot{X}_1}\right], \quad \text{and} \quad \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}(t) := \log M_{\dot{X}_1}(t), \] both finite for every \(t \in \mathbb{R}\).

The Legendre-Fenchel transform of the CGF is given by: \[ \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}^*(\epsilon) := \sup_{t \in \mathbb{R}} \left\{\epsilon t - \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}(t)\right\} = \sup_{t \ge 0} \left\{\epsilon t - \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}(t)\right\}, \] where the second equality holds for every \(\epsilon > 0\) and was proved in Part I (the concave function \(t \mapsto \epsilon t - \Psi_{\dot X_1}(t)\) vanishes at \(t=0\), has positive derivative there, and lies below the line \(t \mapsto \epsilon t\), so nothing is lost by restricting to \(t \ge 0\)).

Finally, set \[ b := \operatorname*{ess\,sup} \dot X_1 \in (0, +\infty] , \] which is strictly positive because \(\mathbb{E}[\dot X_1] = 0\) and \(\dot X_1\) is not a.s. constant, so \(\mathbb{P}(\dot X_1 > 0) > 0\).

Then, Cramér’s Large Deviation Principle (LDP) states that the tail probability of the sample sum \(\dot{S}_n\) decays exponentially with rate \(\Psi_{\dot{X}_1}^*(\epsilon)\):

Theorem 43.1 (Cramér’s Large Deviation Theorem) For every threshold \(\epsilon\) with \(0 < \epsilon < b\), the exponential decay rate of the tail probability is given by: \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \log \mathbb{P}\left(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon\right) = -\Psi_{\dot{X}_1}^*(\epsilon) < 0 . \]

Remark 43.1. The restriction \(\epsilon < b\) is genuinely needed. If \(b < \infty\) and \(\mathbb{P}(\dot X_1 = b) > 0\), then at \(\epsilon = b\) the event \(\{\dot S_n > nb\}\) is null, so the left-hand side is \(-\infty\), while \(\Psi^*_{\dot X_1}(b) = -\log\mathbb{P}(\dot X_1 = b) < \infty\). For \(\epsilon > b\) both sides equal \(-\infty\) and the identity survives. Strict negativity of the right-hand side is the statement \(\Psi^*_{\dot X_1}(\epsilon) > 0\) proved in Part I.

From Part I, we have already established the upper bound: \[ \limsup_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \log \mathbb{P}\left(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon\right) \le -\Psi_{\dot{X}_1}^*(\epsilon). \]

Thus, to complete the proof of Cramér’s Theorem, it suffices to prove the matching lower bound: \[ \liminf_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \log \mathbb{P}\left(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon\right) \ge -\Psi_{\dot{X}_1}^*(\epsilon). \]

43.2 Analytical Setup & Preliminary Notation

For readability, we adopt the following standard shorthand notation:

  • Moment Generating Function (MGF): \(M(t) := M_{\dot{X}_1}(t) = \mathbb{E}\left[e^{t\dot{X}_1}\right]\)
  • Cumulant Generating Function (CGF): \(\Psi(t) := \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}(t) = \log M(t)\)
  • Legendre-Fenchel Transform (Rate Function): \(\Psi^*(\epsilon) := \Psi_{\dot{X}_1}^*(\epsilon) = \sup_{t \ge 0} \{\epsilon t - \Psi(t)\}\)

43.3 Existence and Uniqueness of the Maximizer \(t_\epsilon\)

The whole proof hinges on the existence of a point at which the supremum defining \(\Psi^*(\epsilon)\) is attained. This is not automatic — it is exactly where the hypothesis \(\epsilon < b\) enters — so we prove it.

Lemma 43.1 (Attainment of the Legendre Supremum) Assume \(\dot X_1\) is exponentially integrable, non-degenerate, and \(\mathbb{E}[\dot X_1] = 0\). For every \(\epsilon\) with \(0 < \epsilon < b = \operatorname*{ess\,sup}\dot X_1\) there exists a unique \(t_\epsilon \in \mathbb{R}\) with \[ \Psi'(t_\epsilon) = \epsilon , \] it satisfies \(t_\epsilon > 0\), and the supremum is attained there: \[ \Psi^*(\epsilon) = \sup_{t \ge 0}\{\epsilon t - \Psi(t)\} = \epsilon t_\epsilon - \Psi(t_\epsilon) < \infty . \]

Proof. By Part I, \(\Psi\) is real-analytic on \(\mathbb{R}\) and, by non-degeneracy, strictly convex; hence \(\Psi'\) is continuous and strictly increasing. Moreover \(\Psi(0) = 0\) and \(\Psi'(0) = \mathbb{E}[\dot X_1] = 0\).

Claim: \(\Psi'(t) \uparrow b\) as \(t \to +\infty\).

Upper bound. If \(b < \infty\) then \(\dot X_1 \le b\) a.s., so \(\dot X_1 e^{t\dot X_1} \le b\,e^{t\dot X_1}\) pointwise a.s. and \[ \Psi'(t) = \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\dot X_1 e^{t\dot X_1}\right]}{M(t)} \le \frac{b\,M(t)}{M(t)} = b . \] If \(b = \infty\) the bound is vacuous.

Lower bound. First, \(\frac{1}{t}\Psi(t) \to b\) as \(t \to +\infty\). Indeed, for any \(c < b\) we have \(p_c := \mathbb{P}(\dot X_1 > c) > 0\) by definition of the essential supremum, so \[ M(t) \ge \mathbb{E}\left[e^{t\dot X_1}\mathbb{I}_{\{\dot X_1 > c\}}\right] \ge e^{tc} p_c \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\Psi(t)}{t} \ge c + \frac{\log p_c}{t} \xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{} c , \] and letting \(c \uparrow b\) gives \(\liminf_{t\to\infty}\Psi(t)/t \ge b\); conversely \(M(t) \le e^{tb}\) when \(b<\infty\), so \(\Psi(t)/t \le b\). Second, convexity of \(\Psi\) together with \(\Psi(0)=0\) gives the tangent-line inequality \(0 = \Psi(0) \ge \Psi(t) + \Psi'(t)(0-t)\), i.e. \[ \Psi'(t) \ge \frac{\Psi(t)}{t}\qquad (t > 0). \] Combining, \(\liminf_{t\to\infty}\Psi'(t) \ge b\), which with the upper bound proves the claim.

Conclusion. \(\Psi'\) is a continuous, strictly increasing map with \(\Psi'(0) = 0\) and \(\sup_{t>0}\Psi'(t) = b\). Since \(0 < \epsilon < b\), the intermediate value theorem supplies a point \(t_\epsilon > 0\) with \(\Psi'(t_\epsilon) = \epsilon\), and strict monotonicity makes it unique. Finally, \(g(t) := \epsilon t - \Psi(t)\) is concave and differentiable with \(g'(t_\epsilon) = \epsilon - \Psi'(t_\epsilon) = 0\); a stationary point of a concave function is a global maximum, so \(\Psi^*(\epsilon) = g(t_\epsilon) = \epsilon t_\epsilon - \Psi(t_\epsilon)\), which is finite. \(\blacksquare\)

43.4 Essential Construct: Exponential Tilting (Esscher Transform)

Given an exponentially integrable random variable \(Y\) on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\), we construct a family of exponentially tilted probability measures \(\mathbb{P}_t\) on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) parameterized by \(t \in \mathbb{R}\):

\[ d\mathbb{P}_t := \frac{e^{tY}}{M_Y(t)} \, d\mathbb{P}. \]

NoteVerification of Probability Measure

The density \(e^{tY}/M_Y(t)\) is nonnegative, \(\mathbb{P}\)-integrable, and in fact strictly positive everywhere. Integrating over the entire sample space \(\Omega\): \[ \int_{\Omega} d\mathbb{P}_t = \int_{\Omega} \frac{e^{tY}}{M_Y(t)} \, d\mathbb{P} = \frac{1}{M_Y(t)} \int_{\Omega} e^{tY} \, d\mathbb{P} = \frac{M_Y(t)}{M_Y(t)} = 1. \] This confirms that \(\mathbb{P}_t\) is a valid probability measure for any \(t\) with \(M_Y(t) < \infty\), which by exponential integrability means every \(t \in \mathbb{R}\). Strict positivity of the density further gives \(\mathbb{P}_t \sim \mathbb{P}\) (mutual absolute continuity), so \(\mathbb{P}\)-a.s. statements and \(\mathbb{P}_t\)-a.s. statements are interchangeable.

43.5 Main Proposition: The Lower Bound

Theorem 43.2 (Lower Bound Estimate) Under the standing assumptions (i.i.d., exponentially integrable, non-degenerate), for every \(\epsilon\) with \(0 < \epsilon < b\), \[ \liminf_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \log \mathbb{P}\left(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon\right) \ge -\Psi^*(\epsilon) = \Psi(t_\epsilon) - \epsilon t_\epsilon \] where \(t_\epsilon\) is the maximizer supplied by Lemma 43.1.

43.6 Detailed Proof

Throughout the proof \(\epsilon \in (0,b)\) is fixed, and we freeze the tilting parameter at the maximizer \(t_\epsilon\) once and for all. This matters: the auxiliary variables constructed below depend on \(t_\epsilon\), so the parameter cannot be left free and optimized afterwards.

43.6.1 Step 1: Characterization of the Tilted Measure

By Lemma 43.1 the maximizer \(t_\epsilon > 0\) satisfies the first-order condition \(\Psi'(t_\epsilon) = \epsilon\). Unpacking the derivative of the CGF (differentiation under the expectation being justified in Part I by exponential integrability): \[ \begin{align*} \Psi'(t) &= \frac{d}{dt} \log M(t) = \frac{M'(t)}{M(t)} = \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\dot{X}_1 e^{t\dot{X}_1}\right]}{M(t)} \\ &= \int_{\Omega} \dot{X}_1 \frac{e^{t\dot{X}_1}}{M(t)} \, d\mathbb{P} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_t}[\dot{X}_1]. \end{align*} \]

Therefore, setting \(t = t_\epsilon\) explicitly forces the expectation under the tilted measure to equal \(\epsilon\): \[ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{t_\epsilon}}[\dot{X}_1] = \epsilon. \]

Denote by \(\mu := \mu_{\dot X_1}\) the law of \(\dot X_1\) under \(\mathbb{P}\) and define the tilted law on \((\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))\): \[ \nu(dx) := \frac{e^{t_\epsilon x}}{M(t_\epsilon)}\, \mu(dx) . \] This is the law of \(\dot X_1\) under \(\mathbb{P}_{t_\epsilon}\), and it is a probability measure by the callout above. Since its density is strictly positive, the relation inverts: \[ \mu(dx) = M(t_\epsilon)\, e^{-t_\epsilon x}\, \nu(dx) . \]

Let \(\{W_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\) be an i.i.d. sequence with common law \(\nu\), realised on an auxiliary probability space \((\tilde\Omega, \tilde{\mathcal{F}}, \tilde{\mathbb{P}})\) (take the product space \((\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}, \nu^{\otimes\mathbb{N}})\) with the coordinate maps); expectations on this space are written \(\tilde{\mathbb{E}}\). Keeping the two spaces distinct is what makes the change-of-measure identity of Step 2 an honest statement rather than a notational coincidence.

The \(W_n\) inherit the following three properties, all of which are used later:

  1. Mean. \(\tilde{\mathbb{E}}[W_n] = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{t_\epsilon}}[\dot X_1] = \Psi'(t_\epsilon) = \epsilon\). This is the point of tilting: the rare event \(\{\dot S_n > n\epsilon\}\) becomes the typical event \(\{T_n > n\epsilon\}\).
  2. Finite variance. \(\tilde{\mathbb{E}}[W_1^2] = \mathbb{E}[\dot X_1^2 e^{t_\epsilon \dot X_1}]/M(t_\epsilon) < \infty\), since \(\dot X_1^2 e^{t_\epsilon\dot X_1} \le e^{(2 + t_\epsilon)|\dot X_1|}\) is integrable by exponential integrability.
  3. Non-degeneracy. \(\sigma_W^2 := \operatorname{Var}_{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}(W_1) = \operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb{P}_{t_\epsilon}}(\dot X_1) = \Psi''(t_\epsilon) > 0\), by the strict convexity of \(\Psi\) established in Part I. Hence \(\sigma_W^2 \in (0,\infty)\) and the CLT of Step 5 applies.

43.6.2 Step 2: Change of Measure for \(n\)-dimensional Product Space

Let \(f: \mathbb{R}^n \to [0, \infty]\) be Borel measurable and non-negative (so that every integral below is defined in \([0,\infty]\) and Tonelli’s theorem licenses the factorisation). Because \(\dot X_1,\dots,\dot X_n\) are i.i.d. with law \(\mu\), their joint law is the product measure \(\mu^{\otimes n}\); likewise \(W_1,\dots,W_n\) are i.i.d. with joint law \(\nu^{\otimes n}\). Substituting \(\mu(dx) = M(t_\epsilon)e^{-t_\epsilon x}\nu(dx)\) coordinatewise:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[f(\dot{X}_1, \dots, \dot{X}_n)\right] &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f(x_1, \dots, x_n) \, \mu(dx_1) \cdots \mu(dx_n) \\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f(x_1, \dots, x_n) \prod_{i=1}^n \left( M(t_\epsilon) e^{-t_\epsilon x_i} \nu(dx_i) \right) \\ &= M(t_\epsilon)^n \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f(x_1, \dots, x_n) e^{-t_\epsilon \sum_{i=1}^n x_i} \, \nu(dx_1) \cdots \nu(dx_n) \\ &= M(t_\epsilon)^n \, \tilde{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(W_1, \dots, W_n) e^{-t_\epsilon T_n}\right], \end{aligned} \]

where \(T_n := \sum_{i=1}^n W_i\). Note that the identity relates an expectation on \((\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb{P})\) to one on the auxiliary space \((\tilde\Omega,\tilde{\mathcal F},\tilde{\mathbb{P}})\).

43.6.3 Step 3: Bounding the Indicator Function

Apply the identity to the (non-negative, Borel) indicator \(f(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \mathbb{I}_{\{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i > n\epsilon\}}\):

\[ \mathbb{P}\left(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon\right) = M(t_\epsilon)^n \, \tilde{\mathbb{E}}\left[ e^{-t_\epsilon T_n} \mathbb{I}_{\{T_n > n\epsilon\}} \right]. \]

Now fix \(\delta > 0\) and shrink the domain of integration to the slice \(\{n\epsilon < T_n \le n(\epsilon+\delta)\}\). The strict lower endpoint is essential: this slice is a subset of \(\{T_n > n\epsilon\}\), whereas the closed slice \(\{n\epsilon \le T_n \le \cdots\}\) need not be, since the law of \(T_n\) may carry an atom at \(n\epsilon\). On the slice \(e^{-t_\epsilon T_n} \ge e^{-t_\epsilon n(\epsilon+\delta)}\), because \(t_\epsilon > 0\). Hence

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}\left(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon\right) &\ge M(t_\epsilon)^n \, \tilde{\mathbb{E}}\left[ e^{-t_\epsilon T_n} \mathbb{I}_{\{n\epsilon < T_n \le n(\epsilon + \delta)\}} \right] \\ &\ge M(t_\epsilon)^n e^{-t_\epsilon n(\epsilon + \delta)} \, \tilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(n\epsilon < T_n \le n(\epsilon + \delta)\right) \\ &= e^{n(\Psi(t_\epsilon) - t_\epsilon\epsilon)} e^{-n t_\epsilon \delta} \, \tilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(n\epsilon < T_n \le n(\epsilon + \delta)\right), \end{aligned} \]

using \(M(t_\epsilon)^n = e^{n\Psi(t_\epsilon)}\) in the last line. Since \(\Psi(t_\epsilon) - t_\epsilon \epsilon = -\Psi^*(\epsilon)\) by Lemma 43.1,

\[ \mathbb{P}\left(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon\right) \ge e^{-n \Psi^*(\epsilon)} e^{-n t_\epsilon \delta} \, \tilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(n\epsilon < T_n \le n(\epsilon + \delta)\right). \]

43.6.4 Step 4: Taking the Logarithmic Limit

Step 5 shows the last factor is eventually positive, so the right-hand side is eventually strictly positive and we may apply the increasing map \(\frac{1}{n}\log(\cdot)\) to both sides. This turns the product into a sum, and taking \(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\) (using superadditivity of \(\liminf\)) gives:

\[ \begin{align*} \liminf_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \log \mathbb{P}\left(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon\right) &\ge -\Psi^*(\epsilon) - t_\epsilon \delta \\ &+ \liminf_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \log \tilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(n\epsilon < T_n \le n(\epsilon + \delta)\right) \quad (\star) \end{align*} \]

43.6.5 Step 5: Asymptotic Analysis of the Probability Term via CLT

To complete the proof, we show that the last probability term in \((\star)\) vanishes on the log scale as \(n \to \infty\); equivalently, that it stays bounded away from \(0\).

By Step 1, \(\tilde{\mathbb{E}}[T_n] = \sum_{i=1}^n \tilde{\mathbb{E}}[W_i] = n\epsilon\) and \(\sigma_W^2 = \operatorname{Var}(W_1) \in (0,\infty)\). Write the normalised sum \[ Z_n := \frac{T_n - n\epsilon}{\sigma_W\sqrt{n}} , \] so that, splitting the slice into two half-lines, \[ \tilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(n\epsilon < T_n \le n(\epsilon + \delta)\right) = \tilde{\mathbb{P}}\!\left(Z_n \le \frac{\delta\sqrt{n}}{\sigma_W}\right) - \tilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z_n \le 0\right). \]

The two terms are handled by different theorems.

  • Second term (CLT). The \(W_i\) are i.i.d. with finite, non-zero variance, so the Lindeberg-Lévy CLT gives \(Z_n \Rightarrow \mathcal{N}(0,1)\). The limit distribution function \(\Phi\) is continuous, so \(0\) is a continuity point and \[ \tilde{\mathbb{P}}(Z_n \le 0) \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} \Phi(0) = \frac{1}{2}. \]
  • First term (WLLN). Here the threshold \(\delta\sqrt{n}/\sigma_W \to \infty\), so the CLT cannot be invoked at a fixed point; use instead the weak law of large numbers, \(T_n/n \to \epsilon\) in \(\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\)-probability: \[ \tilde{\mathbb{P}}\!\left(Z_n > \frac{\delta\sqrt n}{\sigma_W}\right) = \tilde{\mathbb{P}}\!\left(\frac{T_n}{n} - \epsilon > \delta\right) \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 0, \] so the first term tends to \(1\).

Combining, \[ \lim_{n \to \infty}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(n\epsilon < T_n \le n(\epsilon + \delta)\right) = 1 - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2} > 0 , \] which is the rigorous form of the heuristic \(\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2 / 2} \, dx = \frac{1}{2}\). In particular the probability exceeds \(\tfrac14\) for all large \(n\), so the sandwich

\[ 0 \ge \frac{1}{n} \log \tilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(n\epsilon < T_n \le n(\epsilon + \delta)\right) \ge \frac{\log(1/4)}{n} \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 0 \]

gives \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \log \tilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(n\epsilon < T_n \le n(\epsilon + \delta)\right) = 0 . \]

Substituting back into \((\star)\):

\[ \liminf_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \log \mathbb{P}\left(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon\right) \ge -\Psi^*(\epsilon) - t_\epsilon \delta \]

The left-hand side does not depend on \(\delta\), and \(t_\epsilon\) is a fixed finite number, so letting \(\delta \downarrow 0\) yields the desired lower bound:

\[ \liminf_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \log \mathbb{P}\left(\dot{S}_n > n\epsilon\right) \ge -\Psi^*(\epsilon) \quad \blacksquare \]

Together with the upper bound from Part I, this proves Theorem 43.1 for every \(\epsilon \in (0,b)\).

43.7 Takeaways

  1. The proof of the lower bound in Cramér’s LDP relies on exponential tilting (the Esscher transform) to construct a new probability measure under which the rare event becomes typical: the tilting parameter is chosen to be the Legendre maximizer \(t_\epsilon\), precisely so that \(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{t_\epsilon}}[\dot X_1] = \Psi'(t_\epsilon) = \epsilon\). The parameter must be frozen at \(t_\epsilon\) before the auxiliary variables \(W_n\) are defined, since their law depends on it.
  2. Existence of \(t_\epsilon\) is a theorem, not a formality. It holds exactly when \(0 < \epsilon < \operatorname{ess\,sup}\dot X_1\), because \(\Psi'\) increases strictly from \(\Psi'(0) = 0\) up to \(\operatorname{ess\,sup}\dot X_1\). Outside this range the supremum is not attained and the argument breaks down.
  3. The Central Limit Theorem (lower endpoint of the slice) and the weak law of large numbers (upper endpoint) together show that the tilted sum lands in the slice with probability tending to \(\tfrac12\), hence with no exponential decay; this is what isolates the exponential rate in the original measure.
  4. The slice must have a strict lower endpoint, \(\{n\epsilon < T_n \le n(\epsilon+\delta)\}\): the law of \(T_n\) may have an atom at \(n\epsilon\), so the closed slice need not be contained in \(\{T_n > n\epsilon\}\).
  5. The error factor \(e^{-n t_\epsilon \delta}\) is removed at the very end by letting \(\delta \downarrow 0\), which is legitimate because the left-hand side of the bound does not depend on \(\delta\).
  6. The combination of these techniques establishes the matching lower bound, completing the proof of Cramér’s Large Deviation Principle.
