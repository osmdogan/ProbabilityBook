44.1 Convergence Revisited

In earlier sections, we considered several modes of convergence for random variables \(\{X_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) to \(X\) defined on a common probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\):

  1. Almost Sure Convergence (\(X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} X\)): \[ \mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{n \to \infty} X_n = X\right) = 1 \iff \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{k \ge n} \{|X_k - X| > \epsilon\}\right) = 0, \] for all \(\epsilon>0\).

  2. Convergence in \(L^p\) (\(X_n \xrightarrow{L^p} X\)): \[ \begin{align*} \lim_{n \to \infty} \|X_n - X\|_{L^p}^p& = \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{E}\left[|X_n - X|^p\right] \\ &= \lim_{n \to \infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^2} |y - x|^p \, \mu_{(X_n, X)}(dy, dx)=0, \end{align*} \] where the joint distribution is \(\mu_{(X_n,X)}=\mathbb{P}\circ(X_n,X)^{-1}\). Equivalently \[ \lim_{n \to \infty}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_n - X|^p\right] = \lim_{n \to \infty}\int_{\Omega} |X_n(\omega) - X(\omega)|^p\,\mathbb{P}(d\omega)=0, \]

  3. Convergence in Probability (\(X_n \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}} X\)): \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}\left(|X_n - X| > \epsilon\right) = \lim_{n \to \infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^2} \mathbb{I}_{\{|y - x| > \epsilon\}} \, \mu_{(X_n, X)}(dy, dx)=0. \] Equivalently, \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}\left(|X_n - X| > \epsilon\right) = \lim_{n \to \infty}\int_{\Omega} \mathbb{I}_{\{|X_n(\omega) - X(\omega)| > \epsilon\}}\mathbb{P}(d\omega)= 0. \]

NoteKey Observation

All of these modes of convergence require information about the joint distribution \(\mu_{(X_n, X)}\) of \((X_n, X)\). We now turn to notions of convergence that depend only on individual distributions.

44.2 Total Variation Distance

Let \(\mu, \nu\) be probability measures on a measurable space \((S, \mathcal{B})\). Because probability measures are real-valued functions on the \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{B}\), we can equip them with natural function-space metrics.

Definition 44.1 (Total Variation Distance) Let \(\mu, \nu\) be probability measures on \((S, \mathcal{B})\). The Total Variation Distance between them is defined by: \[ d_{\text{TV}}(\mu, \nu) := \sup_{B \in \mathcal{B}} |\mu(B) - \nu(B)|. \] If \(X\) and \(Y\) are \((S, \mathcal{B})\)-valued random variables, we set: \[ d_{\text{TV}}(X, Y) := d_{\text{TV}}(\mu_X, \mu_Y) = \sup_{B \in \mathcal{B}} |\mathbb{P}(X \in B) - \mathbb{P}(Y \in B)|. \]

Some Observations:

  1. If \(d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\nu)=0\), then \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) assign exactly the same probability to every event, so \(\mu=\nu\).
  2. If \(d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\nu)\) is small, then no event can have substantially different probabilities under the two measures.
  3. If \(d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\nu)\) is close to \(1\), there is an event that strongly distinguishes the two distributions.
  4. The maximum possible value is \(1\).

44.3 Scheffé’s Lemma

The following lemma, known as Scheffé’s Lemma, provides an important characterization of the total variation distance in terms of densities with respect to a dominating measure.

Lemma 44.1 (Scheffé’s Lemma) Let \(\mu, \nu \in \text{Prob}(S, \mathcal{B})\) and let \(\alpha\) be a \(\sigma\)-finite measure on \((S, \mathcal{B})\) such that \(\mu \ll \alpha\) and \(\nu \ll \alpha\). By the Radon-Nikodym theorem there exist non-negative densities \(u, v \in L^1(\alpha)\) with \(d\mu = u \, d\alpha\), \(d\nu = v \, d\alpha\) and \(\int_S u \, d\alpha = \int_S v \, d\alpha = 1\). Then: \[ d_{\text{TV}}(\mu, \nu) = \frac{1}{2} \|u - v\|_{L^1(\alpha)} = \frac{1}{2} \int_S |u - v| \, d\alpha. \]

Proof (Proof of Scheffé’s Lemma). For any event \(B \in \mathcal{B}\): \[ |\mu(B) - \nu(B)| = \left| \int_B u \, d\alpha - \int_B v \, d\alpha \right| \le \int_B |u - v| \, d\alpha \tag{44.1}\]

Similarly, applying (1) to the complement \(B^c \in \mathcal{B}\) and using \(\mu(B^c) = 1 - \mu(B)\), \(\nu(B^c) = 1 - \nu(B)\) (here we use that \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are probability measures): \[ \begin{aligned} |\mu(B) - \nu(B)| &= |(1 - \mu(B)) - (1 - \nu(B))| \\ &= |\mu(B^c) - \nu(B^c)| \le \int_{B^c} |u - v| \, d\alpha \end{aligned} \tag{44.2}\]

Adding inequalities Equation 44.1 and Equation 44.2: \[ \begin{aligned} 2 |\mu(B) - \nu(B)| &\le \int_B |u - v| \, d\alpha + \int_{B^c} |u - v| \, d\alpha\\ & = \int_S |u - v| \, d\alpha = \|u - v\|_{L^1(\alpha)} \end{aligned} \] Taking the supremum over all \(B \in \mathcal{B}\) yields: \[ d_{\text{TV}}(\mu, \nu) \le \frac{1}{2} \|u - v\|_{L^1(\alpha)} \]

To achieve equality, define \(A := \{x \in S : u(x) > v(x)\}\). Since \(\int_S u \, d\alpha = \int_S v \, d\alpha = 1\), we have \(\int_S (u - v) \, d\alpha = 0\). Thus: \[ \int_A (u - v) \, d\alpha + \int_{A^c} (u - v) \, d\alpha = 0 \implies \int_A |u - v| \, d\alpha = \int_{A^c} |u - v| \, d\alpha \] Evaluating \(|\mu(A) - \nu(A)|\): \[ \begin{aligned} |\mu(A) - \nu(A)| &= \int_A (u - v) \, d\alpha \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \left( \int_A |u - v| \, d\alpha + \int_{A^c} |u - v| \, d\alpha \right)\\ &= \frac{1}{2} \int_S |u - v| \, d\alpha \end{aligned} \] Since \(A \in \mathcal{B}\), the upper bound is attained, establishing \(d_{\text{TV}}(\mu, \nu) = \frac{1}{2} \|u - v\|_{L^1(\alpha)}\). \(\blacksquare\)

NoteConstruction of a Dominating Measure \(\alpha\)

A common dominating measure always exists for a countable family. For any sequence \(\{\mu_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset \text{Prob}(S, \mathcal{B})\), set: \[ \alpha = \sum_{n=1}^\infty 2^{-n} \mu_n . \] This is a well-defined measure with \(\alpha(S) = \sum_{n \ge 1} 2^{-n} = 1\), so \(\alpha\) is finite (indeed a probability measure). If \(\alpha(B) = 0\) then \(2^{-k}\mu_k(B) \le \alpha(B) = 0\), hence \(\mu_k \ll \alpha\) for every \(k\), and by the Radon-Nikodym theorem \(d\mu_k = u_k \, d\alpha\) with \(u_k \ge 0\) and \(\int_S u_k \, d\alpha = \mu_k(S) = 1\).

(For a sequence of general finite measures one normalizes first, e.g. \(\alpha = \sum_{n \ge 1} 2^{-n} \mu_n / (1 + \mu_n(S))\).)

44.4 Properties of the Total Variation Metric

Next, we discuss some important properties of the total variation metric. The following corollary summarizes these properties.

Corollary 44.1 (Completeness) \(d_{\text{TV}}\) is a complete metric on \(\text{Prob}(S, \mathcal{B})\), the space of probability measures on \((S, \mathcal{B})\).

Proof (Proof of Corollary 44.1). Given any countable family of measures in \(\text{Prob}(S, \mathcal{B})\), construct the dominating probability measure \(\alpha\) as above and let \(u_n\) denote the density \(d\mu_n = u_n \, d\alpha\). All quantities below are then computed via Lemma 44.1 with respect to this common \(\alpha\).

  1. Metric Axioms: Let \(\mu_1, \mu_2, \mu_3 \in \text{Prob}(S, \mathcal{B})\) and take \(\alpha\) dominating these three measures.
    • Finiteness and non-negativity: \(0 \le d_{\text{TV}}(\mu_1, \mu_2) = \frac{1}{2}\|u_1 - u_2\|_{L^1(\alpha)} \le \frac{1}{2}(\|u_1\|_{L^1(\alpha)} + \|u_2\|_{L^1(\alpha)}) = 1\).
    • Symmetry: \(|\mu_1(B) - \mu_2(B)| = |\mu_2(B) - \mu_1(B)|\) for every \(B \in \mathcal{B}\), hence \(d_{\text{TV}}(\mu_1, \mu_2) = d_{\text{TV}}(\mu_2, \mu_1)\).
    • Identity of indiscernibles: \(d_{\text{TV}}(\mu_1, \mu_2) = 0 \iff \frac{1}{2}\int_S |u_1 - u_2| d\alpha = 0 \iff u_1 = u_2\) \(\alpha\)-a.s. \(\iff \mu_1 = \mu_2\).
    • Triangle inequality holds by \(L^1(\alpha)\) norm bounds: \[ \begin{align*} d_{\text{TV}}(\mu_1, \mu_3) &= \frac{1}{2} \|u_1 - u_3\|_{L^1(\alpha)} \le \frac{1}{2}\left(\|u_1 - u_2\|_{L^1(\alpha)} + \|u_2 - u_3\|_{L^1(\alpha)}\right)\\ & = d_{\text{TV}}(\mu_1, \mu_2) + d_{\text{TV}}(\mu_2, \mu_3) \end{align*} \]
  2. Completeness: Let \(\{\mu_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset \text{Prob}(S, \mathcal{B})\) be \(d_{\text{TV}}\)-Cauchy and let \(\alpha = \sum_{n \ge 1} 2^{-n}\mu_n\) with \(d\mu_n = u_n \, d\alpha\). Since \(\|u_n - u_m\|_{L^1(\alpha)} = 2 d_{\text{TV}}(\mu_n, \mu_m)\), the sequence \(\{u_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) is Cauchy in \(L^1(\alpha)\). By completeness of \(L^1(\alpha)\), there exists \(u \in L^1(\alpha)\) such that \(u_n \to u\) in \(L^1(\alpha)\). Passing to a subsequence converging \(\alpha\)-a.s. gives \(u \ge 0\) \(\alpha\)-a.s., and \(\left|\int_S u \, d\alpha - 1\right| = \left|\int_S (u - u_n) \, d\alpha\right| \le \|u - u_n\|_{L^1(\alpha)} \to 0\) gives \(\int_S u \, d\alpha = 1\). Define \(d\mu := u \, d\alpha\), so \(\mu \in \text{Prob}(S, \mathcal{B})\). Then: \[ d_{\text{TV}}(\mu_n, \mu) = \frac{1}{2} \|u_n - u\|_{L^1(\alpha)} \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 0 \] Hence \(\mu_n \to \mu\) in \(d_{\text{TV}}\), establishing completeness. \(\blacksquare\)

44.5 Integral Bounds & Variational Representation

The following corollary provides bounds on integrals of bounded measurable functions with respect to probability measures in terms of the total variation distance.

Corollary 44.2 (Bounded Functions Characterization) If \(h : S \to \mathbb{R}\) is a bounded measurable function on \((S, \mathcal{B})\), then for any \(\mu, \nu \in \text{Prob}(S, \mathcal{B})\): \[ \left| \int_S h \, d\mu - \int_S h \, d\nu \right| \le 2 d_{\text{TV}}(\mu, \nu) \cdot \sup_{x \in S} |h(x)| \] Furthermore, \(d_{\text{TV}}\) has the dual variational formulation: \[ \begin{aligned} d_{\text{TV}}(\mu, \nu) &= \frac{1}{2} \sup \biggl\{ \left| \int_S h \, d\mu - \int_S h \, d\nu \right| : h : S \to \mathbb{R} \text{ measurable},\\ &\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad \sup_{x \in S} |h(x)| \le 1 \biggr\} \end{aligned} \]

Proof (Proof of Corollary 44.2). Fix a dominating measure \(\alpha\) for the pair \(\{\mu, \nu\}\) (e.g. \(\alpha = \frac{1}{2}(\mu + \nu)\)) with \(d\mu = u \, d\alpha\) and \(d\nu = v \, d\alpha\). Since \(h\) is bounded and measurable, \(h \in L^1(\mu) \cap L^1(\nu)\) and all integrals below are finite: \[ \begin{align*} \left| \int_S h \, d\mu - \int_S h \, d\nu \right| &= \left| \int_S h(u - v) \, d\alpha \right| \le \int_S |h| |u - v| \, d\alpha \\ &\le \left(\sup_{x \in S} |h(x)|\right) \int_S |u - v| \, d\alpha \end{align*} \] By Lemma 44.1, \(\int_S |u - v| d\alpha = 2 d_{\text{TV}}(\mu, \nu)\), giving the first bound.

If \(\sup_S |h| \le 1\), this implies \(\left|\int_S h \, d\mu - \int_S h \, d\nu\right| \le 2 d_{\text{TV}}(\mu, \nu)\), so the supremum is at most \(2 d_{\text{TV}}(\mu, \nu)\). To achieve equality, choose \(h(x) = \mathbb{I}_{\{u > v\}}(x) - \mathbb{I}_{\{u \le v\}}(x)\), which is measurable because \(u\) and \(v\) are, and satisfies \(\sup_S |h| = 1 \le 1\). Substituting \(h\): \[ \begin{align*} \int_S h \, d\mu - \int_S h \, d\nu &= \int_S (\mathbb{I}_{\{u > v\}} - \mathbb{I}_{\{u \le v\}})(u - v) \, d\alpha \\ &= \int_{\{u > v\}} |u - v| \, d\alpha + \int_{\{u \le v\}} |u - v| \, d\alpha \\ &= \int_S |u - v| \, d\alpha = 2 d_{\text{TV}}(\mu, \nu) \end{align*} \] Taking the supremum establishes the variational representation. \(\blacksquare\)

44.6 Total Variation on Countable Spaces

Total variation distance simplifies substantially when the underlying space \(S\) is countable.

Lemma 44.2 (Countable State Space) Let \(S\) be a countable set equipped with \(\mathcal{B} = 2^S\) (so that every singleton \(\{k\}\) is measurable) and let \(\mu, \nu \in \text{Prob}(S, \mathcal{B})\). Then: \[ d_{\text{TV}}(\mu, \nu) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k \in S} |\mu(\{k\}) - \nu(\{k\})| \] Consequently, for \(\mu_n, \mu \in \text{Prob}(S, \mathcal{B})\): \(\mu_n \xrightarrow{d_{\text{TV}}} \mu \iff \mu_n(\{k\}) \to \mu(\{k\})\) pointwise for every \(k \in S\).

Proof (Proof of Lemma 44.2). Enumerate \(S = \{k_1, k_2, \dots\}\) and take the finite dominating measure \(\alpha = \sum_{k \in S} \alpha_k \delta_k\) with weights \(\alpha_{k_j} := 2^{-j} > 0\), so that \(\alpha(S) = \sum_{k \in S}\alpha_k \le 1 < \infty\). Since \(\alpha(B) = 0\) forces \(B = \emptyset\), every measure on \((S, 2^S)\) is absolutely continuous with respect to \(\alpha\), and the Radon-Nikodym densities are \(u(k) = \frac{\mu(\{k\})}{\alpha_k}\) and \(v(k) = \frac{\nu(\{k\})}{\alpha_k}\). Applying Lemma 44.1: \[ \begin{align*} d_{\text{TV}}(\mu, \nu) &= \frac{1}{2} \int_S |u - v| \, d\alpha = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k \in S} \alpha_k |u(k) - v(k)| \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k \in S} \left| u(k)\alpha_k - v(k)\alpha_k \right| = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k \in S} |\mu(\{k\}) - \nu(\{k\})| \end{align*} \]

For the convergence equivalence:

  • (\(\Rightarrow\)) Taking \(B = \{k\}\) in the definition gives \(|\mu_n(\{k\}) - \mu(\{k\})| \le d_{\text{TV}}(\mu_n, \mu) \to 0\) for each fixed \(k\).
  • (\(\Leftarrow\)) Suppose \(\mu_n(\{k\}) \to \mu(\{k\})\) for every \(k\). Set \[ g_n(k) := \mu_n(\{k\}) + \mu(\{k\}) - |\mu_n(\{k\}) - \mu(\{k\})| \ge 0 , \] where non-negativity follows from \(|a - b| \le a + b\) for \(a, b \ge 0\). By Fatou’s lemma for series (i.e. with respect to counting measure on \(S\)) and \(g_n(k) \to 2\mu(\{k\})\), \[ \begin{aligned} &2 = \sum_{k \in S} \liminf_{n \to \infty} g_n(k) \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k \in S} g_n(k) \\ &= 2 - \limsup_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k \in S} |\mu_n(\{k\}) - \mu(\{k\})|, \end{aligned} \] using \(\sum_{k} \mu_n(\{k\}) = \sum_{k} \mu(\{k\}) = 1\). Hence \(\limsup_n \sum_{k} |\mu_n(\{k\}) - \mu(\{k\})| \le 0\), i.e. \(d_{\text{TV}}(\mu_n, \mu) \to 0\).

44.7 Example 1: Bernoulli Distributions

Let \(\mu_p \triangleq \text{Bernoulli}(p)\) and \(\mu_q \triangleq \text{Bernoulli}(q)\) for \(p, q \in [0, 1]\). Using Lemma 44.2 on \(S = \{0, 1\}\): \[ \begin{align*} d_{\text{TV}}(\mu_p, \mu_q) &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_{x=0}^1 |\mu_p(\{x\}) - \mu_q(\{x\})|\\ &= \frac{1}{2} \left( |(1 - p) - (1 - q)| + |p - q| \right) = |p - q| \end{align*} \]

44.8 Example 2: Poisson Distribution

Let \(\nu_\lambda \triangleq \text{Poisson}(\lambda)\) where \(\nu_\lambda(\{k\}) = \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k}{k!}\) for \(k \in \mathbb{N}_0\). Consider \(\nu_\lambda\) and \(\nu_\eta\) for \(\lambda, \eta > 0\). Using Lemma 44.2 on \(S = \mathbb{N}_0\): \[ \begin{align*} d_{\text{TV}}(\nu_\lambda, \nu_\eta) &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=0}^\infty |\nu_\lambda(\{k\}) - \nu_\eta(\{k\})| = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \left| \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k}{k!} - \frac{e^{-\eta} \eta^k}{k!} \right| \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k!} \left| e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k - e^{-\eta} \eta^k \right| \leq 1 - e^{-|\lambda - \eta|} \le |\lambda - \eta|. \end{align*} \]

The last two bounds are justified as follows. Assume without loss of generality that \(\lambda \le \eta\) and let \(X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)\) and \(Z \sim \text{Poisson}(\eta - \lambda)\) be independent, so that \(Y := X + Z \sim \text{Poisson}(\eta)\) by the convolution property of Poisson laws. For any \(B \subseteq \mathbb{N}_0\), \[ \begin{aligned} |\nu_\lambda(B) - \nu_\eta(B)| &= |\mathbb{P}(X \in B) - \mathbb{P}(Y \in B)| \\ &\le \mathbb{P}(X \ne Y) = \mathbb{P}(Z \ne 0)\\ & = 1 - e^{-(\eta - \lambda)}, \end{aligned} \] and taking the supremum over \(B\) gives \(d_{\text{TV}}(\nu_\lambda, \nu_\eta) \le 1 - e^{-|\lambda - \eta|}\). The final inequality is the elementary bound \(1 - e^{-t} \le t\) for \(t \ge 0\).

44.9 Example 3: Bernoulli vs. Poisson Comparison

Fix \(p \in [0, 1]\) and regard both laws as elements of \(\text{Prob}(\mathbb{N}_0, 2^{\mathbb{N}_0})\). Consider \(d_{\text{TV}}(\mu_p, \nu_p)\) where \(\mu_p = \text{Bernoulli}(p)\) and \(\nu_p = \text{Poisson}(p)\). Since \(\mu_p(\{k\}) = 0\) for all \(k \ge 2\), Lemma 44.2 gives:

\[ \begin{align*} d_{\text{TV}}(\mu_p, \nu_p) &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_{x=0}^\infty |\mu_p(\{x\}) - \nu_p(\{x\})| \\ &= \frac{1}{2} |\mu_p(\{0\}) - \nu_p(\{0\})| \\ &\quad + \frac{1}{2} |\mu_p(\{1\}) - \nu_p(\{1\})| + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k \ge 2} \nu_p(\{k\}). \end{align*} \]

Evaluating individual terms:

  • \(\mu_p(\{0\}) - \nu_p(\{0\}) = 1 - p - e^{-p}\)
  • \(\mu_p(\{1\}) - \nu_p(\{1\}) = p - p e^{-p} = p(1 - e^{-p}) \ge 0\)
  • \(\sum_{k \ge 2} \nu_p(\{k\}) = 1 - \nu_p(\{0\}) - \nu_p(\{1\}) = 1 - e^{-p} - p e^{-p} \ge 0\)

Using the standard inequality \(1 - p \le e^{-p}\), we have \(1 - p - e^{-p} \le 0\), so \(|1 - p - e^{-p}| = e^{-p} + p - 1\).

Combining all components: \[ \begin{aligned} d_{\text{TV}}(\mu_p, \nu_p) &= \frac{1}{2} (e^{-p} + p - 1) + \frac{1}{2} p(1 - e^{-p}) + \frac{1}{2} (1 - e^{-p} - p e^{-p}) \\ &= \frac{1}{2} e^{-p} + \frac{1}{2} p - \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} p - \frac{1}{2} p e^{-p} + \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2} e^{-p} - \frac{1}{2} p e^{-p} \\ &= p (1 - e^{-p}). \end{aligned} \]

Thus, the exact total variation distance is: \[ d_{\text{TV}}(\text{Bernoulli}(p), \text{Poisson}(p)) = p (1 - e^{-p}). \]
