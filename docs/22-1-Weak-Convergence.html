46  Lecture 22.1: Weak Convergence

46.1 Motivation: Limitations of Total Variation Distance

In non-discrete probability settings, Total Variation (TV) convergence is often too strong or restrictive.

Definition 46.1 (Total Variation Distance) For two probability measures \(\mu, \nu \in \mathcal{P}(S, \mathcal{B}(S))\), the total variation distance is defined as: \[d_{TV}(\mu, \nu) = \sup_{B \in \mathcal{B}(S)} |\mu(B) - \nu(B)|\]

If \(\mu(A) = 0\) and \(\nu(A) = 1\) for some set \(A \in \mathcal{B}(S)\), then \(d_{TV}(\mu, \nu) = 1\).

Example 46.1 (Dirac Measures (Point Masses)) Let \(a_n, a \in \mathbb{R}\) with \(a_n \to a\) as \(n \to \infty\), and suppose that \(a_n \neq a\) for infinitely many \(n\). Intuitively, Dirac measures should satisfy \(\delta_{a_n} \to \delta_a\). For every \(n\) such that \(a_n \neq a\), evaluating their total variation distance gives: \[ \begin{align*} d_{TV}(\delta_{a_n}, \delta_a) &= \sup_{B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})} |\delta_{a_n}(B) - \delta_a(B)| \ge |\delta_{a_n}(\{a\}) - \delta_a(\{a\})|\\ & = |0 - 1| = 1. \end{align*} \]

Since \(d_{TV}\) is at most \(1\) for probability measures, \(d_{TV}(\delta_{a_n}, \delta_a)=1\) whenever \(a_n\neq a\). Thus it does not converge to \(0\), even though \(a_n \to a\). (If \(a_n=a\) eventually, then the total variation distance is eventually \(0\).)

Example 46.2 (Discrete Approximation of \(\text{Unif}([0,1])\)) Consider approximating the continuous uniform measure \(\lambda = \text{Unif}([0,1])\) on \([0,1]\) using discrete empirical measures: \[\mu_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n \delta_{k/n}\]

  • Evaluating \(d_{TV}\): Let \(A = \{1/n, 2/n, \dots, n/n\}\). Then \(\mu_n(A) = 1\), but \(\lambda(A) = 0\). \[d_{TV}(\mu_n, \lambda) \ge |\mu_n(A) - \lambda(A)| = 1 - 0 = 1\] Since total variation distance between probability measures is at most \(1\), \(d_{TV}(\mu_n, \lambda) = 1\) for every \(n\).

  • Evaluating via Integration (Riemann Sums): Recall the integration bound from Lecture 21.1: \[\left| \int_S h \, d\mu - \int_S h \, d\nu \right| \le 2 \, d_{TV}(\mu, \nu) \cdot \sup_{x \in S} |h(x)|\] Since \(d_{TV}(\mu_n, \lambda) = 1\) for every \(n\), this bound degenerates to \(2\sup|h|\) and carries no information. Yet the integrals themselves do converge: since \([0,1]\) is compact, \(C_b([0,1]) = C([0,1])\), and every \(h \in C([0,1])\) is Riemann integrable, so \[\int_{[0,1]} h \, d\mu_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n h\left(\frac{k}{n}\right) \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} \int_0^1 h(x) \, dx = \int_{[0,1]} h \, d\lambda\] because the left-hand side is the right-endpoint Riemann sum of \(h\) over the uniform partition of \([0,1]\) into \(n\) subintervals.

    Hence \(\mu_n \implies \lambda\) in the sense of Definition 46.2 below, even though \(d_{TV}(\mu_n, \lambda) = 1\) for all \(n\). Testing against continuous functions captures the limit, whereas \(d_{TV}\) fails due to its extreme sensitivity to singular sets.

46.2 Weak Convergence of Probability Measures

To bypass the rigidity of \(d_{TV}\), we restrict test functions to bounded continuous functions \(f \in C_b(S)\), which effectively “smear out” point masses.

Definition 46.2 (Weak Convergence (Convergence in Distribution)) Let \((S, d)\) be a metric space, and let \(\mu_n, \mu \in \mathcal{P}(S, \mathcal{B}(S))\). We say \(\mu_n\) converges weakly to \(\mu\), written as \(\mu_n \xrightarrow{w} \mu\) or \(\mu_n \implies \mu\), if: \[\int_S f \, d\mu_n \longrightarrow \int_S f \, d\mu \quad \forall f \in C_b(S)\]

Example 46.3 (Dirac Measures Revisited) If \(a_n \to a\) in \(S\), then for any \(f \in C_b(S)\): \[\int_S f \, d\delta_{a_n} = f(a_n) \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} f(a) = \int_S f \, d\delta_a\] Thus \(\delta_{a_n} \implies \delta_a\) holds under weak convergence.

Theorem 46.1 (TV Convergence Implies Weak Convergence) If \(d_{TV}(\mu_n, \mu) \to 0\), then \(\mu_n \implies \mu\).

Proof (Proof of Theorem 46.1). For any \(f \in C_b(S)\), \(\sup |f| < \infty\). By the integration bound: \[\left| \int_S f \, d\mu_n - \int_S f \, d\mu \right| \le 2 \, d_{TV}(\mu_n, \mu) \cdot \sup_{x \in S} |f(x)| \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 0\] Hence \(\int f \, d\mu_n \to \int f \, d\mu\).

46.3 Convergence Modes of Random Variables

Let \(X_n, X: (\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P) \to (S, \mathcal{B}(S))\) be random variables/elements. We write \(X_n \xrightarrow{w} X\) (or \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} X\)) if \(\mu_{X_n} \implies \mu_X\), where \(\mu_{X_n} = P \circ X_n^{-1}\).

Throughout this section \((S,d)\) is assumed separable, so that \(d : S \times S \to \mathbb{R}\) is \(\mathcal{B}(S) \otimes \mathcal{B}(S)\)-measurable and \(d(X_n, X)\) is a genuine random variable. Convergence in probability then means \(P(d(X_n, X) > \epsilon) \to 0\) for every \(\epsilon > 0\).

Lemma 46.1 (Lemma: Preservation of Convergence in Probability) Let \(g : S \to \mathbb{R}\) be continuous. If \(X_n \xrightarrow{P} X\), then \(g(X_n) \xrightarrow{P} g(X)\).

Proof (Proof of Lemma 46.1). To prove convergence in probability, it is enough to show that every subsequence of \((g(X_n))\) has a further subsequence converging almost surely to \(g(X)\). Let \((X_{n_k})\) be any subsequence. Since \(X_n \xrightarrow{P} X\), there exists a further subsequence \((X_{n_{k_j}})\) such that \[X_{n_{k_j}} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}} X.\] On the event of probability one on which this convergence holds, continuity of \(g\) gives \[g(X_{n_{k_j}}) \longrightarrow g(X).\] Thus every subsequence of \((g(X_n))\) has an almost surely convergent further subsequence with limit \(g(X)\), and hence \(g(X_n) \xrightarrow{P} g(X)\).

Proof (Alternative Proof of Lemma 46.1). Fix \(\epsilon>0\). For \(\delta>0\), define \[ B_{\epsilon,\delta} =\left\{x\in S: \exists\, y\in S \text{ such that } d(x,y)<\delta \text{ and } |g(x)-g(y)|>\epsilon\right\}. \] The set \(B_{\epsilon,\delta}\) is open: if \(x\in B_{\epsilon,\delta}\) has a witness \(y\), then the same strict inequalities continue to hold for all points sufficiently close to \(x\). In particular, it is Borel measurable. Moreover, as \(m\to\infty\), \[ B_{\epsilon,1/m}\downarrow\emptyset, \] because continuity of \(g\) at each \(x\) gives a neighborhood on which \(|g(x)-g(y)|\le\epsilon\). Hence, by continuity from above of the probability measure, \[ P(X\in B_{\epsilon,1/m})\longrightarrow0. \]

For every \(\delta>0\), \[ \{|g(X_n)-g(X)|>\epsilon\} \subseteq \{d(X_n,X)\ge\delta\}\cup\{X\in B_{\epsilon,\delta}\}. \] Indeed, if \(d(X_n,X)<\delta\) and \(X\notin B_{\epsilon,\delta}\), take \(x=X\) and \(y=X_n\) in the definition of \(B_{\epsilon,\delta}\) to obtain \(|g(X_n)-g(X)|\le\epsilon\).

Let \(\eta>0\). First choose \(m\) such that \(P(X\in B_{\epsilon,1/m})<\eta/2\). Since \(X_n\xrightarrow{P}X\), there exists \(N\) such that, for every \(n\ge N\), \[ P\bigl(d(X_n,X)\ge1/m\bigr)<\eta/2. \] The preceding inclusion then gives \[ P\bigl(|g(X_n)-g(X)|>\epsilon\bigr)<\eta\qquad(n\ge N), \] which proves \(g(X_n)\xrightarrow{P}g(X)\).

Theorem 46.2 (Convergence in Probability Implies Weak Convergence) If \(X_n \xrightarrow{P} X\), then \(X_n \xrightarrow{w} X\).

Proof (Proof of Theorem 46.2). Let \(f \in C_b(S)\).

  1. By Lemma 46.1, \(X_n \xrightarrow{P} X \implies f(X_n) \xrightarrow{P} f(X)\).
  2. If \(M=\|f\|_\infty\), then \(|f(X_n)-f(X)|\le 2M\). Split the expectation on \(\{|f(X_n)-f(X)|>\epsilon\}\) and its complement: for every \(\epsilon>0\), \[ \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}\left[|f(X_n)-f(X)|\right] &=\mathbb{E}\bigl[|f(X_n)-f(X)| \mathbf{1}_{\{|f(X_n)-f(X)|\le\epsilon\}}\bigr]\\ & +\mathbb{E}\bigl[|f(X_n)-f(X)| \mathbf{1}_{\{|f(X_n)-f(X)|>\epsilon\}}\bigr]\\ & \le \epsilon+2M\,P\bigl(|f(X_n)-f(X)|>\epsilon\bigr). \end{align*} \] The probability on the right tends to \(0\), so \(\limsup_n\mathbb{E}|f(X_n)-f(X)|\le\epsilon\). Letting \(\epsilon\downarrow0\) shows that \(f(X_n)\to f(X)\) in \(L^1\), and therefore \[\mathbb{E}[f(X_n)] \longrightarrow \mathbb{E}[f(X)].\]
  3. Rewriting in terms of pushforward measures: \[\int_S f \, d\mu_{X_n} = \mathbb{E}[f(X_n)] \longrightarrow \mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \int_S f \, d\mu_X\] Hence \(X_n \xrightarrow{w} X\).

Corollary 46.1 (Almost Sure and \(L^p\) Convergence) If \(X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} X\), then \(X_n \xrightarrow{w} X\). If in addition \(S\) is a normed space (e.g. \(S = \mathbb{R}^d\)) and \(X_n \xrightarrow{L^p} X\) for some \(p \ge 1\), then \(X_n \xrightarrow{w} X\).

Proof (Proof of Corollary 46.1). Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability: \(\mathbf{1}_{\{d(X_n,X) > \epsilon\}} \to 0\) a.s., so \(P(d(X_n,X)>\epsilon) \to 0\) by dominated convergence. Convergence in \(L^p\) implies convergence in probability by Markov’s inequality, \(P(\|X_n - X\| > \epsilon) \le \epsilon^{-p}\,\mathbb{E}\|X_n - X\|^p \to 0\). In either case Theorem 46.2 applies.

46.4 Continuity Sets

Total variation distance \(d_{TV}\) fails for sequences of distributions because it evaluates arbitrary sets, including “discontinuity sets”.

Definition 46.3 (Continuity Set) Let \(\mu\) be a Borel probability measure on a metric space \(S\). A set \(A \in \mathcal{B}(S)\) is called a \(\mu\)-continuity set if: \[\mu(\partial A) = 0\] where \(\partial A = \bar{A} \setminus \text{int}(A)\) is the boundary of \(A\). Since \(\text{int}(A) \subseteq A \subseteq \bar{A}\) and \(\bar{A} \setminus \text{int}(A) = \partial A\), this implies \(\mu(A) = \mu(\bar{A}) = \mu(\text{int}(A))\).

Example 46.4 (Examples of Continuity and Non-Continuity Sets)  

  • Dirac Measure: \((-\infty, a]\) is not a continuity set for \(\delta_a\), since \(\overline{(-\infty,a]} = (-\infty,a]\) and \(\text{int}(-\infty,a] = (-\infty,a)\), so \(\partial(-\infty, a] = \{a\}\) and \(\delta_a(\{a\}) = 1 \neq 0\).
  • Continuous Distribution on \(\mathbb{R}\): If \(\mu\) has a continuous CDF \(F_\mu\) (equivalently, \(\mu(\{a\}) = 0\) for all \(a \in \mathbb{R}\)), then every interval \(I\) with endpoints \(\alpha \le \beta\) is a \(\mu\)-continuity set, because \(\partial I \subseteq \{\alpha, \beta\}\) and \(\mu(\{\alpha,\beta\}) = 0\).
  • Rational Numbers: \(A = \mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]\) is not a continuity set for any measure \(\mu\) with \(\mu([0,1]) > 0\). Indeed \(\bar{A} = [0,1]\) by density of the rationals and \(\text{int}(A) = \emptyset\) since \(A\) contains no interval, so \(\partial A = [0,1] \setminus \emptyset = [0,1]\) and \(\mu(\partial A) = \mu([0,1]) > 0\).

46.5 The Portmanteau Theorem

The Portmanteau Theorem provides several equivalent characterizations of weak convergence of probability measures.

Theorem 46.3 (Portmanteau Theorem) Let \((S,d)\) be a metric space and let \(\mu_n, \mu \in \mathcal{P}(S, \mathcal{B}(S))\). The following statements are equivalent:

  1. Definition: \(\int_S f \, d\mu_n \to \int_S f \, d\mu \quad \forall f \in C_b(S)\) (\(\mu_n \xrightarrow{w} \mu\)).
  2. Lipschitz continuous functions: \(\int_S f \, d\mu_n \to \int_S f \, d\mu \quad \forall f \in \text{Lip}_b(S)\) (bounded Lipschitz continuous functions).
  3. Upper bound on closed sets: \(\limsup_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(F) \le \mu(F) \quad \forall \text{ closed } F \subseteq S\).
  4. Lower bound on open sets: \(\liminf_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(G) \ge \mu(G) \quad \forall \text{ open } G \subseteq S\).
  5. Continuity sets: \(\lim_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(A) = \mu(A) \quad \forall \mu\text{-continuity sets } A \in \mathcal{B}(S)\).

46.6 Complete Step-by-Step Proof of Theorem 46.3

We establish the cycle \((1) \implies (2) \implies (3) \iff (4) \implies (5) \implies (1)\), which yields the equivalence of all five statements.

46.7 Step 1: Proof of \((1 \implies 2)\)

Since every bounded Lipschitz function is bounded and continuous, \(\text{Lip}_b(S) \subseteq C_b(S)\). Thus \((1 \implies 2)\) is immediate.

46.8 Step 2: Proof of \((2 \implies 3)\)

Goal. Assume \(\int_S f \, d\mu_n \to \int_S f \, d\mu\) for every \(f \in \text{Lip}_b(S)\). Show that \(\limsup_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(F) \le \mu(F)\) for every closed \(F \subseteq S\).

The obstruction. We would like to plug \(f = \mathbf{1}_F\) into hypothesis (2), but \(\mathbf{1}_F\) is not continuous (unless \(F\) is also open). The remedy is to approximate \(\mathbf{1}_F\) from above by a sequence of bounded Lipschitz functions \(f_k \downarrow \mathbf{1}_F\). Each \(f_k\) is a legitimate test function, and the approximation is one-sided, which is exactly why we obtain the one-sided conclusion \(\limsup \mu_n(F) \le \mu(F)\) rather than equality.

If \(F = \emptyset\) both sides equal \(0\), so assume \(F \neq \emptyset\) from now on (this is needed for \(d(x,F)\) to be well defined).

46.8.1 Step 2a: Construction of the test functions

Define the distance to \(F\) by \[d(x, F) := \inf_{y \in F} d(x, y),\] let \(\psi(t) := \max(0, 1 - t)\) for \(t \ge 0\), and set \[f_k(x) := \psi\bigl(k \cdot d(x, F)\bigr), \qquad k \ge 1.\]

Explicitly, \[ f_k(x) = \begin{cases} 1 - k\,d(x,F), & \text{if } d(x,F) < 1/k,\\[2pt] 0, & \text{if } d(x,F) \ge 1/k. \end{cases} \] So \(f_k\) equals \(1\) on \(F\), decays linearly to \(0\) as \(x\) moves away from \(F\), and vanishes entirely outside the open \(\tfrac1k\)-neighborhood \(F^{1/k} := \{x : d(x,F) < 1/k\}\). As \(k\) grows, this collar shrinks down onto \(F\).

46.8.2 Step 2b: Verification that \(f_k \in \text{Lip}_b(S)\)

(i) \(x \mapsto d(x,F)\) is \(1\)-Lipschitz. For any \(z \in F\), the triangle inequality gives \(d(x, z) \le d(x, y) + d(y, z)\), hence \(d(x, F) \le d(x,y) + d(y,z)\). Taking the infimum over \(z \in F\) on the right yields \[d(x, F) - d(y, F) \le d(x, y).\] Swapping \(x\) and \(y\) gives the reverse bound, so \(|d(x,F) - d(y,F)| \le d(x,y)\).

(ii) \(\psi\) is \(1\)-Lipschitz. Both \(t \mapsto 0\) and \(t \mapsto 1-t\) are \(1\)-Lipschitz, and the pointwise maximum of two \(1\)-Lipschitz functions is \(1\)-Lipschitz.

(iii) \(f_k\) is \(k\)-Lipschitz. Composing (i) and (ii): \[|f_k(x) - f_k(y)| \le \bigl| k\,d(x, F) - k\,d(y, F) \bigr| = k\,\bigl|d(x,F) - d(y,F)\bigr| \le k\, d(x, y).\]

(iv) \(f_k\) is bounded. Since \(d(x,F) \ge 0\), we have \(\psi(k\,d(x,F)) \in [0,1]\), so \(0 \le f_k \le 1\).

Together, (iii) and (iv) give \(f_k \in \text{Lip}_b(S)\), so hypothesis (2) may be applied to each \(f_k\).

46.8.3 Step 2c: \(f_k\) decreases pointwise to \(\mathbf{1}_F\)

\(f_k \ge \mathbf{1}_F\) everywhere. If \(x \in F\) then \(d(x,F) = 0\), so \(f_k(x) = \psi(0) = 1 = \mathbf{1}_F(x)\). If \(x \notin F\) then \(f_k(x) \ge 0 = \mathbf{1}_F(x)\).

Monotonicity in \(k\). The quantity \(k \, d(x,F)\) is nondecreasing in \(k\) (because \(d(x,F) \ge 0\)), and \(\psi\) is nonincreasing on \([0,\infty)\). Hence \(f_{k+1}(x) \le f_k(x)\) for every \(x\).

Pointwise limit. For \(x \in F\), \(f_k(x) = 1\) for all \(k\). For \(x \notin F\): since \(F\) is closed, its complement is open, so there is \(r > 0\) with \(B(x, r) \subseteq F^c\); every \(y \in F\) therefore satisfies \(d(x,y) \ge r\), giving \(d(x, F) \ge r > 0\). Consequently, as soon as \(k > 1/d(x,F)\) we have \(k\,d(x,F) > 1\) and thus \(f_k(x) = 0\). In particular \(f_k(x) \to 0\).

Note

This is the only place where closedness of \(F\) is used, and it is essential. If \(F\) were not closed, points of \(\bar{F} \setminus F\) would satisfy \(d(x,F) = 0\), forcing \(f_k(x) = 1\) for all \(k\); the limit would then be \(\mathbf{1}_{\bar{F}}\) rather than \(\mathbf{1}_F\).

Combining the three bullets: \[f_k \downarrow \mathbf{1}_F \quad \text{pointwise on } S.\]

46.8.4 Step 2d: Passing to the limit, first in \(n\), then in \(k\)

Fix \(k\). Since \(f_k \ge \mathbf{1}_F\) pointwise and integration is monotone, for every \(n\): \[\mu_n(F) = \int_S \mathbf{1}_F \, d\mu_n \le \int_S f_k \, d\mu_n.\]

Hypothesis (2) applies to \(f_k \in \text{Lip}_b(S)\), so \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_S f_k \, d\mu_n\) exists and equals \(\int_S f_k \, d\mu\). Taking \(\limsup_{n\to\infty}\) of the displayed inequality (the \(\limsup\) of the left side exists in \([0,1]\) even though \(\mu_n(F)\) itself need not converge) gives \[\limsup_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(F) \;\le\; \limsup_{n\to\infty} \int_S f_k \, d\mu_n \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_S f_k \, d\mu_n \;=\; \int_S f_k \, d\mu.\]

Now let \(k \to \infty\). The left-hand side is a single number \(L := \limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu_n(F)\) that does not depend on \(k\), so the previous display says \[L \le \int_S f_k \, d\mu \qquad \text{for every } k \ge 1.\] By Step 2c, \(f_k \to \mathbf{1}_F\) pointwise with \(0 \le f_k \le 1\); the constant function \(1\) is \(\mu\)-integrable because \(\mu\) is a probability measure. The Dominated Convergence Theorem therefore gives \[\int_S f_k \, d\mu \xrightarrow{k\to\infty} \int_S \mathbf{1}_F \, d\mu = \mu(F).\] (Equivalently, since \(f_k\) is decreasing, the Monotone Convergence Theorem for decreasing sequences applies.)

Taking \(k \to \infty\) in \(L \le \int_S f_k \, d\mu\) yields \(L \le \mu(F)\), that is, \[\limsup_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(F) \le \mu(F) \qquad \text{for every closed } F \subseteq S. \qquad \blacksquare\]

TipWhy only an inequality?

The bound \(\limsup_n \mu_n(F) \le \mu(F)\) cannot be upgraded to equality. Take \(S = \mathbb{R}\), \(\mu_n = \delta_{1/n}\), \(\mu = \delta_0\), and \(F = \{0\}\), which is closed. Then \(\mu_n(F) = 0\) for every \(n\), while \(\mu(F) = 1\), so \[0 = \limsup_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(F) < \mu(F) = 1.\] Mass can “arrive on” a closed set in the limit, but it cannot leak out of one. This is precisely the asymmetry that statement (5) repairs by restricting attention to sets \(A\) with \(\mu(\partial A) = 0\) — and here \(\partial F = \{0\}\) has \(\mu\)-measure \(1\).

46.9 Step 3: Proof of \((3 \iff 4)\)

Let \(G \subseteq S\) be open, so \(G^c = S \setminus G\) is closed. Using complementation \(\mu_n(G) = 1 - \mu_n(G^c)\): \[\liminf_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(G) = \liminf_{n\to\infty} (1 - \mu_n(G^c)) = 1 - \limsup_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(G^c)\]

Applying (3) to the closed set \(G^c\): \[\limsup_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(G^c) \le \mu(G^c) = 1 - \mu(G)\]

Substituting this back gives: \[\liminf_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(G) \ge 1 - (1 - \mu(G)) = \mu(G)\]

Conversely, if (4) holds and \(F\) is closed, then \(F^c\) is open. Applying (4) to \(F^c\) and taking complements gives \[ \limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu_n(F) =1-\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu_n(F^c) \le 1-\mu(F^c)=\mu(F). \] Thus \((3 \iff 4)\) holds. \(\blacksquare\)

46.10 Step 4: Proof of \((3 + 4 \implies 5)\)

Let \(A \in \mathcal{B}(S)\) be a \(\mu\)-continuity set, meaning \(\mu(\partial A) = 0\). Recall \(\text{int}(A) \subseteq A \subseteq \bar{A}\), where \(\text{int}(A)\) is open and \(\bar{A}\) is closed.

  1. Using statement (4) on open \(\text{int}(A)\): \[\mu(\text{int}(A)) \le \liminf_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(\text{int}(A)) \le \liminf_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(A)\]

  2. Using statement (3) on closed \(\bar{A}\): \[\limsup_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(A) \le \limsup_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(\bar{A}) \le \mu(\bar{A})\]

Combining the inequalities: \[\mu(\text{int}(A)) \le \liminf_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(A) \le \limsup_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(A) \le \mu(\bar{A})\]

Since \(\mu(\partial A) = 0\), we have \(\mu(\text{int}(A)) = \mu(A) = \mu(\bar{A})\). Therefore, the lower and upper limits collapse to \(\mu(A)\), yielding: \[\lim_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(A) = \mu(A)\] \(\blacksquare\)

46.11 Step 5: Proof of \((5 \implies 1)\)

Goal. Assume \(\mu_n(A) \to \mu(A)\) for every \(\mu\)-continuity set \(A\). Show that \(\int_S f \, d\mu_n \to \int_S f \, d\mu\) for every \(f \in C_b(S)\).

Strategy. Hypothesis (5) is a statement about sets, while the conclusion is a statement about integrals. The bridge is the layer-cake (horizontal slicing) formula, which rewrites \(\int_S f \, d\mu\) purely in terms of the measures of the superlevel sets \(\{f \ge t\}\). Since \(f\) is continuous, these sets are closed with boundary contained in the level set \(\{f = t\}\), and a counting argument shows \(\{f \ge t\}\) is a \(\mu\)-continuity set for all but countably many \(t\). Hypothesis (5) then applies for Lebesgue-a.e. \(t\), and a convergence theorem in the \(t\) variable finishes the proof.

Fix \(f \in C_b(S)\) and set \(M := \|f\|_\infty = \sup_{x \in S}|f(x)| < \infty\), \(a := -M\), \(b := M\), so that \(a \le f(x) \le b\) for all \(x\). If \(M = 0\) then \(f \equiv 0\) and both sides vanish; assume therefore \(M > 0\), so \(a < b\).

46.11.1 Step 5a: The layer-cake representation

For each fixed \(x \in S\) and \(t \in [a,b]\), the indicator \(\mathbf{1}_{\{f(x) \ge t\}}\) equals \(1\) precisely when \(t \le f(x)\), i.e. for \(t \in [a, f(x)]\), an interval of length \(f(x) - a\). Hence \[f(x) - a = \int_a^b \mathbf{1}_{\{f(x) \ge t\}} \, dt \qquad \text{for every } x \in S.\] This is the “horizontal slicing” identity: instead of adding up the values of \(f\), we add up the heights \(t\) at which \(f\) still exceeds \(t\).

46.11.2 Step 5b: Integrating the identity (Tonelli)

Let \(\nu\) denote either \(\mu_n\) or \(\mu\). The function \[(x,t) \longmapsto \mathbf{1}_{\{f(x) \ge t\}}\] is \(\mathcal{B}(S) \otimes \mathcal{B}([a,b])\)-measurable, since it is the indicator of the set \(\{(x,t) : f(x) - t \ge 0\}\), which is the preimage of \([0,\infty)\) under the jointly measurable map \((x,t) \mapsto f(x) - t\). The integrand is nonnegative, \(\nu\) is a probability measure, and Lebesgue measure on \([a,b]\) is finite, so both measures are \(\sigma\)-finite and Tonelli’s Theorem permits exchanging the order of integration: \[ \int_S \bigl(f(x) - a\bigr) \, \nu(dx) = \int_S \!\! \int_a^b \mathbf{1}_{\{f(x) \ge t\}} \, dt \, \nu(dx) = \int_a^b \!\! \int_S \mathbf{1}_{\{f(x) \ge t\}} \, \nu(dx) \, dt = \int_a^b \nu(\{f \ge t\}) \, dt. \] Since \(\nu(S) = 1\), this reads \[\int_S f \, d\mu_n = a + \int_a^b \mu_n(\{f \ge t\}) \, dt, \qquad \int_S f \, d\mu = a + \int_a^b \mu(\{f \ge t\}) \, dt.\]

It therefore suffices to prove \[\int_a^b \mu_n(\{f \ge t\}) \, dt \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} \int_a^b \mu(\{f \ge t\}) \, dt.\]

46.11.3 Step 5c: \(\{f \ge t\}\) is a \(\mu\)-continuity set for all but countably many \(t\)

(i) The boundary sits inside the level set. Because \(f\) is continuous, \(\{f \ge t\} = f^{-1}([t,\infty))\) is closed and \(\{f > t\} = f^{-1}((t,\infty))\) is open. An open subset of \(\{f \ge t\}\) is contained in its interior, so \(\{f > t\} \subseteq \text{int}\{f \ge t\}\), and therefore \[\partial \{f \ge t\} = \overline{\{f \ge t\}} \setminus \text{int}\{f \ge t\} = \{f \ge t\} \setminus \text{int}\{f \ge t\} \subseteq \{f \ge t\} \setminus \{f > t\} = \{f = t\}.\]

(ii) Only countably many levels carry mass. Put \[E := \{ t \in [a, b] : \mu(\{f = t\}) > 0 \}, \qquad E_m := \{t \in [a,b] : \mu(\{f = t\}) \ge 1/m\},\] so that \(E = \bigcup_{m \ge 1} E_m\). Each \(E_m\) is finite: if \(t_1, \dots, t_j \in E_m\) are distinct, the level sets \(\{f = t_1\}, \dots, \{f = t_j\}\) are pairwise disjoint, so \[1 = \mu(S) \ \ge \ \sum_{i=1}^j \mu(\{f = t_i\}) \ \ge \ \frac{j}{m},\] forcing \(j \le m\). A countable union of finite sets is countable, so \(E\) is at most countable and in particular has Lebesgue measure zero.

(iii) Conclusion. For every \(t \in [a,b] \setminus E\) — that is, for Lebesgue-a.e. \(t\) — combining (i) and (ii) gives \[\mu\bigl(\partial\{f \ge t\}\bigr) \le \mu(\{f = t\}) = 0,\] so \(\{f \ge t\}\) is a \(\mu\)-continuity set. Hypothesis (5) therefore yields \[\mu_n(\{f \ge t\}) \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} \mu(\{f \ge t\}) \qquad \text{for Lebesgue-a.e. } t \in [a,b].\]

Note

The exceptional set \(E\) cannot be dispensed with. Take \(S = \mathbb{R}\), \(\mu_n = \delta_{1/n}\), \(\mu = \delta_0\) and \(f(x) = e^{-x^2} \in C_b(\mathbb{R})\). At the single level \(t = 1\) we have \(\{f \ge 1\} = \{0\}\), so \(\mu_n(\{f \ge 1\}) = 0\) for all \(n\) while \(\mu(\{f \ge 1\}) = 1\): convergence fails. But \(E = \{1\}\) is a Lebesgue-null subset of \([a,b] = [-1,1]\), so it does not affect the integral over \(t\).

46.11.4 Step 5d: Passing to the limit (Bounded Convergence)

For each \(n\), the map \(t \mapsto \mu_n(\{f \ge t\})\) is nonincreasing (as \(t\) increases the sets \(\{f \ge t\}\) shrink), hence Borel measurable, and it is bounded by \(1\). Lebesgue measure on \([a,b]\) is finite, so the constant \(1\) is an integrable dominating function and the Bounded Convergence Theorem applies to the a.e.-convergence established in Step 5c: \[\int_a^b \mu_n(\{f \ge t\}) \, dt \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} \int_a^b \mu(\{f \ge t\}) \, dt.\]

Adding the constant \(a\) and using the two identities from Step 5b: \[\int_S f \, d\mu_n = a + \int_a^b \mu_n(\{f \ge t\}) \, dt \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} a + \int_a^b \mu(\{f \ge t\}) \, dt = \int_S f \, d\mu.\]

Since \(f \in C_b(S)\) was arbitrary, statement (1) holds. This closes the cycle \((1) \implies (2) \implies (3) \iff (4) \implies (5) \implies (1)\), so all five statements are equivalent. \(\blacksquare\)

46.12 Takeaways

  1. Weak convergence is convergence against every bounded continuous test function. It captures limits such as \(\delta_{a_n}\implies\delta_a\) when \(a_n\to a\), even though total variation convergence can fail completely.
  2. Total variation convergence implies weak convergence, but not conversely: discrete approximations of \(\mathrm{Unif}([0,1])\) converge weakly through Riemann sums while remaining at total variation distance \(1\).
  3. For random elements on a common probability space, convergence in probability, almost sure convergence, and \(L^p\) convergence (\(p\ge1\)) each imply convergence in distribution.
  4. The Portmanteau Theorem gives interchangeable tests for weak convergence: bounded Lipschitz functions, upper bounds on closed sets, lower bounds on open sets, and convergence on every \(\mu\)-continuity set.
  5. Set probabilities should generally be tested only on \(\mu\)-continuity sets. Boundaries with positive limiting mass are precisely where weak convergence need not determine \(\mu_n(A)\to\mu(A)\).
