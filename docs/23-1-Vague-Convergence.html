48  Lecture 23.1: Vague Convergence

48.1 Motivation: Loss of Mass in the Limit

In previous lectures, we established that on \(\mathbb{R}^d\), weak convergence \(\mu_n \implies \mu\) is equivalent to testing against functions in \(C_c(\mathbb{R}^d)\) provided both \(\mu_n\) and \(\mu\) are probability measures. However, when sequence elements escape to infinity, testing against compactly supported functions \(C_c(\mathbb{R}^d)\) can lead to a limit measure \(\mu\) that fails to preserve total probability mass \(\mu(\mathbb{R}^d) = 1\).

Example 48.1 Fix a unit vector \(e_1 \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and let \(\mu_n=\frac{1}{2}\delta_0+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{n e_1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^d,\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\) for \(n \in \mathbb{N}\). Does \(\mu_n\) converge weakly to a probability measure \(\mu\)? For any test function \(f \in C_c(\mathbb{R}^d)\), there exists a radius \(R < \infty\) such that \(\text{supp}(f) \subseteq B_R\). Then for all \(n > R\): \[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f \, d\mu_n = \frac{1}{2}f(0) + \frac{1}{2}f(n e_1) = \frac{1}{2}f(0). \] Thus \(\mu_n \xrightarrow{v} \frac{1}{2}\delta_0\), which is a sub-probability measure with \(\mu(\mathbb{R}^d) = 1/2 < 1\). Note that \(\mu_n \not\implies \frac{1}{2}\delta_0\), because \[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} 1 \, d\mu_n = 1 \nrightarrow \frac{1}{2} = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} 1 \, d\mu. \] In fact, \(\mu_n\) does not possess any weakly convergent subsequence. Indeed, every subsequence is non-tight: for any compact \(K \subset \mathbb{R}^d\), boundedness of \(K\) implies \(n_k e_1 \notin K\) for all sufficiently large \(k\), and hence \(\mu_{n_k}(K) \le 1/2\). Since every weakly convergent sequence of probability measures on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) is tight, no subsequence can converge weakly.

48.2 Vague Convergence

To accommodate limit operations where mass escapes, we introduce the concept of vague convergence for general Radon/Borel measures.

Definition 48.1 (Vague Convergence) Let \(\mu_n, \mu\) be positive Radon measures on \(\mathbb{R}^d\), meaning locally finite regular Borel measures. (In particular, every finite Borel measure on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) is Radon.) We say \(\mu_n\) converges vaguely to \(\mu\), denoted \(\mu_n \xrightarrow{v} \mu\), if: \[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f \, d\mu_n \longrightarrow \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f \, d\mu \quad \forall f \in C_c(\mathbb{R}^d) \]

It is possible to lose mass, but not gain mass under vague convergence. The following proposition formalizes this property.

Theorem 48.1 (One-Sided Conservation of Mass) Let \(\{\mu_n\}\) be a sequence of probability measures on \(\mathbb{R}^d\). If \(\mu_n\xrightarrow{v}\mu\) for some Radon measure \(\mu\), then \[ \mu(\mathbb{R}^d) \leq \liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu_n(\mathbb{R}^d) =1. \] Thus, under vague convergence, total mass can be lost in the limit, but it cannot be gained.

Proof (Proof of Theorem 48.1). Suppose for contradiction that \(\mu(\mathbb{R}^d) > 1\). Since \(\mu(B_R) \uparrow \mu(\mathbb{R}^d)\) as \(R \to \infty\), there exists some finite radius \(R < \infty\) such that \(\mu(B_R) > 1\). Choose a continuous radial cutoff \(f \in C_c(\mathbb{R}^d)\) that equals \(1\) on \(B_R\), vanishes outside \(B_{R+1}\), and satisfies \(0 \le f \le 1\). Thus \(\mathbf{1}_{B_R} \le f \le 1\). By vague convergence: \[\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f \, d\mu_n = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f \, d\mu \ge \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{1}_{B_R} \, d\mu = \mu(B_R) > 1\]

However, for all \(n\): \[\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f \, d\mu_n \le \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} 1 \, d\mu_n = \mu_n(\mathbb{R}^d) = 1\] This yields the contradiction \(\lim_{n\to\infty} \int f d\mu_n > 1\), showing \(\mu(\mathbb{R}^d) \le 1\).

48.3 Tightness of Probability Measures

To bridge the gap between vague convergence and weak convergence, we need a condition preventing probability mass from fleeing to infinity.

Definition 48.2 (Tightness) A family \(\mathcal{A} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(S, \mathcal{B}(S))\) of probability measures on a topological space \(S\) is called tight if for every \(\epsilon > 0\), there exists a compact set \(K_\epsilon \subseteq S\) such that: \[\mu(K_\epsilon) \ge 1 - \epsilon \quad \forall \mu \in \mathcal{A}\]

Thus, a sequence \(\{\mu_n\}\) is tight exactly when, for every \(\epsilon > 0\), there exists a compact set \(K_\epsilon\) such that \(\inf_n \mu_n(K_\epsilon) \ge 1 - \epsilon\).

  • The key word is uniformity: the same compact set \(K_\epsilon\) must work for all measures in the family \(\mathcal{A}\) for a given \(\epsilon\). So the probability mass of every measure in the family is concentrated, up to an arbitrarily small amount, inside a common compact set.

Example 48.2 (Examples of Tightness)  

  • Weakly Convergent Sequences: If \(\mu_n \implies \mu\) on \(\mathbb{R}^d\), we proved in Lemma 49.1 that \(\lim_{R\to\infty} \inf_n \mu_n(\bar{B}_R) = 1\). Taking \(K_\epsilon = \bar{B}_R\) for large enough \(R\) demonstrates that every weakly convergent sequence is tight. Thus, weak convergence implies tightness.
  • Fleeing Sequence: The sequence \(\mu_n =\frac{1}{2}\delta_0 + \frac{1}{2}\delta_n\) is not tight. For any compact set \(K \subset \mathbb{R}\), there exists \(N\) such that \(n > N\) implies \(n \notin K\). Then \(\mu_n(K) \le 1/2 < 1 - \epsilon\) for \(\epsilon < 1/2\), showing that no compact set can capture at least \(1 - \epsilon\) of the mass for all \(n\).

48.4 Characterization of Weak Convergence via Tightness

Theorem 48.2 (Equivalence of Weak Convergence and Tight Vague Limits) Let \(\mu_n \in \mathcal{P}\left(\mathbb{R}^d,\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\right)\) and let \(\mu\) be a positive Radon measure on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) such that \(\mu_n \xrightarrow{v} \mu\). Then \(\mu(\mathbb{R}^d) = 1\) if and only if the sequence \(\{\mu_n\}\) is tight. In that case, \(\mu\) is a probability measure and \(\mu_n \implies \mu\).

Proof (Proof of Theorem 48.2).

  • \((\implies)\): Suppose \(\mu(\mathbb{R}^d) = 1\), so \(\mu\) is a probability measure. Since \(\mu_n \xrightarrow{v} \mu\) means \(\int f \, d\mu_n \to \int f \, d\mu\) for all \(f \in C_c(\mathbb{R}^d)\), and both \(\mu_n\) and \(\mu\) are probability measures, the \(C_c\) characterization of weak convergence (Theorem 49.1) gives \(\mu_n \implies \mu\). By Lemma 49.1 (proved in the previous lecture), every weakly convergent sequence on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) is tight.

  • \((\impliedby)\): Assume \(\{\mu_n\}\) is tight. Fix \(\epsilon > 0\). By tightness, choose a compact set \(K_\epsilon \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) such that \(\mu_n(K_\epsilon) \ge 1 - \epsilon\) for all \(n\). Define \(f(x)=\max\{1-\operatorname{dist}(x,K_\epsilon),0\}\). Then \(f \in C_c(\mathbb{R}^d)\) because the closed unit neighborhood of the compact set \(K_\epsilon\) is compact, and \(\mathbf{1}_{K_\epsilon} \le f \le 1\).

    Since \(f \ge \mathbf{1}_{K_\epsilon}\), we have \(\int f \, d\mu_n \ge \mu_n(K_\epsilon) \ge 1 - \epsilon\) for all \(n\). Using \(f \in C_c\) and vague convergence: \[\mu(\mathbb{R}^d) \ge \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f \, d\mu = \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f \, d\mu_n \ge \liminf_{n\to\infty} \mu_n(K_\epsilon) \ge 1 - \epsilon.\]

    Since \(\epsilon > 0\) was arbitrary, \(\mu(\mathbb{R}^d) \ge 1\). Combined with Theorem 48.1 (\(\mu(\mathbb{R}^d) \le 1\)), we conclude \(\mu(\mathbb{R}^d) = 1\), so \(\mu\) is a probability measure. Since \(\mu_n \xrightarrow{v} \mu\) already gives \(\int f \, d\mu_n \to \int f \, d\mu\) for all \(f \in C_c(\mathbb{R}^d)\), and both \(\mu_n\) and \(\mu\) are probability measures, the \(C_c\) characterization (Theorem 49.1) yields \(\mu_n \implies \mu\).

Example 48.3 Let \(\mu_n=\delta_n\), where \(\delta_n\) is the point mass at \(n\in\mathbb R\): \(\delta_n(A)=\mathbf 1\{n\in A\}\). Consider the sequence \(\delta_1,\delta_2,\delta_3,\ldots\). For any compact set \(K\subset\mathbb R\), there exists \(M<\infty\) such that \(K\subset[-M,M]\). For every \(n>M\), \(\delta_n(K)=0\). Thus, for example, if \(\epsilon=1/2\), there is no compact \(K\) such that \[ \delta_n(K)\geq1-\epsilon=\frac12 \qquad\text{for every }n. \] Hence \(\{\delta_n\}_{n\ge 1}\) is not tight.

Take any \(f\in C_c(\mathbb R)\). Because \(f\) has compact support, there exists \(M<\infty\) such that \(f(x)=0\) whenever \(|x|>M\). Therefore, for all \(n>M\), \[ \int f\,d\delta_n=f(n)=0. \] Consequently, \(\int f\,d\delta_n\longrightarrow0\). Thus \[ \delta_n\xrightarrow{v}0, \] where \(0\) denotes the zero measure. The limiting measure has mass \(0(\mathbb R)=0\). So the mass has completely escaped to infinity.

The theorem says that if \(\mu_n\xrightarrow{v}\mu\) and the sequence \(\{\mu_n\}\) is tight, then necessarily \(\mu(\mathbb R)=1\) and therefore \(\mu_n\implies\mu\). Here, the sequence is not tight, and indeed the vague limit has mass \(0\), not \(1\). In particular, there cannot be weak convergence: \(\delta_n\not\implies0\), because \(0\) is not a probability measure.

48.5 Vague Convergence via Interval Differences on \(\mathbb{R}\)

Theorem 48.3 (Characterization by CDF Differences) Let \(\mu_n \in \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))\) and let \(\mu\) be a finite Borel measure on \(\mathbb{R}\). Define the CDFs \(F_n(t) = \mu_n((-\infty, t])\) and the cumulative mass function \(F(t) = \mu((-\infty, t])\); unlike a probability CDF, \(F\) satisfies \(F(+\infty)=\mu(\mathbb{R})\), which need not equal \(1\). Then \(\mu_n \xrightarrow{v} \mu\) if and only if \[ F_n(b) - F_n(a) \longrightarrow F(b) - F(a) \quad \forall a, b \in \text{Cont}(F) \text{ with } a < b. \]

Proof (Proof of Theorem 48.3). First observe that, for \(a<b\), \[ F_n(b)-F_n(a)=\mu_n((a,b]) \quad\text{and}\quad F(b)-F(a)=\mu((a,b]). \] Moreover, \(t\in\operatorname{Cont}(F)\) if and only if \(\mu(\{t\})=0\). Indeed, \[ F(t)-F(t^-)=\mu(\{t\}). \]

  • \((\implies)\): Suppose that \(\mu_n\xrightarrow{v}\mu\), and fix \(a,b\in\operatorname{Cont}(F)\) with \(a<b\). For \(0<\delta<(b-a)/2\), choose functions \(\ell_\delta,u_\delta\in C_c(\mathbb R)\) as follows:

    • \(\ell_\delta\) is \(0\) outside \((a,b)\), is \(1\) on \([a+\delta,b-\delta]\), is linear on the two intervening intervals, and satisfies \(0\leq\ell_\delta\leq1\);
    • \(u_\delta\) is \(1\) on \([a,b]\), is \(0\) outside \((a-\delta,b+\delta)\), is linear on the two intervening intervals, and satisfies \(0\leq u_\delta\leq1\).

    These functions satisfy \[ \ell_\delta\leq\mathbf 1_{(a,b]}\leq u_\delta. \] Therefore, \[ \int \ell_\delta\,d\mu_n \leq \mu_n((a,b]) \leq \int u_\delta\,d\mu_n. \] Vague convergence applies to both cutoff functions, so \[ \int \ell_\delta\,d\mu \leq \liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu_n((a,b]) \leq \limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu_n((a,b]) \leq \int u_\delta\,d\mu. \] As \(\delta\downarrow0\), the lower cutoffs converge pointwise to \(\mathbf 1_{(a,b)}\), while the upper cutoffs converge pointwise to \(\mathbf 1_{[a,b]}\). All these functions are bounded by the indicator of a fixed compact interval once \(\delta\) is restricted, and \(\mu\) is finite on compact sets. Hence dominated convergence gives \[ \int \ell_\delta\,d\mu\longrightarrow\mu((a,b)), \qquad \int u_\delta\,d\mu\longrightarrow\mu([a,b]). \] Since \(a,b\in\operatorname{Cont}(F)\), we have \(\mu(\{a\})=\mu(\{b\})=0\), and consequently \[ \mu((a,b))=\mu((a,b])=\mu([a,b]). \] Letting \(\delta\downarrow0\) in the preceding bounds yields \[ \mu_n((a,b])\longrightarrow\mu((a,b]), \] which is exactly \[ F_n(b)-F_n(a)\longrightarrow F(b)-F(a). \]

  • \((\impliedby)\): Conversely, suppose that \[ \mu_n((a,b])\longrightarrow\mu((a,b]) \qquad\text{for every }a,b\in\operatorname{Cont}(F),\ a<b. \] Because \(F\) is nondecreasing, it has at most countably many discontinuities. Thus \(\operatorname{Cont}(F)\) is dense in \(\mathbb R\).

    Let \(f\in C_c(\mathbb R)\). Choose \(A<B\) such that \(\operatorname{supp}(f)\subseteq[A,B]\). Given \(\eta>0\), uniform continuity of \(f\) gives \(\rho>0\) such that \[ |x-y|<\rho\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |f(x)-f(y)|<\eta. \] Using the density of \(\operatorname{Cont}(F)\), choose a finite partition \[ x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_k, \] all of whose points belong to \(\operatorname{Cont}(F)\), such that \(x_0<A\), \(x_k>B\), and \(x_j-x_{j-1}<\rho\) for every \(j\). Define \[ f_\pi(x)=\sum_{j=1}^k f(x_j)\mathbf 1_{(x_{j-1},x_j]}(x). \] Since \(f\) vanishes outside \([A,B]\) and every interval of the partition has length less than \(\rho\), we have \[ \lVert f-f_\pi\rVert_\infty\leq\eta. \] The assumed interval convergence and finiteness of the sum imply \[ \begin{aligned} \int f_\pi\,d\mu_n &=\sum_{j=1}^k f(x_j)\mu_n((x_{j-1},x_j])\\ &\longrightarrow \sum_{j=1}^k f(x_j)\mu((x_{j-1},x_j]) =\int f_\pi\,d\mu. \end{aligned} \] Because each \(\mu_n\) is a probability measure and \(\mu\) is finite, \[ \begin{aligned} \left|\int f\,d\mu_n-\int f\,d\mu\right| &\leq \left|\int(f-f_\pi)\,d\mu_n\right| +\left|\int f_\pi\,d\mu_n-\int f_\pi\,d\mu\right|\\ &\quad+\left|\int(f_\pi-f)\,d\mu\right|\\ &\leq \eta+\left|\int f_\pi\,d\mu_n-\int f_\pi\,d\mu\right| +\eta\mu(\mathbb R). \end{aligned} \] Taking \(\limsup_{n\to\infty}\) gives \[ \limsup_{n\to\infty} \left|\int f\,d\mu_n-\int f\,d\mu\right| \leq \eta\bigl(1+\mu(\mathbb R)\bigr). \] Since \(\eta>0\) is arbitrary, the left-hand side is \(0\). Therefore \[ \int f\,d\mu_n\longrightarrow\int f\,d\mu \qquad\forall f\in C_c(\mathbb R), \] which proves \(\mu_n\xrightarrow{v}\mu\).

Example 48.4 (Symmetric Split Mass) Consider the sequence of measures \(\mu_n = \frac{1}{2}(\delta_n + \delta_{-n})\) on \(\mathbb{R}\). For any test function \(f \in C_c(\mathbb{R})\) supported on \([-R, R]\): \[\int_{\mathbb{R}} f \, d\mu_n = \frac{1}{2}(f(n) + f(-n)) = 0 \quad \forall n > R\]

Thus \(\mu_n \xrightarrow{v} \mathbf{0}\), where \(\mathbf{0}\) is the zero measure. Evaluating the CDFs of \(\mu_n\): \[F_n(t) = \begin{cases} 0, & t < -n \\ 1/2, & -n \le t < n \\ 1, & t \ge n \end{cases}\]

For every fixed \(t \in \mathbb{R}\), eventually \(-n < t < n\), so \(F_n(t) \to 1/2\) pointwise. However, the vague limit is \(\mu = \mathbf{0}\), whose CDF is \(F(t) = \mu((-\infty,t]) = 0\) for all \(t\). The pointwise limit of \(F_n\) (which is \(1/2\)) is therefore not the CDF of the vague limit — this discrepancy is precisely the mass-loss phenomenon.

To verify the CDF-difference criterion in Theorem 48.3 consistently with the vague convergence already established above, note that for any \(a < b\) and all \(n > \max(|a|, |b|)\), \(F_n(a) = F_n(b) = 1/2\), so: \[F_n(b) - F_n(a) = 0 \longrightarrow 0 = F(b) - F(a),\] confirming vague convergence to the zero measure. Both halves of the probability mass escaped to opposite infinities.

48.6 Takeaways

  1. Vague convergence is \(C_c\)-convergence for general measures.
    \(\mu_n \xrightarrow{v} \mu\) means \(\int f \, d\mu_n \to \int f \, d\mu\) for all \(f \in C_c(\mathbb{R}^d)\). Unlike weak convergence, the limit \(\mu\) need not be a probability measure — mass can escape to infinity and be lost. Vague convergence is the natural notion for sequences of measures that may not remain tight.

  2. Mass can only be lost, never gained, under vague convergence.
    By Theorem 48.1, if \(\mu_n\) are probability measures and \(\mu_n \xrightarrow{v} \mu\), then \(\mu(\mathbb{R}^d) \le 1\). The proof constructs a test function \(f \in C_c\) with \(\mathbf{1}_{B_R} \le f \le 1\) and uses vague convergence to derive a contradiction if \(\mu(\mathbb{R}^d) > 1\). By contrast, mass can strictly decrease: the fleeing example \(\mu_n = \frac{1}{2}\delta_0 + \frac{1}{2}\delta_{n e_1}\) gives \(\mu_n \xrightarrow{v} \frac{1}{2}\delta_0\) with only half the mass preserved.

  3. Tightness bridges vague and weak convergence.
    By Theorem 48.2, if \(\mu_n \xrightarrow{v} \mu\) (with \(\mu_n\) probability measures), then \(\mu_n \implies \mu\) (i.e., \(\mu(\mathbb{R}^d) = 1\) and vague convergence upgrades to weak convergence) if and only if \(\{\mu_n\}\) is tight. The key directions are:

    • Tight \(\Rightarrow\) weak: tightness gives a cutoff function \(f \ge \mathbf{1}_{K_\epsilon}\) that forces \(\mu(\mathbb{R}^d) \ge 1 - \epsilon\); since \(\epsilon\) is arbitrary, \(\mu(\mathbb{R}^d) = 1\), and the \(C_c\) characterization (Theorem 22.2) then gives \(\mu_n \implies \mu\).
    • Weak \(\Rightarrow\) tight: weak convergence implies \(\lim_{R\to\infty} \inf_n \mu_n(\bar{B}_R) = 1\) (Lemma 22.2).

  4. Vague convergence on \(\mathbb{R}\) is characterized by differences of CDFs.
    By Theorem 48.3, \(\mu_n \xrightarrow{v} \mu\) iff \(F_n(b) - F_n(a) \to F(b) - F(a)\) for all continuity points \(a < b\) of \(F\), where \(F\) is the CDF of \(\mu\). Note that CDF differences (not pointwise CDF values) are the correct quantity: the pointwise limit of \(F_n\) need not equal the CDF of the vague limit.

  5. The pointwise limit of CDFs need not be the CDF of the vague limit.
    The example \(\mu_n = \frac{1}{2}(\delta_n + \delta_{-n}) \xrightarrow{v} \mathbf{0}\) illustrates this: \(F_n(t) \to 1/2\) pointwise for every finite \(t\), yet the CDF of \(\mathbf{0}\) is \(F \equiv 0\). The CDF differences \(F_n(b) - F_n(a) = 0 \to 0 = F(b) - F(a)\) correctly capture vague convergence, even though the pointwise limits of \(F_n\) are not meaningful for the limit measure.
