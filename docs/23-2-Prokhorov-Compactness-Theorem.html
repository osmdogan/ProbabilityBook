49  Lecture 23.2: Prokhorov’s Compactness Theorem

49.1 Motivation & Counterexample

Some sequences of probability measures have no weakly convergent subsequences.

WarningCounterexample: Mass Escape to Infinity

Consider the sequence of Dirac measures on \(\mathbb{R}\): \[ \mu_n=\delta_n. \] For any compact set \(K\subset\mathbb{R}\), there exists \(M>0\) such that \(K\subset[-M,M]\). Hence, for all sufficiently large \(n\), \[ \mu_n(K)=\delta_n(K)=0. \] Thus, for every compact set \(K\), \[ \liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu_n(K)=0. \] Consequently, \((\mu_n)_{n=1}^\infty\) is not tight, and neither is any subsequence. Since weak convergence of probability measures implies tightness, \((\mu_n)_{n=1}^\infty\) has no weakly convergent subsequence.

Intuitively, the entire probability mass moves farther and farther away from every fixed compact set; the mass escapes to infinity.

49.2 Main Statements

Theorem 49.1 (Prokhorov’s Compactness Theorem) Let \(S\) be a separable metric space. If \(\{\mu_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} \subset \text{Prob}(S, \mathcal{B}(S))\), then there exists a vaguely convergent subsequence.

Corollary 49.1 (Weak Convergence via Tightness) If \(\{\mu_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\) is also tight, then there exists a weakly convergent subsequence \(\{\mu_{n_k}\}_{k=1}^\infty\) whose limit \(\mu\) is a proper probability measure (i.e., \(\mu(S) = 1\)).

In these notes, we present the classic proof for the foundational baseline case where \(S = \mathbb{R}\), using Helly’s Selection Theorem.

49.3 Proof of Helly’s Selection Theorem (\(S = \mathbb{R}\))

Let \(F_n(x) = \mu_n((-\infty, x])\) be the sequence of Cumulative Distribution Functions (CDFs) associated with the probability measures \(\mu_n\).

49.4 Step 1: Cantor’s Diagonal Method on Rational Numbers

Enumerate the countable set of rational numbers \(\mathbb{Q}\): \[ \mathbb{Q} = \{q_1, q_2, q_3, \dots, q_j, \dots\} \]

  1. For \(q_1\): The numerical sequence \(\{F_n(q_1)\}_{n=1}^\infty\) is bounded in \([0, 1]\). By the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem, there exists a convergent subsequence: \[ \{F_{m_1(k)}(q_1)\}_{k=1}^\infty \]
  2. For \(q_2\): Extract a sub-subsequence \(\{m_2(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty \subset \{m_1(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty\) such that \(\{F_{m_2(k)}(q_2)\}_{k=1}^\infty\) converges.
  3. Inductively: For each \(j \ge 1\), \(\{m_j(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty\) is a subsequence of \(\{m_{j-1}(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty\) such that \(F_{m_j(k)}(q_i)\) converges as \(k \to \infty\) for all \(1 \le i \le j\).

Define the diagonal sequence \(n_k := m_k(k)\). By construction, \(\{n_k\}_{k=1}^\infty\) is eventually a subsequence of \(\{m_j(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty\) for any fixed \(j\). Therefore, the pointwise limit exists on all of \(\mathbb{Q}\): \[ F_{n_k}(q) \xrightarrow{k\to\infty} G(q) \in [0, 1], \quad \forall q \in \mathbb{Q} \] Since each \(F_{n_k}\) is non-decreasing on \(\mathbb{R}\), the pointwise limit \(G\) is non-decreasing on \(\mathbb{Q}\).

49.5 Step 2: Extension from \(\mathbb{Q}\) to \(\mathbb{R}\)

We wish for the limiting object to define a valid CDF on \(\mathbb{R}\). To ensure it is right-continuous, define the function \(F: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) by: \[ F(x) := \inf \{G(q) : q \in \mathbb{Q}, q > x\} \]

49.6 Verification of CDF Properties for \(F(x)\)

49.6.1 Property 1: Non-decreasing

Let \(x < y\). If \(q \in \mathbb{Q}\) satisfies \(q > y\), then automatically \(q > x\). Thus: \[ \{G(q) : q \in \mathbb{Q}, q > y\} \subseteq \{G(q) : q \in \mathbb{Q}, q > x\} \] Taking the infimum over a larger set yields a smaller or equal value: \[ F(y) = \inf \{G(q) : q \in \mathbb{Q}, q > y\} \ge \inf \{G(q) : q \in \mathbb{Q}, q > x\} = F(x) \]

49.6.2 Property 2: Right-Continuous

Let \(x_n \downarrow x\) as \(n \to \infty\). Since \(F\) is non-decreasing, the sequence \(F(x_n)\) is non-increasing, so \(\lim_{n\to\infty} F(x_n) = \inf_n F(x_n)\).

Evaluating the nested infima: \[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{n \to \infty} F(x_n) &= \inf_{n} F(x_n) \\ &= \inf_{n} \left( \inf \{G(q) : q \in \mathbb{Q}, q > x_n\} \right) \\ &= \inf \{G(q) : q \in \mathbb{Q}, \exists n \text{ s.t. } q > x_n\} \\ &= \inf \{G(q) : q \in \mathbb{Q}, q > x\} \\ &= F(x) \end{aligned} \] Thus, \(F(x)\) is right-continuous, making it a valid generalized CDF of a sub-probability measure \(\mu\) on \(\mathbb{R}\).

49.7 Step 3: Convergence at Continuity Points of \(F\)

To complete the proof of vague/weak convergence (Theorem 48.3 and Theorem 51.1), it suffices to show that: \[ F_{n_k}(b) - F_{n_k}(a) \longrightarrow F(b) - F(a), \quad \forall a, b \in \text{Cont}(F) \] In fact, we prove the stronger claim that: \[ F_{n_k}(x) \longrightarrow F(x), \quad \forall x \in \text{Cont}(F) \] Fix \(x \in \text{Cont}(F)\). Choose rational sequences \(q_j, r_j \in \mathbb{Q}\) such that: \[ q_j \uparrow x \quad \text{and} \quad r_j \downarrow x \quad \implies \quad q_j < x < r_j \] Since each \(F_{n_k}\) is non-decreasing: \[ F_{n_k}(q_j) \le F_{n_k}(x) \le F_{n_k}(r_j) \] Taking the limit superior and limit inferior as \(k \to \infty\): \[ G(q_j) = \lim_{k \to \infty} F_{n_k}(q_j) \le \liminf_{k \to \infty} F_{n_k}(x) \le \limsup_{k \to \infty} F_{n_k}(x) \le \lim_{k \to \infty} F_{n_k}(r_j) = G(r_j) \]

Since this holds for all \(j\):

  1. Upper bound — \(G(r_j) \to F(x)\): Each \(G(r_j) \ge F(x)\) because \(r_j > x\), so \(G(r_j)\) belongs to the infimum set defining \(F(x)\). For any rational \(r^{*} > x\), since \(r_j \downarrow x < r^{*}\), eventually \(r_j < r^{*}\); non-decreasingness of \(G\) on \(\mathbb{Q}\) then gives \(G(r_j) \le G(r^{*})\). Hence \(\limsup_j G(r_j) \le G(r^{*})\) for every rational \(r^{*} > x\), so \(\limsup_j G(r_j) \le \inf_{r^{*} > x} G(r^{*}) = F(x)\). Combined with \(G(r_j) \ge F(x)\): \(G(r_j) \downarrow F(x)\).

  2. Lower bound — \(G(q_j) \to F(x)\): Since \(G\) is non-decreasing on \(\mathbb{Q}\) and \(q_j\) is increasing, \(G(q_j)\) is non-decreasing and bounded, so \(L := \lim_j G(q_j)\) exists.

    • \(L \le F(x)\): For any rational \(r > x\), we have \(r > q_j\) for all \(j\), so \(G(r) \ge G(q_j)\) for all \(j\), hence \(G(r) \ge L\). Taking the infimum over all rational \(r > x\) gives \(F(x) \ge L\).
    • \(L \ge F(x)\): For any \(z < x\), choose a rational \(r \in (z, x)\). Eventually \(q_j > r\), so \(G(q_j) \ge G(r)\); and since \(r > z\), non-decreasingness places \(G(r)\) in the infimum set for \(F(z)\), giving \(G(r) \ge F(z)\). Thus \(L \ge F(z)\) for every \(z < x\). Taking \(z \uparrow x\) and using continuity of \(F\) at \(x\) (i.e.~\(F(x^-) = F(x)\)): \(L \ge F(x)\).
    • Hence \(G(q_j) \uparrow F(x)\).

Hence, taking \(j \to \infty\): \[ F(x) \le \liminf_{k \to \infty} F_{n_k}(x) \le \limsup_{k \to \infty} F_{n_k}(x) \le F(x). \] By squeeze theorem: \[ \lim_{k \to \infty} F_{n_k}(x) = F(x), \quad \forall x \in \text{Cont}(F). \]

Example 49.1 (A tight sequence with an oscillating mean) Let \[ \mu_n = \begin{cases} N(0,1), & n \text{ even},\\ N(1,1), & n \text{ odd}. \end{cases} \] Equivalently, if \(X_n\sim\mu_n\), \[ X_n\sim \begin{cases} N(0,1), & n \text{ even},\\ N(1,1), & n \text{ odd}. \end{cases} \] The full sequence does not converge weakly, because the even and odd subsequences have different limits: \(\mu_{2k}=N(0,1)\) and \(\mu_{2k+1}=N(1,1)\).

But the sequence is tight. Indeed, given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(M\) sufficiently large. Then \(\sup_n \mu_n([-M,M]^c) \leq P(|Z|>M-1)\), where \(Z\sim N(0,1)\). Note that the right-hand side tends to zero as \(M\to\infty\). Hence \(\{\mu_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) is tight.

Therefore, by Corollary 49.1, there must be a weakly convergent subsequence. And we can see exactly what the subsequences are: \(\mu_{2k}\xrightarrow{w}N(0,1)\) and \(\mu_{2k+1}\xrightarrow{w}N(1,1)\). Both limits are proper probability measures.

This example demonstrates an important distinction: tightness does not imply convergence of the entire sequence. Rather, tightness guarantees the existence of a weakly convergent subsequence. The sequence can oscillate between different probability distributions, but because its mass cannot escape to infinity, some subsequence must settle down to a genuine probability distribution.

For contrast, consider \(\mu_n=\delta_n\). Here the mass moves farther and farther to the right: \(\delta_1,\delta_2,\delta_3,\ldots\) and the sequence is not tight. It has no weakly convergent subsequence in \(\operatorname{Prob}(\mathbb R)\). Its vague limit is instead \(\delta_n\xrightarrow{v}0\), where \(0\) is the zero measure, not a probability measure.

49.8 Takeaways

NoteSummary

1. Helly’s Selection Theorem (the \(S = \mathbb{R}\) engine). Every sequence of probability measures on \(\mathbb{R}\) has a subsequence that converges vaguely to a sub-probability measure \(\mu\) with \(\mu(\mathbb{R}) \le 1\). The proof has three layers:

  • Cantor’s diagonal (Step 1): Bolzano–Weierstrass + diagonal extraction yields a subsequence \(\{F_{n_k}\}\) that converges pointwise on \(\mathbb{Q}\) to a non-decreasing limit \(G\).
  • Infimum extension (Step 2): Setting \(F(x) = \inf\{G(q): q \in \mathbb{Q},\, q > x\}\) produces a non-decreasing, right-continuous function — a valid sub-CDF — automatically, without any additional extraction.
  • Continuity-point convergence (Step 3): A two-sided squeeze argument shows \(F_{n_k}(x) \to F(x)\) at every continuity point of \(F\): the upper bound uses \(G(r_j) \downarrow F(x)\) (non-decreasingness of \(G\) plus the infimum definition), and the lower bound uses \(G(q_j) \uparrow F(x)\) (which requires continuity of \(F\) at \(x\) to match the left limit \(F(x^-)\) to \(F(x)\)).

2. Vague \(\neq\) Weak. The sub-CDF \(F\) may satisfy \(F(+\infty) < 1\) or \(F(-\infty) > 0\) when probability mass escapes to \(\pm\infty\) (cf.~the \(\delta_n\) counterexample). The associated measure \(\mu\) is then a strict sub-probability measure, and convergence is only vague, not weak.

3. Prokhorov’s Theorem: tightness upgrades vague to weak. A family \(\{\mu_n\}\) is tight if \[ \forall\,\varepsilon > 0,\ \exists\,\text{compact } K:\quad \inf_n \mu_n(K) \ge 1 - \varepsilon. \] Tightness is exactly what prevents mass escape:

  • If \(\{\mu_n\}\) is tight, any vaguely convergent subsequence \(\mu_{n_k}\) converges weakly and its limit is a proper probability measure (\(\mu(S) = 1\)).
  • Conversely, every weakly convergent sequence of probability measures is tight.
  • On a Polish (separable, completely metrizable) space, tightness \(\Leftrightarrow\) relative sequential compactness in the weak topology.

4. A practical tightness criterion. For random variables \(\{X_n\}\) on \(\mathbb{R}\), a sufficient condition for tightness is \[ \sup_n \mathbf{E}[|X_n|^p] < \infty \quad \text{for some } p > 0. \] By Markov’s inequality, \(\mu_n([-M,M]^c) \le M^{-p}\,\mathbf{E}[|X_n|^p] \to 0\) uniformly in \(n\) as \(M \to \infty\), which is precisely the tightness condition.
