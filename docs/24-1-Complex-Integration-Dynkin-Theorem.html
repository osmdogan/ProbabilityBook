52 Integration of Complex-Valued Functions

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) be a measure space. Consider a complex-valued function \(f: \Omega \to \mathbb{C}\) that is \(\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C})\)-measurable, where \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^2)\) under the usual identification \(\mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{R}^2\). Throughout, “measurable” for a \(\mathbb{C}\)-valued function means \(\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C})\)-measurable.

We can write \(f\) in terms of its real and imaginary components: \[ f(\omega) = u(\omega) + i v(\omega), \qquad u := \operatorname{Re} f, \quad v := \operatorname{Im} f, \] where \(u, v: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\). Since \(\mathbb{C}\) is a separable metric space, \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}) \otimes \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\); consequently \(f\) is measurable if and only if \(u\) and \(v\) are \(\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\)-measurable.

Definition 52.1 (Complex \(L^1\) Space) A measurable function \(f = u + iv : \Omega \to \mathbb{C}\) belongs to \(L_{\mathbb{C}}^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) if and only if \(u, v \in L_{\mathbb{R}}^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\). In this case, the complex integral is defined componentwise as: \[\int_{\Omega} f \, d\mu := \int_{\Omega} u \, d\mu + i \int_{\Omega} v \, d\mu\]

Proposition 52.1 (Conjugation Commutes with Integration) If \(f \in L_{\mathbb{C}}^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\), then \(\bar{f} \in L_{\mathbb{C}}^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) and \[\overline{\int_{\Omega} f \, d\mu} = \int_{\Omega} \bar{f} \, d\mu\]

Proof (Proof of Proposition 52.1). Write \(f = u + iv\) with \(u, v \in L_{\mathbb{R}}^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\). The function \(\bar{f} = u + i(-v)\) has real part \(u\) and imaginary part \(-v\), both in \(L_{\mathbb{R}}^1\), so \(\bar{f} \in L_{\mathbb{C}}^1\) by Definition 52.1. Using Definition 52.1 twice and \(\mathbb{R}\)-linearity of the real integral: \[ \overline{\int_{\Omega} f \, d\mu} = \overline{\int_{\Omega} u \, d\mu + i \int_{\Omega} v \, d\mu} = \int_{\Omega} u \, d\mu - i \int_{\Omega} v \, d\mu = \int_{\Omega} \bar{f} \, d\mu. \]

52.1 Norm Equivalence & Triangle Inequality

Let \(f = u + iv : \Omega \to \mathbb{C}\) be measurable, so that \(|f| = \sqrt{u^2 + v^2}\) is measurable as a continuous function of \(f\). Using the bounds relating the components to the magnitude: \[\max\{|u|, |v|\} \le \sqrt{u^2 + v^2} \le \sqrt{2} \max\{|u|, |v|\}\]

it follows that a measurable \(f\) satisfies \(f \in L_{\mathbb{C}}^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) if and only if \(|f| \in L_{\mathbb{R}}^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\), i.e.,: \[\|f\|_{L_{\mathbb{C}}^1} := \int_{\Omega} |f| \, d\mu < \infty\]

The measurability hypothesis cannot be omitted: on a probability space one may have \(|f| \equiv 1\) integrable while \(u\) is non-measurable. As in the real case, \(\|\cdot\|_{L_{\mathbb{C}}^1}\) is only a seminorm on functions; it is a norm on \(L_{\mathbb{C}}^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) once functions agreeing \(\mu\)-a.e. are identified, and we adopt this identification throughout.

It is routine to verify that \(\int_{\Omega} \cdot \, d\mu\) is \(\mathbb{C}\)-linear on \(L_{\mathbb{C}}^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\). Standard integration theorems transfer to complex-valued functions by applying them separately to real and imaginary parts. For instance, in the Dominated Convergence Theorem, if \(f_n \to f\) pointwise with \(|f_n| \le g\) for some \(g \in L_{\mathbb{R}}^1\), then \(|\operatorname{Re} f_n| \le g\) and \(|\operatorname{Im} f_n| \le g\), so the real theorem applies componentwise and yields \(\int_{\Omega} f_n \, d\mu \to \int_{\Omega} f \, d\mu\).

Theorem 52.1 (Triangle Inequality for Complex Integrals) If \(f \in L_{\mathbb{C}}^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\), then: \[\left| \int_{\Omega} f \, d\mu \right| \le \int_{\Omega} |f| \, d\mu\]

Proof (Proof of Theorem 52.1). If \(\int_{\Omega} f \, d\mu = 0\), the inequality holds trivially.

Suppose \(\int_{\Omega} f \, d\mu \ne 0\). Express the complex number \(\int_{\Omega} f \, d\mu\) in polar form: \[\int_{\Omega} f \, d\mu = r e^{i\theta}, \quad \text{where } r = \left| \int_{\Omega} f \, d\mu \right| > 0 \text{ and } \theta \in [0, 2\pi)\]

Multiplying by \(e^{-i\theta}\), we obtain a real non-negative quantity: \[r = e^{-i\theta} \int_{\Omega} f \, d\mu = \int_{\Omega} e^{-i\theta} f \, d\mu\]

Since \(r \in \mathbb{R}\), taking the real part of both sides yields: \[r = \operatorname{Re}\left( \int_{\Omega} e^{-i\theta} f \, d\mu \right) = \int_{\Omega} \operatorname{Re}\left( e^{-i\theta} f \right) d\mu\]

Using the pointwise real part bound \(\operatorname{Re}(z) \le |z|\) for any \(z \in \mathbb{C}\): \[\operatorname{Re}\left( e^{-i\theta} f(\omega) \right) \le \left| e^{-i\theta} f(\omega) \right| = |e^{-i\theta}| \cdot |f(\omega)| = |f(\omega)|\]

Both \(\operatorname{Re}(e^{-i\theta} f)\) and \(|f|\) lie in \(L_{\mathbb{R}}^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\), so integrating the pointwise bound and using monotonicity of the real integral gives: \[ \left| \int_{\Omega} f \, d\mu \right| = r = \int_{\Omega} \operatorname{Re}\left( e^{-i\theta} f \right) d\mu \le \int_{\Omega} |f| \, d\mu. \]

53 Dynkin’s Multiplicative Systems Theorem (Complex Case)

We extend Dynkin’s Multiplicative Systems Theorem to spaces of complex-valued bounded functions.

53.1 Notation and Conventions

Throughout this section \(\Omega\) is a non-empty set. For a \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{G}\) on \(\Omega\) and \(\mathbb{K} \in \{\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}\}\) we write \[ \mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{K}}(\Omega, \mathcal{G}) \triangleq \{ f: \Omega \to \mathbb{K} \mid f \text{ is bounded and } \mathcal{G}/\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{K})\text{-measurable} \}. \] We set \(\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{K}}(\Omega) \triangleq \mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{K}}(\Omega, 2^\Omega)\) so that \(\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{K}}(\Omega)\) is the set of all bounded \(\mathbb{K}\)-valued functions on \(\Omega\). In this notation \(\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}(\Omega, \mathcal{G})\) is the space written \(\mathbb{B}(\Omega, \mathcal{G}, \mathbb{R})\) in Theorem 29.1. The symbol \(\mathcal{B}(\cdot)\) is reserved for Borel \(\sigma\)-algebras.

Definition 53.1 (Bounded Convergence, Complex Case) A set \(\mathcal{H}\) of \(\mathbb{C}\)-valued functions on \(\Omega\) is closed under bounded convergence if for every sequence \(\{f_n\} \subseteq \mathcal{H}\) satisfying

  1. \(\sup_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \sup_{\omega \in \Omega} |f_n(\omega)| < \infty\), and
  2. \(f_n(\omega) \to f(\omega) \in \mathbb{C}\) for every \(\omega \in \Omega\),

the limit \(f\) belongs to \(\mathcal{H}\). For \(\mathbb{R}\)-valued families this reduces to Definition 29.1.

For a family \(M\) of \(\mathbb{C}\)-valued functions on \(\Omega\), \(\sigma(M)\) denotes the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\Omega\) making every \(f \in M\) measurable: \[ \sigma(M) \triangleq \sigma\big( f^{-1}(B) : f \in M, \, B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}) \big). \] Since a \(\mathbb{C}\)-valued \(f\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable if and only if \(\operatorname{Re}(f)\) and \(\operatorname{Im}(f)\) are, we also have \(\sigma(M) = \sigma\big( \{ \operatorname{Re}(f), \operatorname{Im}(f) : f \in M \} \big)\).

Theorem 53.1 (Complex Dynkin’s Multiplicative Systems Theorem) Let \(\mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}}(\Omega)\) be a \(\mathbb{C}\)-vector subspace containing the constant function \(1\) and closed under bounded convergence in the sense of Definition 53.1. Let \(M \subseteq \mathcal{H}\) be a multiplicative system (i.e., \(f, g \in M \implies fg \in M\)) that is closed under complex conjugation (\(\bar{M} \subseteq M\)).

Then \(\mathcal{H}\) contains all bounded, \(\mathbb{C}\)-valued, \(\sigma(M)\)-measurable functions: \[\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}}(\Omega, \sigma(M)) \subseteq \mathcal{H}\]

Proof (Proof of Theorem 53.1). Step 1 (\(M_0\) is a conjugation-closed algebra). Let \(M_0 := \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}(M \cup \{1\})\). Since \(\mathcal{H}\) is a \(\mathbb{C}\)-vector subspace containing \(M\) and \(1\), we have \(M_0 \subseteq \mathcal{H}\). It is an algebra: for \(f_i, g_j \in M \cup \{1\}\) and \(a_i, b_j \in \mathbb{C}\), \[ \Big( \sum_i a_i f_i \Big) \Big( \sum_j b_j g_j \Big) = \sum_{i, j} a_i b_j (f_i g_j) \in M_0, \] because \(f_i g_j \in M \cup \{1\}\) (\(M\) is multiplicative and \(1\) is the multiplicative unit). It is closed under conjugation because \(\overline{\sum_i a_i f_i} = \sum_i \bar{a}_i \bar{f}_i\), with \(\bar{f}_i \in M \cup \{1\}\) by the hypothesis \(\bar{M} \subseteq M\).

Step 2 (real parts). Define the sets of real-valued elements \[\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}} := \{ f \in \mathcal{H} : \operatorname{Im}(f) = 0 \}, \qquad M_0^{\mathbb{R}} := \{ f \in M_0 : \operatorname{Im}(f) = 0 \}.\]

  • \(1 \in \mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}}\), and \(\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}}\) is an \(\mathbb{R}\)-vector subspace of \(\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}(\Omega)\).
  • \(\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}}\) is closed under bounded convergence in the sense of Definition 29.1: if \(f_n \in \mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}}\) are uniformly bounded and converge pointwise to \(f\), then \(f \in \mathcal{H}\) since \(\mathcal{H}\) is closed under bounded convergence, and \(f\) is real-valued as a pointwise limit of real-valued functions.
  • Since \(M_0\) is closed under conjugation (Step 1), every \(f \in M_0\) decomposes into elements of \(M_0^{\mathbb{R}}\): \[\operatorname{Re}(f) = \frac{f + \bar{f}}{2} \in M_0^{\mathbb{R}}, \qquad \operatorname{Im}(f) = \frac{f - \bar{f}}{2i} \in M_0^{\mathbb{R}}\]
  • \(M_0^{\mathbb{R}} \subseteq M_0 \cap \mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}}\), and \(M_0^{\mathbb{R}}\) is a real multiplicative system: if \(f, g \in M_0^{\mathbb{R}}\) then \(fg \in M_0\) because \(M_0\) is an algebra, and \(fg\) is real-valued, hence \(fg \in M_0^{\mathbb{R}}\).

Step 3 (\(\sigma(M) = \sigma(M_0) = \sigma(M_0^{\mathbb{R}})\)). This is the only place where the hypothesis \(\bar{M} \subseteq M\) is used.

  • (\(\sigma(M) \subseteq \sigma(M_0^{\mathbb{R}})\)) For \(f \in M\) we have \(\operatorname{Re}(f), \operatorname{Im}(f) \in M_0^{\mathbb{R}}\) by Step 2, so both are \(\sigma(M_0^{\mathbb{R}})\)-measurable, and therefore so is \(f = \operatorname{Re}(f) + i \operatorname{Im}(f)\).
  • (\(\sigma(M_0^{\mathbb{R}}) \subseteq \sigma(M_0) \subseteq \sigma(M)\)) Every element of \(M_0\) is a finite \(\mathbb{C}\)-linear combination of elements of \(M \cup \{1\}\), hence \(\sigma(M)\)-measurable.

Step 4 (conclusion). By Step 2 the pair \(M_0^{\mathbb{R}} \subseteq \mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}} \subseteq \mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}(\Omega)\) satisfies all hypotheses of the real Dynkin Multiplicative Systems Theorem (Theorem 29.1), so \[\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}(\Omega, \sigma(M_0^{\mathbb{R}})) \subseteq \mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}}\]

Now let \(g \in \mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}}(\Omega, \sigma(M))\) and write \(g = u + iv\). Then \(u, v\) are \(\sigma(M)\)-measurable and \(|u|, |v| \le |g|\) are bounded, so by Step 3, \[u, v \in \mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}(\Omega, \sigma(M_0^{\mathbb{R}})) \subseteq \mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}} \subseteq \mathcal{H}.\] Since \(\mathcal{H}\) is a \(\mathbb{C}\)-vector subspace, \(g = u + iv \in \mathcal{H}\). Thus, \(\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}}(\Omega, \sigma(M)) \subseteq \mathcal{H}\).

53.2 Application to Fourier Exponentials on \(\mathbb{R}^d\)

The following result shows that if a complex vector subspace \(\mathcal H\) of all bounded complex Borel functions contains every exponential \(e_\xi(x)=e^{i\xi\cdot x}\) and is closed under bounded convergence, then \(\mathcal H\) must be the entire space of bounded complex Borel functions.

Corollary 53.1 (Exponential Generators) Let \(\mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\) be a \(\mathbb{C}\)-vector subspace of bounded complex Borel functions that is closed under bounded convergence. For \(\xi \in \mathbb{R}^d\), define the character/exponential function \(e_\xi(x) := e^{i \xi \cdot x}\).

If \(e_\xi \in \mathcal{H}\) for all \(\xi \in \mathbb{R}^d\), then \(\mathcal{H}\) contains all bounded complex Borel-measurable functions: \[ \mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)) \subseteq \mathcal{H} \]

Proof (Proof of Corollary 53.1). Set \(M := \{ e_\xi : \xi \in \mathbb{R}^d \} \subseteq \mathcal{H}\).

  1. Contains 1: \(1 = e_0 \in M \subseteq \mathcal{H}\), so the constant function \(1\) belongs to \(\mathcal{H}\).
  2. Closed under conjugation: \(\overline{e_\xi(x)} = \overline{e^{i\xi \cdot x}} = e^{-i\xi \cdot x} = e_{-\xi}(x)\), so \(\bar{M} = M\).
  3. Multiplicative System: \(e_\xi(x) e_\eta(x) = e^{i\xi \cdot x} e^{i\eta \cdot x} = e^{i(\xi + \eta) \cdot x} = e_{\xi + \eta}(x) \in M\).

All hypotheses of Theorem 53.1 are met (note that conjugation-closure is required of \(M\) only, not of \(\mathcal{H}\)), so \(\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^d, \sigma(M)) \subseteq \mathcal{H}\).

Claim: \(\sigma(M) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\).

\(\sigma(M) \subseteq \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\): Each \(e_\xi\) is continuous on \(\mathbb{R}^d\), hence \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)/\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C})\)-measurable.

\(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) \subseteq \sigma(M)\): Fix \(j \in \{1, \dots, d\}\) and let \(\pi_j(x) := x_j\) be the \(j\)-th coordinate map. Writing \(\mathbf{e}_j\) for the \(j\)-th standard basis vector, the function \(e_{k^{-1} \mathbf{e}_j}(x) = e^{i x_j / k}\) is \(\sigma(M)\)-measurable for every \(k \in \mathbb{N}\), hence so is \[ g_k(x) := k \operatorname{Im}\big( e_{k^{-1} \mathbf{e}_j}(x) \big) = k \sin(x_j / k). \] Since \(\sin(t)/t \to 1\) as \(t \to 0\), we have \(g_k(x) \to x_j\) for every \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\) (for \(x_j = 0\) both sides vanish). A pointwise limit of \(\sigma(M)\)-measurable real functions is \(\sigma(M)\)-measurable, so each \(\pi_j\) is \(\sigma(M)\)-measurable, and therefore \[\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) = \sigma(\pi_1, \dots, \pi_d) \subseteq \sigma(M).\] The functions \(g_k\) are not uniformly bounded, so this argument establishes measurability only; it is not an appeal to bounded convergence.

Combining, \(\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)) = \mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^d, \sigma(M)) \subseteq \mathcal{H}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 53.1 (An Alternative Argument for \(d = 1\)). Let \(d = 1\) and \(M = \{ e_\xi : \xi \in \mathbb{R} \}\) as in the proof of Corollary 53.1. For \(\alpha < \beta\) let \(A(\alpha, \beta) := \{ e^{i\theta} : \alpha < \theta < \beta \} \subseteq \mathbb{T}\). Each such set is relatively open in \(\mathbb{T}\), hence Borel in \(\mathbb{C}\), so its \(e_\xi\)-preimage lies in \(\sigma(M)\). Fix \(-\infty < a < b < \infty\). For every \(\xi > 0\) (no restriction on the arc length is needed), \(e^{i \xi x} \in A(\xi a, \xi b)\) if and only if \(\xi x \in (\xi a, \xi b) + 2\pi \mathbb{Z}\), so \[e_\xi^{-1}\big( A(\xi a, \xi b) \big) = \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} \left( (a, b) + \frac{2\pi n}{\xi} \right) \in \sigma(M)\] Taking \(\xi = 1/k\) for \(k \in \mathbb{N}\), so that the period is \(2\pi k\), we claim \[(a, b) = \bigcap_{k \in \mathbb{N}} e_{1/k}^{-1}\left( A\left( \frac{a}{k}, \frac{b}{k} \right) \right) \in \sigma(M)\] The inclusion “\(\subseteq\)” is the term \(n = 0\) of each union. For “\(\supseteq\)”, put \(C := \max(|a|, |b|)\) and suppose \(x\) lies in the intersection but \(x \notin (a, b)\). Then for each \(k\) there is \(n_k \ne 0\) with \(x - 2\pi k n_k \in (a, b)\), whence \(2\pi k \le |2\pi k n_k| \le |x| + C\); this fails as soon as \(2\pi k > |x| + C\), a contradiction. Since the bounded open intervals generate \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) (every open subset of \(\mathbb{R}\) is a countable union of bounded open intervals), \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}) \subseteq \sigma(M)\).

54 Uniqueness of Measures via Characteristic Functions

Using these Dynkin machinery results, we obtain the fundamental uniqueness theorem for probability measures on \(\mathbb{R}^d\).

Corollary 54.1 (Uniqueness Theorem for Characteristic Functions) Let \(\mu, \nu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\) be two probability measures. Suppose their characteristic functions coincide for all \(\xi \in \mathbb{R}^d\): \[\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} e^{i \xi \cdot x} \, d\mu(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} e^{i \xi \cdot x} \, d\nu(x), \quad \forall \xi \in \mathbb{R}^d\]

Then \(\mu = \nu\) on \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\).

Proof (Proof of Corollary 54.1). Every \(f \in \mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\) is \(\mu\)- and \(\nu\)-integrable, since \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are finite; in particular both sides of the hypothesis are well defined. Define the set of bounded Borel functions whose integrals under \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) agree: \[\mathcal{H} := \left\{ f \in \mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)) : \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f \, d\mu = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f \, d\nu \right\}\]

We check the hypotheses of Corollary 53.1:

  1. \(\mathbb{C}\)-vector subspace: Follows from \(\mathbb{C}\)-linearity of the integral on \(L_{\mathbb{C}}^1\).
  2. Closed under bounded convergence: If \(f_n \in \mathcal{H}\) with \(f_n \to f\) pointwise on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) and \(|f_n| \le C < \infty\) for all \(n\), then \(f\) is bounded and Borel, and the constant \(C\) is integrable for both \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) because these measures are finite. Hence the Dominated Convergence Theorem applies to both and gives \[\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f \, d\mu = \lim_{n \to \infty} \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f_n \, d\mu = \lim_{n \to \infty} \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f_n \, d\nu = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f \, d\nu \implies f \in \mathcal{H}\]
  3. Contains the characters: By assumption, \(e_\xi \in \mathcal{H}\) for all \(\xi \in \mathbb{R}^d\).

(The set \(\mathcal{H}\) is also closed under complex conjugation, by Proposition 52.1 applied to both measures; this is not needed here, since Corollary 53.1 requires conjugation-closure only of the multiplicative system \(\{e_\xi\}\).)

By Corollary 53.1, \(\mathcal{H}\) contains all bounded complex Borel-measurable functions: \[ \mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)) \subseteq \mathcal{H}. \]

In particular, for any Borel set \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\), taking the indicator function \(f = \mathbf{1}_B \in \mathcal{H}\) gives: \[ \mu(B) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{1}_B \, d\mu = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{1}_B \, d\nu = \nu(B). \]

Therefore, \(\mu = \nu\) on \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\).

Remark 54.1 (Extension to Finite Measures). The proof used only that \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are finite. Consequently, if \(\mu, \nu\) are finite Borel measures on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) whose Fourier transforms \(\hat{\mu}(\xi) = \int e^{i \xi \cdot x} \, d\mu(x)\) and \(\hat{\nu}(\xi)\) agree for all \(\xi \in \mathbb{R}^d\), then \(\mu = \nu\); the case \(\xi = 0\) forces the total masses to coincide.
