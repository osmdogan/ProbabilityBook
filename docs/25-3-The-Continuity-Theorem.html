55  Lecture 25.3: The Continuity Theorem

55.1 Introduction

In probability theory, weak convergence of probability measures (\(\mu_n \implies \mu\)) is equivalent to pointwise convergence of their characteristic functions (\(\hat{\mu}_n(\xi) \to \hat{\mu}(\xi)\)).

A key result is that the converse also holds provided the pointwise limit function \(\varphi(\xi)\) is continuous at the origin \(\xi = 0\).

55.2 Key Theorems and Technical Lemmas

The characteristic function of one probability measure can be integrated against another probability measure in either order, because the Fourier kernel \(e^{i\xi^\top x}\) is bounded and Fubini’s theorem allows the order of integration to be exchanged.

Lemma 55.1 (Fubini/Fourier Duality Identity) Let \(\mu, \nu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\) be probability measures on \(\mathbb{R}^d\). Then: \[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \hat{\mu}(\xi) \nu(d\xi) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \hat{\nu}(x) \mu(dx) \]

Proof (Proof of Lemma 55.1). By definition of the characteristic function \(\hat{\mu}(\xi) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} e^{i x \cdot \xi} \mu(dx)\), we write: \[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \hat{\mu}(\xi) \nu(d\xi) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} e^{i x \cdot \xi} \mu(dx) \right) \nu(d\xi) \] Since \(|e^{i x \cdot \xi}| = 1\), we apply Fubini’s Theorem to interchange the order of integration: \[ = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mu(dx) \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} e^{i x \cdot \xi} \nu(d\xi) \right) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \hat{\nu}(x) \mu(dx) \]

Remark 55.1. This identity says that the two measures can be interchanged in the Fourier kernel: \[ \int\!\!\int e^{i\xi^\top x}\,\mu(dx)\nu(d\xi) \] can be integrated first with respect to either measure. Symbolically, \[ \boxed{ \int \hat\mu\,d\nu = \int \hat\nu\,d\mu. } \] This is essentially a Fourier duality identity: the characteristic function of \(\mu\), integrated against \(\nu\), equals the characteristic function of \(\nu\), integrated against \(\mu\).

The following corollary shows that the real part of the characteristic function also satisfies a similar duality identity. Using \(\operatorname{Re}\hat\mu(\xi) = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\cos(\xi^\top x)\,\mu(dx)\), we have \[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\hat{\mu}(\xi) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left[ 1 - \cos(\xi^\top x) \right] \mu(dx). \]

Corollary 55.1 (Real Part Difference Identity) Taking the real part of both sides yields: \[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\hat{\mu}(\xi) \right] \nu(d\xi) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\hat{\nu}(x) \right] \mu(dx) \]

Proof (Proof of Corollary 55.1). From the previous lemma, \[ \int_{\mathbb R^d}\hat\mu(\xi)\,\nu(d\xi) = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\hat\nu(x)\,\mu(dx). \] Since both sides are absolutely integrable, we may take real parts: \[ \int_{\mathbb R^d}\operatorname{Re}\hat\mu(\xi)\,\nu(d\xi) = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\operatorname{Re}\hat\nu(x)\,\mu(dx). \] Now use the fact that both \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are probability measures. Hence \[ \int_{\mathbb R^d}1\,\nu(d\xi)=1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \int_{\mathbb R^d}1\,\mu(dx)=1. \] Subtracting the preceding equality from \(1=1\), we obtain \[ \begin{aligned} \int_{\mathbb R^d} \left[1-\operatorname{Re}\hat\mu(\xi)\right]\nu(d\xi) &= 1-\int_{\mathbb R^d}\operatorname{Re}\hat\mu(\xi)\,\nu(d\xi)\\ &= 1-\int_{\mathbb R^d}\operatorname{Re}\hat\nu(x)\,\mu(dx)\\ &= \int_{\mathbb R^d} \left[1-\operatorname{Re}\hat\nu(x)\right]\mu(dx). \end{aligned} \]

55.3 Characteristic Tail Estimate

A characteristic function being sufficiently close to 1 around the origin forces the underlying probability distribution to put little mass in the tails.

Theorem 55.1 (Characteristic Tail Estimate) Let \(\rho(x)\) be a probability density on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) supported in the closed unit ball \(\bar{B}_1 = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^d : |x| \le 1\}\).

Write \[ \hat{\rho}(\xi) := \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} e^{i\xi\cdot x}\rho(x)\,dx. \]

By the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma (Theorem 53.1), \(\hat{\rho}(\xi) \to 0\) as \(|\xi| \to \infty\), so choose \(M > 0\) such that \(|\hat{\rho}(\xi)| \le \frac{1}{2}\) for all \(|\xi| \ge M\).

Then for any probability measure \(\mu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\) and any \(a > 0\): \[ \mu\left( \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^d : |x| \ge a \} \right) \le 2 \int_{B_1} \left[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\hat{\mu}\left( \frac{M}{a} x \right) \right] \rho(x) \, dx \]

Special Case: 1D Uniform Density (\(d = 1\)):

Let \(\rho(x) = \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}\). Its characteristic function is \[ \hat{\rho}(\xi) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\sin \xi}{\xi}, & \xi \ne 0,\\ 1, & \xi = 0. \end{cases} \]

  • We have \(|\hat{\rho}(\xi)| \le \frac{1}{|\xi|} \le \frac{1}{2} \iff |\xi| \ge 2\), so we choose \(M = 2\).
  • For any real-valued random variable \(X\): \[ \mathbb{P}(|X| \ge a) \le \int_{-1}^{1} \left[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\varphi_X\left( \frac{2}{a} x \right) \right] dx = \frac{a}{2} \int_{-2/a}^{2/a} \left[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\varphi_X(u) \right] du \] This is a very useful inequality because it relates a tail probability to the behavior of the characteristic function arbitrarily close to zero. For large \(a\), \(\left[-\frac2a,\frac2a\right]\) is a very small neighborhood of zero.

Proof (Proof of Theorem 55.1). For any \(\varepsilon > 0\), define the rescaled probability measure \(\nu(dx) = \varepsilon^{-d} \rho(x/\varepsilon) \, dx\). Its characteristic function is: \[ \hat{\nu}(\xi) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} e^{i \xi \cdot x} \varepsilon^{-d} \rho(x/\varepsilon) \, dx = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} e^{i (\varepsilon \xi) \cdot y} \rho(y) \, dy = \hat{\rho}(\varepsilon \xi)\]

Applying Corollary 55.1, we have: \[\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\hat{\mu}(\xi) \right] \varepsilon^{-d} \rho(\xi/\varepsilon) \, d\xi = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\hat{\rho}(\varepsilon x) \right] \mu(dx)\] Restricting the integral on the right-hand side to \(\{x \in \mathbb{R}^d : |x| \ge M/\varepsilon\}\), where \(1 - \operatorname{Re}\hat{\rho}(\varepsilon x) \ge 1 - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\): \[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\hat{\rho}(\varepsilon x) \right] \mu(dx) \ge \frac{1}{2} \mu\left( \{x \in \mathbb{R}^d : |x| \ge M/\varepsilon\} \right) \] Setting \(a = M/\varepsilon\) (so \(\varepsilon = M/a\)) and combining the two displays above: \[ \frac{1}{2}\mu\left( \{x \in \mathbb{R}^d : |x| \ge a\} \right) \le \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\hat{\mu}(\xi) \right] \varepsilon^{-d} \rho(\xi/\varepsilon) \, d\xi \] Substitute \(x = \xi/\varepsilon\) (so \(\xi = \tfrac{M}{a}x\) and \(d\xi = (M/a)^d\,dx\)) on the right-hand side. The factors \(\varepsilon^{-d}\) and \((M/a)^d = \varepsilon^d\) cancel, and since \(\rho\) is supported on \(\bar{B}_1\): \[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\hat{\mu}(\xi) \right] \varepsilon^{-d} \rho(\xi/\varepsilon) \, d\xi = \int_{\bar{B}_1} \left[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\hat{\mu}\!\left( \tfrac{M}{a} x \right) \right] \rho(x) \, dx \] Multiplying both sides by \(2\) yields: \[ \mu\left( \{x \in \mathbb{R}^d : |x| \ge a\} \right) \le 2 \int_{B_1} \left[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\hat{\mu}\left( \frac{M}{a} x \right) \right] \rho(x) \, dx \]

Example 55.1 Take \(d=1\) and choose \(\rho(x)=\frac12\mathbf 1_{\{|x|\le1\}}\) so that $(u)= and \(M=2\). Now let \(X\sim N(0,1)\). Its characteristic function is \(\varphi_X(u)=e^{-u^2/2}\). Since this is real and positive, \[ 1-\operatorname{Re}\varphi_X(u) = 1-e^{-u^2/2}. \] The theorem therefore says that, for every \(a>0\), \[ \mathbb P(|X|\ge a) \le \int_{-1}^{1} \left[ 1-e^{-\frac12(2x/a)^2} \right]dx, \] Take \(a=2\). Then \(\mathbb P(|X|\ge2) \le \int_{-1}^{1} (1-e^{-x^2/2})\,dx\). The right-hand side is approximately \(0.301\). The actual normal tail is \(\mathbb P(|X|\ge2) = 2(1-\Phi(2)) \approx0.0455\). So the bound is not particularly sharp here: \(0.0455\le0.301\). But sharpness is not the main purpose of the theorem.

Think of the two sides as measuring two completely different things: \[ \underbrace{\mathbb P(|X|\ge a)}_{\text{probability in the far tail}} \] versus \[ \underbrace{ \int_{-1}^{1} \left[1-\varphi_X\left(\frac{2x}{a}\right)\right]dx }_{\text{behavior of }\varphi_X\text{ near }0}. \] As \(a\) gets large, the arguments \(\frac{2x}{a}\) become very small. Thus the theorem says: To control the probability that \(X\) is very large, it is enough to understand how its characteristic function behaves very close to zero.

55.4 Tightness Criterion

If a sequence of probability distributions has characteristic functions that converge pointwise to a function that is continuous at the origin, then the probability mass cannot escape to infinity.

Corollary 55.2 (Tightness Criterion) Let \(\{\mu_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) be a sequence of probability measures on \((\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\). If:

  1. \(\varphi(\xi) := \lim_{n \to \infty} \hat{\mu}_n(\xi)\) exists for all \(\xi \in \mathbb{R}^d\), and
  2. \(\varphi(\xi)\) is continuous at \(\xi = 0\),

then the family \(\{\mu_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) is tight.

Proof (Proof of Corollary 55.2). Fix \(\varepsilon > 0\). By Theorem 55.1 \[ \mu_n(\{x \in \mathbb{R}^d : |x| > a\}) \le 2 \int_{B_1} \left[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\hat{\mu}_n\left( \frac{M}{a} x \right) \right] \rho(x) \, dx \] Since \(\hat{\mu}_n(0) = 1\) for all \(n\), we have \(\varphi(0) = \lim_n \hat{\mu}_n(0) = 1\). Since \(\varphi\) is continuous at \(0\): \[ \delta(a) := \sup_{|x| \le 1} \left[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\varphi\left( \frac{M}{a} x \right) \right] \xrightarrow{a \to \infty} 0 \] (Note \(1 - \operatorname{Re}\varphi(\xi) \ge 0\) since \(|\varphi(\xi)| \le 1\), so the supremum equals \(\sup|\cdots|\).) As \(a \to \infty\), \(\frac{M}{a}x \to 0\) uniformly for \(|x| \le 1\), so \(\operatorname{Re}\varphi(\frac{M}{a}x) \to 1\) uniformly by continuity, giving \(\delta(a) \to 0\).

Choose \(a_0 > 0\) large enough such that \(2 \delta(a_0) < \frac{\varepsilon}{4}\).

Since \(|\hat{\mu}_n(\xi)| \le 1\) for all \(n\) and \(\xi\), the integrand satisfies \(0 \le 1 - \operatorname{Re}\hat{\mu}_n(\frac{M}{a}x) \le 2\), so \(2\rho(x)\) is an integrable dominating function on \(B_1\) (as \(\int_{B_1}\rho(x)\,dx \le 1\)). By the Dominated Convergence Theorem, using pointwise convergence \(\hat{\mu}_n(\xi) \to \varphi(\xi)\) for each fixed \(\xi\): \[ \begin{align*} &2 \int_{B_1} \left[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\hat{\mu}_n\left( \frac{M}{a_0} x \right) \right] \rho(x) \, dx\\ &\longrightarrow 2 \int_{B_1} \left[ 1 - \operatorname{Re}\varphi\left( \frac{M}{a_0} x \right) \right] \rho(x) \, dx \le 2\delta(a_0) < \frac{\varepsilon}{4} \end{align*} \] Here DCT is applied to the integrands inside the integral, dominated by \(2\rho(x)\); the constant factor \(2\) is outside the integral. Since the limit is \(< \varepsilon/4\), there exists \(N \in \mathbb{N}\) such that for all \(n \ge N\) the integral is within \(\varepsilon/4\) of its limit, hence: \[ \mu_n(\mathbb{R}^d \setminus \bar{B}_{a_0}) \le 2\int_{B_1}\left[1 - \operatorname{Re}\hat{\mu}_n\!\left(\tfrac{M}{a_0}x\right)\right]\rho(x)\,dx < \frac{\varepsilon}{4} + \frac{\varepsilon}{4} = \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \] For each of the finitely many \(n<N\), \(\mu_n(\mathbb{R}^d\setminus\bar{B}_a)\to0\) as \(a\to\infty\). Choose \(a\ge a_0\) so that these finitely many tails are all less than \(\varepsilon\). For \(n\ge N\), enlarging the ball can only decrease the tail, so \[ \mu_n(\mathbb{R}^d\setminus\bar{B}_a) \le \mu_n(\mathbb{R}^d\setminus\bar{B}_{a_0}) < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \] Thus \(\sup_n \mu_n(\mathbb{R}^d\setminus\bar{B}_a) < \varepsilon\), and \(\{\mu_n\}\) is tight.

55.5 Main Result: Lévy’s Continuity Theorem

A pointwise limit of characteristic functions is automatically a characteristic function of a probability distribution, provided the limit is continuous at the origin; moreover, the corresponding probability distributions converge weakly.

Theorem 55.2 (Lévy’s Continuity Theorem) Let \(\{\mu_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\). Suppose that \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \hat{\mu}_n(\xi) = \varphi(\xi)\) exists for all \(\xi \in \mathbb{R}^d\).

If \(\varphi\) is continuous at \(\xi = 0\), then:

  1. \(\varphi\) is the characteristic function of a unique probability measure \(\mu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d))\) (i.e., \(\varphi = \hat{\mu}\)).
  2. \(\mu_n \implies \mu\) weakly as \(n \to \infty\).

Proof (Proof Strategy (Subsequence Argument)).

  1. Tightness: By the Tightness Criterion above, \(\{\mu_n\}\) is tight.

  2. Relative Compactness: By Prokhorov’s Theorem (Theorem 49.1 and Corollary 49.1), tightness implies relative compactness. Thus, every subsequence of \(\{\mu_n\}\) has a further weakly convergent subsequence.

  3. Construction of \(\mu\): By Step 2, pick any subsequence with \(\mu_{n_k} \implies \mu\) for some \(\mu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^d)\). Since \(x \mapsto e^{i\xi\cdot x}\) is bounded and continuous, weak convergence gives \(\hat{\mu}_{n_k}(\xi) \to \hat{\mu}(\xi)\) for every \(\xi\). But by assumption \(\hat{\mu}_{n_k}(\xi) \to \varphi(\xi)\), so \(\hat{\mu} = \varphi\). This establishes Conclusion 1: \(\varphi\) is the characteristic function of \(\mu\), and \(\mu\) is the unique such probability measure (by injectivity of the Fourier transform on \(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^d)\)).

  4. Uniqueness of Every Subsequential Limit: Suppose for contradiction that \(\mu_n \not\implies \mu\). Then there exist \(f \in C_b(\mathbb{R}^d)\), \(\varepsilon > 0\), and a subsequence \(\{\mu_{n_j}\}\) such that: \[ \left| \int f \, d\mu_{n_j} - \int f \, d\mu \right| \ge \varepsilon \quad \forall j \] By relative compactness, \(\{\mu_{n_j}\}\) has a further subsequence \(\mu_{n_{j_k}} \implies \nu\) for some \(\nu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^d)\).

    Since \(x \mapsto e^{i\xi\cdot x}\) is bounded and continuous, weak convergence \(\mu_{n_{j_k}} \implies \nu\) gives \(\hat{\mu}_{n_{j_k}}(\xi) \to \hat{\nu}(\xi)\) for all \(\xi\). But \(\hat{\mu}_{n_{j_k}}(\xi) \to \varphi(\xi) = \hat{\mu}(\xi)\) by assumption and Step 3. By uniqueness of limits: \[ \hat{\nu}(\xi) = \varphi(\xi) = \hat{\mu}(\xi) \] Since characteristic functions uniquely determine probability measures, \(\nu = \mu\).

  5. Conclusion: Since \(\mu_{n_{j_k}} \implies \nu = \mu\) and \(f \in C_b(\mathbb{R}^d)\), we have \(\int f\,d\mu_{n_{j_k}} \to \int f\,d\mu\). This contradicts \(\left|\int f\,d\mu_{n_j} - \int f\,d\mu\right| \ge \varepsilon\) for all \(j\) (in particular for \(j = j_k\) with \(k\) large). Therefore, the entire sequence \(\mu_n \implies \mu\).

55.6 Takeaways

  1. On \(\mathbb{R}^d\), a characteristic function is a complete distributional fingerprint: if two probability measures have the same characteristic function, then they are equal.
  2. Pointwise convergence of characteristic functions is not enough by itself; the crucial extra hypothesis is continuity at the origin. This ensures the limit function is a genuine characteristic function and prevents pathological non-tight behavior.
  3. Lévy’s continuity theorem turns Fourier-space convergence into weak convergence of laws: if \(\hat{\mu}_n(\xi) \to \varphi(\xi)\) for all \(\xi\) and \(\varphi\) is continuous at \(0\), then there exists a probability measure \(\mu\) with \(\varphi = \hat{\mu}\) and \(\mu_n \implies \mu\).
  4. The proof hinges on tightness and Prokhorov’s theorem: from pointwise convergence of characteristic functions, one obtains tightness, hence relative compactness of the sequence, and every subsequential limit must have the same Fourier transform.
  5. In practice, the continuity theorem is the Fourier-analytic bridge between transform convergence and distributional convergence. It is the standard tool for proving CLTs and other weak-limit results by checking pointwise convergence of characteristic functions.
