67  Lecture 31.1: Orthogonal Projection

67.1 Introduction: From Finite Dimensions to Hilbert Spaces

In a finite-dimensional inner product space \(H\), any linear subspace \(K \subseteq H\) admits an orthogonal projection operator \(P_K: H \to K\).

  • \(P_K\) is called the orthogonal projection onto \(K\).
  • If \(K\) has an orthonormal basis \(\{e_n\}\), this projection is given by: \[ P_K(v) = \sum_{n} \langle v, e_n \rangle e_n = \sum_n \left\langle v, \frac{b_n}{\|b_n\|} \right\rangle \frac{b_n}{\|b_n\|} \] where \(P_K(v) = v\) for \(v \in K\), and \(P_K(w) = 0\) for \(w \in K^\perp\), and \(K^\perp\) is the orthogonal complement of \(K\) in \(H\).
  • If \(z=v+w\) with \(v \in K\) and \(w \in K^\perp\), then \(P_K(z) = v\): it extracts the \(K\)-component of a vector and discards its \(K^\perp\)-component: \[ \begin{align*} P_K(z) &= P_K(v + w) = \sum_{n} \langle v + w, e_n \rangle e_n\\ & = \sum_n \langle v, e_n \rangle e_n + \sum_n \langle w, e_n \rangle e_n = v + 0 = v. \end{align*} \]

We extend this geometric concept to infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, specifically the space of square-integrable random variables \(H = L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\).

67.2 Hilbert Space Geometry

Definition 67.1 (Hilbert Space) A Hilbert space \(H\) is a complete inner product space. For random variables \(X, Y \in L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\), the inner product and induced norm are defined by:

\[ \langle X, Y \rangle := \mathbb{E}[XY], \quad \|X\| := \sqrt{\langle X, X \rangle} = \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X^2]} \]

In any inner product space, we have (i) the Pythagorean Theorem and (ii) the Parallelogram Law. These identities are fundamental to the geometry of Hilbert spaces.

  1. Pythagorean Theorem: If \(X \perp Y\) (i.e., \(\langle X, Y \rangle = 0\)), then: \[ \|X + Y\|^2 = \|X\|^2 + \|Y\|^2 \]
  2. Parallelogram Law: For all \(X, Y \in H\): \[ \|X + Y\|^2 + \|X - Y\|^2 = 2\left(\|X\|^2 + \|Y\|^2\right) \]

Lemma 67.1 (Closed Subspaces) A linear subspace \(K \subseteq H\) is a Hilbert space in its own right under the induced inner product if and only if \(K\) is closed in \(H\).

67.3 Best Approximation Theorem

The core geometric result underpinning orthogonal projection in Hilbert spaces is the existence and uniqueness of a closest point in any closed subspace.

Theorem 67.1 (Best Approximation Theorem) Let \(K \subseteq H\) be a closed subspace of a Hilbert space \(H\). For any \(X \in H\), there exists a unique element \(Y \in K\) that minimizes the distance to \(X\):

\[ \|X - Y\|^2 = d(X, K)^2 := \inf_{Z \in K} \|X - Z\|^2. \]

Proof (Proof of Theorem 67.1).

  • Uniqueness: Suppose \(Y, Z \in K\) both satisfy \(\|X - Y\|^2 = \|X - Z\|^2 = d(X, K)^2\). Applying the Parallelogram Law to \(Y - X\) and \(Z - X\):

    \[ \begin{align*} \|Y - Z\|^2 + 4 \left\| \frac{Y + Z}{2} - X \right\|^2 = 2 \left( \|Y - X\|^2 + \|Z - X\|^2 \right). \end{align*} \]

    Since \(\frac{Y + Z}{2} \in K\), we have \(\left\| \frac{Y + Z}{2} - X \right\|^2 \ge d(X, K)^2\). Substituting this inequality yields:

    \[ \begin{align*} &\|Y - Z\|^2 + 4 d(X, K)^2 \le 2(d(X, K)^2 + d(X, K)^2) = 4 d(X, K)^2\\ &\implies \|Y - Z\|^2 \le 0. \end{align*} \]

    Hence \(Y = Z\) almost surely.

  • Existence: Let \(\{Y_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset K\) be a minimizing sequence such that \(\|X - Y_n\|^2 \le d(X, K)^2 + \frac{1}{n}\). Applying the Parallelogram Law to \(Y_n - X\) and \(Y_m - X\):

    \[ \|Y_n - Y_m\|^2 + 4 \left\| \frac{Y_n + Y_m}{2} - X \right\|^2 \le 2 \left( \|Y_n - X\|^2 + \|Y_m - X\|^2 \right). \]

    Since \(\frac{Y_n + Y_m}{2} \in K\), \(\left\| \frac{Y_n + Y_m}{2} - X \right\|^2 \ge d(X, K)^2\). Therefore:

    \[ \begin{align*} &\|Y_n - Y_m\|^2 + 4 d(X, K)^2 \le 4 d(X, K)^2 + 2\left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{1}{m} \right)\\ &\implies \|Y_n - Y_m\|^2 \le 2\left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{1}{m} \right). \end{align*} \]

    Thus, \(\{Y_n\}\) is a Cauchy sequence in \(H\). Since \(H\) is complete and \(K\) is closed, \(Y_n \to Y\) for some \(Y \in K\). By continuity of the norm:

    \[ \|X - Y\| \le \|X - Y_n\| + \|Y_n - Y\| \to d(X, K) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty. \]

    So \(\|X - Y\| = d(X, K)\).

67.4 Characterization via Orthogonality

Theorem 67.2 (Orthogonal Error Characterization) Let \(K \subseteq H\) be a closed subspace and \(X \in H\). An element \(Y \in K\) is the unique closest point to \(X\) if and only if:

\[ X - Y \perp K \quad \text{i.e., } \langle X - Y, Z \rangle = 0 \quad \forall Z \in K. \]

Remark 67.1. Let \(P_K: H \to K\) be the mapping that assigns to each \(X \in H\) its unique closest point in \(K\). Then \(\forall Y \in K\), Theorem 67.2 gives the orthogonality condition: \[ \begin{aligned} X - P_K(X) \perp K &\iff \langle X - P_K(X), Y \rangle = 0 \\ &\iff \mathbb{E}\left[P_K(X) Y\right] = \mathbb{E}[XY]. \end{aligned} \]

Proof (Proof of Theorem 67.2).

  • (\(\Rightarrow\)): Assume \(Y \in K\) minimizes \(d(X, K)\). For any fixed \(Z \in K\) and \(t \in \mathbb{R}\), define the quadratic function:

    \[ g(t) := \|X - (Y + t Z)\|^2 = \|X - Y\|^2 - 2t \langle X - Y, Z \rangle + t^2 \|Z\|^2 \]

    Since \(Y + t Z \in K\), \(g(t)\) achieves its global minimum at \(t = 0\). Differentiating at \(t = 0\) gives:

    \[ 0 = g'(0) = -2 \langle X - Y, Z \rangle \implies \langle X - Y, Z \rangle = 0 \]

  • (\(\Leftarrow\)): Suppose \(X - Y \perp K\). For any other element \(Z \in K\), \(Y - Z \in K\), so \((X - Y) \perp (Y - Z)\). By the Pythagorean Theorem:

    \[ \begin{align*} \|X - Z\|^2 &= \|(X - Y) + (Y - Z)\|^2\\ & = \|X - Y\|^2 + \|Y - Z\|^2 \\ &\ge \|X - Y\|^2 \end{align*} \]

    Equality holds if and only if \(\|Y - Z\|^2 = 0 \implies Z = Y\).

67.5 Properties of the Orthogonal Projection Operator

Let \(P_K: H \to K\) be the mapping that assigns to each \(X \in H\) its unique closest point in \(K\). By Theorem 67.2, this is equivalent to saying that \(X - P_K(X) \perp K\), i.e., \(\langle X - P_K(X), Y \rangle = 0\) for all \(Y \in K\).

Theorem 67.3 (Properties of \(P_K\)) Let \(K \subseteq H\) be a closed subspace of a Hilbert space \(H\). The mapping \(P_K: H \to K\) that assigns to each \(X \in H\) its unique closest point in \(K\) satisfies:

  1. Linearity: \(P_K(\alpha_1 X_1 + \alpha_2 X_2) = \alpha_1 P_K(X_1) + \alpha_2 P_K(X_2)\) for all \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2 \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(X_1, X_2 \in H\).
  2. Idempotency (Projection): \(P_K(Y) = Y\) for all \(Y \in K\).
  3. Kernel: \(P_K(Z) = 0\) for all \(Z \in K^\perp\).
  4. 1-Lipschitz Continuity: \(\|P_K(X)\| \le \|X\|\) and \(\|P_K(X) - P_K(Y)\| \le \|X - Y\|\) for all \(X, Y \in H\).
  5. Self-Adjointness: \(\langle P_K(X), Y \rangle = \langle X, P_K(Y) \rangle\) for all \(X, Y \in H\).
  6. Nested Projections: If \(L \subseteq K \subseteq H\) is another closed subspace, then: \[ P_K P_L = P_L P_K = P_L \]

Proof (Proof of Theorem 67.3).

  • Linearity: For any \(Y \in K\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} &\left\langle (\alpha_1 P_K(X_1) + \alpha_2 P_K(X_2)) - (\alpha_1 X_1 + \alpha_2 X_2), Y \right\rangle \\ &= \alpha_1 \langle P_K(X_1) - X_1, Y \rangle + \alpha_2 \langle P_K(X_2) - X_2, Y \rangle\\ & = \alpha_1 (0) + \alpha_2 (0) = 0 \end{aligned} \]

    By Theorem 67.2, \(P_K(\alpha_1 X_1 + \alpha_2 X_2) = \alpha_1 P_K(X_1) + \alpha_2 P_K(X_2)\).

  • Idempotency (Projection): For any \(Y \in K\), \(P_K(Y)\) is the unique closest point in \(K\) to \(Y\). Since \(Y \in K\), the closest point is \(Y\) itself, so \(P_K(Y) = Y\).

  • Kernel: Let \(Z\in K^\perp\). By definition, \(\langle Z,Y\rangle=0 \qquad\text{for every }Y\in K\). For any \(Y\in K\), the Pythagorean theorem gives \(\|Z-Y\|^2 = \|Z\|^2+\|Y\|^2\), because \(Z\perp Y\). Hence \(\|Z-Y\|^2\geq \|Z\|^2=\|Z-0\|^2\). Thus \(0\in K\) is the unique element of \(K\) closest to \(Z\). Since \(P_K(Z)\) is defined as this unique closest point, \(P_K(Z) = 0\).

  • 1-Lipschitz Continuity: For \(X \in H\), decompose \(X = (X - P_K(X)) + P_K(X)\) where \((X - P_K(X)) \in K^\perp\) and \(P_K(X) \in K\). By the Pythagorean Theorem:

    \[ \begin{align*} &\|X\|^2 = \|X - P_K(X)\|^2 + \|P_K(X)\|^2 \ge \|P_K(X)\|^2 \\ &\implies \|P_K(X)\| \le \|X\| \end{align*} \]

    By linearity, \(\|P_K(X) - P_K(Y)\| = \|P_K(X - Y)\| \le \|X - Y\|\).

  • Self-Adjointness: Using \(X = (X - P_K(X)) + P_K(X)\) and \(Y = (Y - P_K(Y)) + P_K(Y)\):

    \[ \begin{align*} \langle P_K(X), Y \rangle &= \langle P_K(X), P_K(Y) \rangle + \langle P_K(X), Y - P_K(Y) \rangle \\ &= \langle P_K(X), P_K(Y) \rangle \end{align*} \]

    Symmetrically, \(\langle X, P_K(Y) \rangle = \langle P_K(X), P_K(Y) \rangle\), so \(\langle P_K(X), Y \rangle = \langle X, P_K(Y) \rangle\).

  • Nested Projections: If \(L \subseteq K\), then \(P_L(X) \in L \subseteq K\). Since \(P_K\) acts as the identity on \(K\), \(P_K(P_L(X)) = P_L(X)\).

    For the reverse, \(P_L P_K = P_L\) follows from self-adjointness: for any \(X, Y \in H\),

    \[ \begin{align*} \langle P_L P_K(X), Y \rangle &= \langle P_K(X), P_L(Y) \rangle\\ & = \langle X, P_K P_L(Y) \rangle \\ &= \langle X, P_L(Y) \rangle \\ &= \langle P_L(X), Y \rangle \end{align*} \]

    Thus \(P_L P_K(X) = P_L(X)\).

67.6 Takeaways

1. Hilbert spaces generalize finite-dimensional geometry.
\(L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) is a Hilbert space with inner product \(\langle X, Y \rangle = \mathbb{E}[XY]\). The fundamental identities of Euclidean geometry—the Pythagorean Theorem and the Parallelogram Law—hold in full generality, and they are the key tools in every proof of this lecture.

2. Closedness is the infinite-dimensional replacement for finite-dimensionality.
In finite dimensions every subspace is automatically closed. In infinite-dimensional spaces one must assume \(K\) is closed; only then does \(K\) itself become a complete (hence Hilbert) space in which limit arguments can be completed.

3. Best Approximation Theorem: existence and uniqueness via the Parallelogram Law.
For any closed subspace \(K \subseteq H\) and any \(X \in H\), there is a unique \(Y \in K\) achieving \[ \|X - Y\| = d(X, K) := \inf_{Z \in K} \|X - Z\|. \] Uniqueness uses the Parallelogram Law to show two minimizers must coincide. Existence builds a Cauchy minimizing sequence from the same identity, then invokes completeness and closedness.

4. Orthogonality characterizes the best approximation.
\(Y \in K\) is the closest point to \(X\) if and only if \(X - Y \perp K\), i.e. \[ \langle X - Y, Z \rangle = 0 \quad \forall Z \in K. \] The forward direction follows by differentiating \(g(t) = \|X - (Y + tZ)\|^2\) at \(t = 0\); the backward direction is an immediate application of the Pythagorean Theorem.

5. The projection operator \(P_K\) is linear, bounded, and self-adjoint.
Assigning each \(X\) its unique closest point defines a map \(P_K : H \to K\) satisfying:

  • Idempotency: \(P_K^2 = P_K\) (projection onto its own range).
  • 1-Lipschitz: \(\|P_K(X)\| \le \|X\|\) and \(\|P_K(X) - P_K(Y)\| \le \|X - Y\|\), proved via the Pythagorean decomposition \(\|X\|^2 = \|P_K(X)\|^2 + \|X - P_K(X)\|^2\).
  • Self-adjointness: \(\langle P_K(X), Y \rangle = \langle X, P_K(Y) \rangle\), proved by expanding both sides using the orthogonal decomposition.

6. Nested projections satisfy \(P_K P_L = P_L P_K = P_L\) when \(L \subseteq K\).
\(P_K P_L = P_L\) because \(P_L(X) \in L \subseteq K\) and \(P_K\) is the identity on \(K\). The reverse order \(P_L P_K = P_L\) follows from self-adjointness: it suffices to check \(\langle P_L P_K(X), Y \rangle = \langle P_L(X), Y \rangle\) for all \(Y\), which reduces to the already-established \(P_K P_L = P_L\).
