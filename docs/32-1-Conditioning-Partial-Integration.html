69  Lecture 32.1: Conditioning as Partial Integration

In this lecture, we explore the concept of conditional expectation as a form of partial integration in the context of product probability spaces. We will see how conditioning on a sub-\(\sigma\)-field can be interpreted as integrating out certain variables while retaining measurability with respect to others.

69.1 The Averaging Property as the Primary Workhorse

In measure-theoretic probability, the Averaging Property serves as the central operational characterization of conditional expectation.

Definition 69.1 (Conditional Expectation via Averaging) Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\) be a probability space, \(\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}\) a sub-\(\sigma\)-field, and \(X \in L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\). The conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}[X]\) (or \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\)) is the unique element in \(L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{G}, P)\) satisfying: \[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}[X] Y] = \mathbb{E}[XY] \quad \forall Y \in \mathcal{B}_b(\Omega, \mathcal{G}). \] where \(\mathcal{B}_b(\Omega, \mathcal{G})\) denotes the space of bounded, \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable random variables.

69.2 Example 1.0 (Averaging Property in Action)

Let \(X \in L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\). Consider \(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}}[X]\). By definition, \(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}}[X]\) is the unique \(\mathcal{F}\)-measurable random variable satisfying: \[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}}[X] Y] = \mathbb{E}[XY] \quad \forall Y \in \mathcal{B}_b(\Omega, \mathcal{F}). \] Since \(\mathcal{B}_b(\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) contains all bounded \(\mathcal{F}\)-measurable random variables, we can take \(Y = \mathbb{1}_A\) for any \(A \in \mathcal{F}\), yielding: \[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}}[X] \mathbb{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[X \mathbb{1}_A] \quad \forall A \in \mathcal{F}. \] This is precisely the defining property of \(X\) itself, so we conclude: \[ \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}}[X] = X \quad \text{a.s.} \]

For uniqueness, if \(Z \in L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\) also satisfies the averaging property, then \(\mathbb{E}[(Z - X) Y] = 0\) , where \(Y\in \mathcal{B}_b(\Omega, \mathcal{F})\). Taking \(Y = \text{sign}(Z - X) \cdot \mathbb{1}_{\{|Z - X| \leq n\}}\) gives \[ 0=\mathbb{E}[(Z - X) Y] = \mathbb{E}[|Z - X| \cdot \mathbb{1}_{\{|Z - X| \leq n\}}] \to \mathbb{E}[|Z - X|], \] by the Dominated Convergence Theorem (DCT). Thus, \(Z = X\) a.s., proving uniqueness.

69.3 Example 1.1 (Trivial/Constant Case)

If \(X\) is already \(\mathcal{F}\)-measurable and we condition on the trivial \(\sigma\)-field \(\mathcal{G} = \{\emptyset, \Omega\}\), then any \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable function must be constant almost surely: \[ L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{G}, P) = \{ c \cdot \mathbb{1}_{\Omega} \mid c \in \mathbb{R} \}. \] Thus any \(\mathcal G\)-measurable random variable must be constant almost surely. Then, for some constant \(c\in\mathbb R\), \(\mathbb E_{\mathcal G}[X]=c\) \(a.s.\) Now take \(Y=1_{\Omega}\). The averaging property gives \[ \mathbb E[\mathbb E_{\mathcal G}[X]] = \mathbb E[X]. \] Since \(\mathbb E_{\mathcal G}[X]=c\) a.s., we have \(\mathbb E[\mathbb E_{\mathcal G}[X]] = \mathbb E[c] = c\). Therefore, \(c=\mathbb E[X]\), and hence \[ \mathbb E_{\mathcal G}[X]=\mathbb E[X] \qquad\text{a.s.} \]

So the intuition is:

  • conditioning on \(\mathcal F\): you retain all information, so \(\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal F]=X\);
  • conditioning on the trivial \(\sigma\)-field: you retain no information, so the conditional expectation must be a constant, and that constant is chosen to preserve the unconditional mean: \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\{\emptyset,\Omega\}]=\mathbb{E}[X]\).

69.4 Example 1.2 (Total Expectation Law)

Setting \(Y = \mathbb{1}_{\Omega} \in \mathcal{B}_b(\Omega, \mathcal{G})\) in the averaging property for any arbitrary sub-\(\sigma\)-field \(\mathcal{G}\) recovers the Law of Total Expectation: \[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}[X]] = \mathbb{E}[X]. \] This reflects the inner product identity \(\langle P_K(X), \mathbb{1} \rangle = \langle X, \mathbb{1} \rangle\) when \(\mathbb{1} \in K\) by self-adjointness of the orthogonal projection \(P_K\).

69.5 Testing via Indicator Functions and Dynkin’s Systems

While the definition requires testing against all bounded \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable functions \(Y \in \mathcal{B}_b(\Omega, \mathcal{G})\), it is often sufficient to test against indicator functions of sets in a generating family.

Lemma 69.1 (Indicator Characterization of Conditional Expectation) Let \(Z \in L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{G}, P)\). Then \(Z = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}[X]\) if and only if: \[ \mathbb{E}[Z \mathbb{1}_B] = \mathbb{E}[X \mathbb{1}_B] \quad \forall B \in \mathcal{G}. \]

Proof (Proof of Lemma 69.1). \((\implies)\) If \(Z = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}[X]\), taking \(Y = \mathbb{1}_B \in \mathcal{B}_b(\Omega, \mathcal{G})\) for any \(B \in \mathcal{G}\) yields \(\mathbb{E}[Z \mathbb{1}_B] = \mathbb{E}[X \mathbb{1}_B]\) directly.

\((\impliedby)\) Assume \(\mathbb{E}[Z \mathbb{1}_B] = \mathbb{E}[X \mathbb{1}_B]\) for all \(B \in \mathcal{G}\).
By linearity, \(\mathbb{E}[ZY] = \mathbb{E}[XY]\) holds for all \(\mathcal{G}\)-simple functions \(Y = \sum_{i=1}^k c_i \mathbb{1}_{B_i}\) (\(B_i \in \mathcal{G}\)).

For any bounded \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable function \(Y \in \mathcal{B}_b(\Omega, \mathcal{G})\), there exists a sequence of simple functions \(Y_n\) such that \(|Y_n| \le |Y|\) and \(Y_n \to Y\) pointwise. By Dynkin’s Multiplication Systems Theorem (or standard approximation by simple functions and the Dominated Convergence Theorem): \[ \mathbb{E}[ZY] = \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{E}[ZY_n] = \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{E}[XY_n] = \mathbb{E}[XY] \] Thus, \(Z\) satisfies the averaging property for all \(Y \in \mathcal{B}_b(\Omega, \mathcal{G})\), so \(Z = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}[X]\) a.s.

69.6 Product Probability Spaces and Partial Integration

Conditional expectation can be understood geometrically as partial integration—integrating out a subset of variables while retaining measurability with respect to the remaining ones.

69.7 Product Space Setup

Let \((\Omega_1, \mathcal{F}_1, P_1)\) and \((\Omega_2, \mathcal{F}_2, P_2)\) be two probability spaces.
Define the product probability space: \[ \Omega = \Omega_1 \times \Omega_2, \quad \mathcal{F} = \mathcal{F}_1 \otimes \mathcal{F}_2, \quad P = P_1 \otimes P_2. \] Consider the sub-\(\sigma\)-field generated by events in the first coordinate: \[ \mathcal{G} = \{ A_1 \times \Omega_2 \mid A_1 \in \mathcal{F}_1 \}. \] Notice that \(\mathcal{G}\) contains information only about \(\omega_1 \in \Omega_1\), leaving \(\omega_2 \in \Omega_2\) unobserved.

69.8 Derivation of \(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}[X]\) as Partial Integration

Let \(X \in L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\), where \(X = X(\omega_1, \omega_2)\). We seek a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable random variable \(Z(\omega_1, \omega_2) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}[X](\omega_1, \omega_2)\).

Since \(Z\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable, \(Z\) must depend only on the first component \(\omega_1\); that is, \(Z(\omega_1, \omega_2) = Z(\omega_1)\).

Using Lemma 69.1, \(Z(\omega_1)\) is uniquely determined by testing against set indicators \(B = A_1 \times \Omega_2 \in \mathcal{G}\) for arbitrary \(A_1 \in \mathcal{F}_1\): \[ \mathbb{E}[Z \mathbb{1}_{A_1 \times \Omega_2}] = \mathbb{E}[X \mathbb{1}_{A_1 \times \Omega_2}]. \]

Evaluating the left-hand side using Fubini’s Theorem: \[ \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[Z \mathbb{1}_{A_1 \times \Omega_2}] &= \int_{\Omega_1} \int_{\Omega_2} Z(\omega_1) \mathbb{1}_{A_1}(\omega_1) \, dP_2(\omega_2) dP_1(\omega_1) \\ &= \int_{A_1} Z(\omega_1) \, dP_1(\omega_1). \end{align*} \]

Evaluating the right-hand side using Fubini’s Theorem: \[ \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[X \mathbb{1}_{A_1 \times \Omega_2}] &= \int_{\Omega_1} \int_{\Omega_2} X(\omega_1, \omega_2) \mathbb{1}_{A_1}(\omega_1) \, dP_2(\omega_2) dP_1(\omega_1) \\ &= \int_{A_1} \left( \int_{\Omega_2} X(\omega_1, \omega_2) \, dP_2(\omega_2) \right) dP_1(\omega_1) \end{align*} \]

Equating the two integral representations for all \(A_1 \in \mathcal{F}_1\): \[ \int_{A_1} Z(\omega_1) \, dP_1(\omega_1) = \int_{A_1} \left( \int_{\Omega_2} X(\omega_1, \omega_2) \, dP_2(\omega_2) \right) dP_1(\omega_1) \quad \forall A_1 \in \mathcal{F}_1. \]

Since this equality holds for all \(A_1 \in \mathcal{F}_1\), the integrands must agree \(P_1\)-almost surely: \[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}[X](\omega_1, \omega_2) = Z(\omega_1) = \int_{\Omega_2} X(\omega_1, \omega_2) \, dP_2(\omega_2) \quad \text{a.s.}\]

Key takeaway: \(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}[X]\) integrates out the unobserved degrees of freedom (\(\omega_2\)) while holding the observed structure (\(\omega_1\)) fixed.

69.9 Intuitive Conceptualization: Projected On vs Conditioned On

NoteTerminology Remark

The term “conditional expectation” can sometimes obscure its underlying geometric nature. Conditioning on a \(\sigma\)-field \(\mathcal{G}\) does not mean restricting or imposing constraints on \(X\); rather, it means projecting \(X\) onto the subspace of random variables resolvable using \(\mathcal{G}\).

  1. Sub-\(\sigma\)-field size:

    • Larger sub-\(\sigma\)-field \(\mathcal{G} \implies\) Larger target subspace \(L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{G}, P) \implies\) Less information discarded, projection is closer to \(X\).
    • Smaller sub-\(\sigma\)-field \(\mathcal{G} \implies\) Smaller target subspace \(L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{G}, P) \implies\) More information coarse-grained or averaged out.

  2. Mental Substitution Rule: Whenever you read “conditioned on \(\mathcal{G}\)”, read it as “projected onto \(\mathcal{G}\)”.

  3. Partial Expectation: Because \(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}[X]\) averages out only unobserved components, a descriptive name for conditional expectation is partial expectation given \(\mathcal{G}\).

  4. Extension to Classic Theorems: Because conditional expectation acts as a partial integral, standard integration theorems and integral inequalities possess direct conditional counterparts:

    • Conditional Monotone Convergence Theorem (MCT)
    • Conditional Dominated Convergence Theorem (DCT)
    • Conditional Fatou’s Lemma
    • Conditional Jensen’s Inequality
