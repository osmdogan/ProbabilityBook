73  Lecture 33.2: Regular Conditional Distributions

In this lecture, we define regular conditional distributions (RCDs), which provide a rigorous framework for conditioning on random variables. We explore the relationship between RCDs and probability kernels, and we discuss the existence and uniqueness of RCDs in various settings.

73.1 Probability Kernels and Conditional Expectation

Probability kernels provide a powerful general framework for evaluating conditional expectations of joint functions \(\mathbb{E}[f(X, Y) \mid X]\). The following theorem shows that if the joint distribution of \((X,Y)\) can be disintegrated into a product measure \(\mu_X \otimes Q\), then the conditional expectation can be computed using the kernel \(Q\).

Theorem 73.1 (Conditional Expectation via Disintegration Kernel) Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) be a probability space, \((S_1, \mathcal{B}_1)\) and \((S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\) be measurable spaces, and \(X: (\Omega, \mathcal{F}) \to (S_1, \mathcal{B}_1)\) and \(Y: (\Omega, \mathcal{F}) \to (S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\) be random variables.

Let \(\mu_{(X,Y)} = (X, Y)^* \mathbb{P} \in \mathcal{P}(S_1 \times S_2, \mathcal{B}_1 \otimes \mathcal{B}_2)\) denote the joint pushforward distribution, and let \(\mu_X = X^* \mathbb{P} \in \mathcal{P}(S_1, \mathcal{B}_1)\) denote the marginal distribution of \(X\).

If there exists a probability kernel \(Q\) from \((S_1, \mathcal{B}_1)\) to \((S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\) such that \[ \mu_{(X,Y)} = \mu_X \otimes Q, \tag{73.1}\] then for any \(f \in L^1(S_1 \times S_2, \mathcal{B}_1 \otimes \mathcal{B}_2, \mu_{(X,Y)})\) (or \(f \ge 0\)), we have \(\mathbb{E}[f(X, Y) \mid X] = g(X)\) a.s., where \[ g(x) = \int_{S_2} f(x, y) \, Q(x, dy). \tag{73.2}\] In short, \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X, Y) \mid X = x] = \int_{S_2} f(x, y) \, Q(x, dy). \]

Remark 73.1 (Pushforward Distribution). Note that \(\mu_X = X^* \mathbb{P} \in \mathcal{P}(S_1, \mathcal{B}_1)\) is the pushforward distribution of \(X\): \[ \mu_X(A) = \mathbb{P}(X \in A) = \mathbb{P}(X^{-1}(A)) \quad \text{for all }A \in \mathcal{B}_1. \] Similarly, \(\mu_{(X,Y)} = (X,Y)^* \mathbb{P} \in \mathcal{P}(S_1 \times S_2, \mathcal{B}_1 \otimes \mathcal{B}_2)\) is the pushforward distribution of the pair \((X,Y)\): \[ \mu_{(X,Y)}(A \times B) = \mathbb{P}(X \in A, Y \in B) = \mathbb{P}((X,Y)^{-1}(A \times B)), \] for all \(A \in \mathcal{B}_1, B \in \mathcal{B}_2\).

Proof (Proof of Theorem 73.1). By the characterization of conditional expectation, \(g(X) = \mathbb{E}[f(X,Y) \mid X]\) a.s. if and only if:

  1. \(g(X)\) is \(\sigma(X)\)-measurable: this holds automatically because \(g\) is \(\mathcal{B}_1\)-measurable (by Lemma 72.1 applied to \(Q\)), and any measurable function of \(X\) is \(\sigma(X)\)-measurable.

  2. For all bounded test functions \(h \in \mathcal{B}_b(S_1, \mathcal{B}_1)\): \[ \mathbb{E}\big[ f(X, Y) h(X) \big] = \mathbb{E}\big[ g(X) h(X) \big]. \]

It therefore suffices to verify condition (ii).

Case 1: Bounded Measurable Functions:

Assume \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S_1 \times S_2, \mathcal{B}_1 \otimes \mathcal{B}_2)\). By Change of Variables and the disintegration assumption \(\mu_{(X,Y)} = \mu_X \otimes Q\): \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\big[ f(X, Y) h(X) \big] &= \int_{S_1 \times S_2} f(x, y) h(x) \, \mu_{(X,Y)}(dx \, dy) \\ &= \int_{S_1 \times S_2} f(x, y) h(x) \, (\mu_X \otimes Q)(dx \, dy) \\ &\stackrel{\text{Fubini}}{=} \int_{S_1} \left( \int_{S_2} f(x, y) \, Q(x, dy) \right) h(x) \, \mu_X(dx) \\ &= \int_{S_1} g(x) h(x) \, \mu_X(dx) \\ &= \mathbb{E}\big[ g(X) h(X) \big]. \end{aligned} \]

Case 2: Integrable Functions (\(f \in L^1\)):

If \(f \in L^1(\mu_{(X,Y)})\), define \(f_n = (f \wedge n) \vee (-n)\). Each \(f_n\) is bounded, so by Case 1, \(g_n(X) = \mathbb{E}[f_n(X,Y) \mid X]\) a.s., where \(g_n(x) = \int_{S_2} f_n(x, y) \, Q(x, dy)\).

As \(n \to \infty\), \(f_n \to f\) in \(L^1(\mathbb{P})\). By \(L^1\)-contractivity of conditional expectation, \(g_n(X) \to \mathbb{E}[f(X,Y) \mid X]\) in \(L^1(\mathbb{P})\). Concurrently: \[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}\left[ \left| g_n(X) - g(X) \right| \right] \\ &= \int_{S_1} \left| \int_{S_2} f_n(x, y) \, Q(x, dy) - \int_{S_2} f(x, y) \, Q(x, dy) \right| \mu_X(dx) \\ &\le \int_{S_1} \left( \int_{S_2} |f_n(x, y) - f(x, y)| \, Q(x, dy) \right) \mu_X(dx) \\ &= \int_{S_1 \times S_2} |f_n(x, y) - f(x, y)| \, (\mu_X \otimes Q)(dx \, dy) \\ &= \|f_n - f\|_{L^1(\mu_{(X,Y)})} \longrightarrow 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty. \end{aligned} \] Thus \(g(X) = \mathbb{E}[f(X,Y) \mid X]\) a.s.

Example 73.1 Suppose \(X\sim N(0,1)\) and, conditional on \(X=x\), \(Y\mid X=x\sim N(x,1)\). Thus, we can write \(Y=X+\varepsilon\), where \(\varepsilon\sim N(0,1)\) is independent of \(X\). The conditional distribution of \(Y\) given \(X=x\) is represented by the kernel \[ Q(x,dy)=\phi(y-x)\,dy, \] where \(\phi\) is the standard normal density. Now consider the joint function \(f(x,y)=xy\). We want to calculate \(\mathbb E[XY\mid X]\). According to the theorem, \[ \mathbb E[XY\mid X=x] = \int_{\mathbb R}xy\,Q(x,dy). \] Since \(x\) is fixed inside the conditional expectation, \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[XY\mid X=x] &=x\int_{\mathbb R}y\,Q(x,dy)\\ &=x\,\mathbb E[Y\mid X=x]. \end{aligned} \] But under the kernel \(Q(x,\cdot)\), \(Y\mid X=x\sim N(x,1)\), so \(\mathbb E[Y\mid X=x]=x\). Therefore, \(\mathbb E[XY\mid X=x]=x^2\), and hence \(\mathbb E[XY\mid X]=X^2\).

The important point is that we do not need to work with the joint density of \((X,Y)\). We only need to know: \[ \boxed{Q(x,\cdot)=\text{the distribution of }Y\text{ when }X=x.} \] Once we know this conditional distribution, the theorem tells us exactly how to calculate conditional expectations: \[ \boxed{ \mathbb E[f(X,Y)\mid X] = \int f(X,y)\,Q(X,dy). } \] For example, with \(f(x,y)=y^2\), we immediately obtain \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[Y^2\mid X=x] &=\int y^2 Q(x,dy)\\ &=x^2+1, \end{aligned} \] because \(Y\mid X=x\sim N(x,1)\). Hence \(\mathbb E[Y^2\mid X]=X^2+1\). So the kernel provides a recipe: fix the observed value of \(X\), use the corresponding distribution \(Q(x,\cdot)\) for \(Y\), and average the function \(f(x,Y)\) with respect to that distribution.

73.2 Regular Conditional Distributions (RCD)

This raises a crucial question: Can we always find a probability kernel \(Q\) such that \(\mu_{(X,Y)} = \mu_X \otimes Q\)? Note that if such a kernel exists and \((S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\) is countably generated, it is unique \(\mu_X\)-almost surely. To that end, we define the regular conditional distribution (RCD) of \(Y\) given \(X\) as a probability kernel \(Q\) satisfying the conditional expectation property in Theorem 73.1.

Definition 73.1 (Regular Conditional Distribution) Let \(X: (\Omega, \mathcal{F}) \to (S_1, \mathcal{B}_1)\) and \(Y: (\Omega, \mathcal{F}) \to (S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\) be random variables. A probability kernel \(Q\) from \((S_1, \mathcal{B}_1)\) to \((S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\) is called a regular conditional distribution (RCD) of \(Y\) given \(X\) if for every \(B \in \mathcal{B}_2\): \[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}_{\{Y\in B\}} \mid \sigma(X)] = \mathbb{P}(Y \in B \mid X) = Q(X, B) \quad \text{a.s.} \tag{73.3}\] Equivalently, for every bounded measurable function \(h \in \mathcal{B}_b(S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\): \[ \mathbb{E}[h(Y) \mid X] = \int_{S_2} h(y) \, Q(X, dy) \quad \text{a.s.} \tag{73.4}\]

The following result shows that \(Q\) is an RCD \(\implies\) \(\mu_{(X,Y)} = \mu_X \otimes Q\). That is, an RCD is equivalent to a disintegration of the joint distribution.

Proposition 73.1 (Equivalence of RCD and Product Disintegration) If \(Q\) is a regular conditional distribution of \(Y\) given \(X\), then \(\mu_{(X,Y)} = \mu_X \otimes Q\). Consequently, \(Q\) is unique \(\mu_X\)-almost surely if \(\mathcal{B}_2\) is countably generated.

Proof. To show \(\mu_{(X,Y)} = \mu_X \otimes Q\), test on measurable rectangles \(A \times B \in \mathcal{B}_1 \otimes \mathcal{B}_2\): \[ \begin{aligned} \mu_{(X,Y)}(A \times B) &= \mathbb{P}(X \in A, Y \in B) \\ &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{I}_A(X) \mathbb{I}_B(Y) \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{I}_A(X) \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}_B(Y) \mid X] \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{I}_A(X) Q(X, B) \right] \quad \\ &= \int_A Q(x, B) \, \mu_X(dx) \\ &= (\mu_X \otimes Q)(A \times B). \end{aligned} \] where the third equality uses the averaging property of conditional expectation, the fourth equality uses the RCD definition in Equation 79.1, and the last equality uses the definition of the product measure in Definition 72.2. Since measures agreeing on generating rectangles agree on \(\mathcal{B}_1 \otimes \mathcal{B}_2\), we have \(\mu_{(X,Y)} = \mu_X \otimes Q\). Uniqueness \(\mu_X\)-a.s. follows directly from the kernel uniqueness theorem.

Remark 73.2 (Heuristic: The Product Rule “Jazzed Up”). To understand why an RCD extends from single-variable functions \(h(Y)\) to joint functions \(f(X,Y)\), consider \(f(x,y) = g(x) h(y)\): \[ \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[f(X, Y) \mid X] &= \mathbb{E}[g(X) h(Y) \mid X] \\ &= g(X) \mathbb{E}[h(Y) \mid X] \\ &= g(X) \int_{S_2} h(y) \, Q(X, dy) \\ &= \int_{S_2} f(X, y) \, Q(X, dy). \end{align*} \] The formal proposition establishes that this identity extends linearly and continuously to all joint integrable functions \(f(X, Y)\).

The reverse implication in Proposition 73.1 is also true: \(Q\) is an RCD of \(Y|X\) \(\iff\) \(\mu_{(X,Y)} = \mu_X \otimes Q\).

73.3 Existence of Regular Conditional Distributions

Does an RCD always exist for arbitrary measurable spaces? The answer is No! However, an RCD is guaranteed to exist whenever the target space \((S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\) is “nice enough”—specifically, a Standard Borel Space (a space measure-isomorphic to a Borel subset of a Polish space). Below are two standard explicit constructions.

73.4 Case 1: Absolutely Continuous Distributions (Radon-Nikodym Construction)

Example 73.2 (RCD for Absolutely Continuous Measures) Let \((S_1, \mathcal{B}_1, \nu_1)\) and \((S_2, \mathcal{B}_2, \nu_2)\) be \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces. Let \((X, Y)\) have joint distribution \(\mu_{(X,Y)} \ll \nu_1 \otimes \nu_2\) with joint density \(f_{X,Y} = \frac{d\mu_{(X,Y)}}{d(\nu_1 \otimes \nu_2)}\).

Define the marginal density \(f_X(x) = \int_{S_2} f_{X,Y}(x, y) \, \nu_2(dy)\) so that \(d\mu_X = f_X \, d\nu_1\). Define the conditional density: \[ f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) := \begin{cases} \dfrac{f_{X,Y}(x, y)}{f_X(x)} & \text{if } f_X(x) > 0, \\[8pt] f_0(y) & \text{if } f_X(x) = 0, \end{cases} \] where \(f_0(y)\) is any arbitrary probability density on \((S_2, \mathcal{B}_2, \nu_2)\).

Then the mapping \[ Q(x, B) := \int_B f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \, \nu_2(dy) \tag{73.5}\] is a Regular Conditional Distribution of \(Y\) given \(X\).

Proof. We verify \(\mu_{(X,Y)} = \mu_X \otimes Q\) by checking on measurable rectangles \(A \times B \in \mathcal{B}_1 \otimes \mathcal{B}_2\) (which generate \(\mathcal{B}_1 \otimes \mathcal{B}_2\), so equality on rectangles implies equality on the whole product \(\sigma\)-algebra by Dynkin’s theorem): \[ \begin{aligned} (\mu_X \otimes Q)(A \times B) &= \int_A Q(x, B) \, \mu_X(dx) \\ &= \int_A Q(x, B) \, f_X(x) \, \nu_1(dx) \\ &= \int_A \left( \int_B f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \, \nu_2(dy) \right) f_X(x) \, \nu_1(dx). \end{aligned} \] On the set \(\{x : f_X(x) > 0\}\), substituting \(f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_X(x)}\) gives \(f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \cdot f_X(x) = f_{X,Y}(x,y)\). On the set \(\{x : f_X(x) = 0\}\), \(f_X(x) = \int_{S_2} f_{X,Y}(x,y) \, \nu_2(dy) = 0\) implies \(f_{X,Y}(x,y) = 0\) for \(\nu_2\)-a.e. \(y\), so both sides contribute zero. Hence: \[ (\mu_X \otimes Q)(A \times B) = \int_A \int_B f_{X,Y}(x,y) \, \nu_2(dy) \, \nu_1(dx) = \mu_{(X,Y)}(A \times B), \] where the last equality uses the Fubini–Tonelli theorem (since \(f_{X,Y} \ge 0\)) and the fact that \(f_{X,Y}\) is the joint density of \((X,Y)\). Thus \(\mu_{(X,Y)} = \mu_X \otimes Q\), and by Proposition 73.1, \(Q\) is an RCD of \(Y\) given \(X\).

73.5 Case 2: Discrete Distributions

Example 73.3 (RCD for Discrete Random Variables) Let \(Y\) be a discrete random variable taking values in a countable set \(S_2 = \{y_1, y_2, \dots\} \subset \mathbb{R}\). For \(x \in S_1\) where \(\mathbb{P}(X = x) > 0\), define the point probabilities: \[ Q(x, \{y\}) = \mathbb{P}(Y = y \mid X = x) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(X = x, Y = y)}{\mathbb{P}(X = x)}. \] Then for any \(B \in \mathcal{B}_2\): \[ Q(x, B) = \sum_{y \in B \cap S_2} \mathbb{P}(Y = y \mid X = x). \] For \(x\) where \(\mathbb{P}(X = x) = 0\), set \(Q(x, \cdot)\) to be any fixed probability measure on \((S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\). Evaluating this kernel at \(X\) yields: \[ \mathbb{P}(Y \in B \mid X) = Q(X, B) = \sum_{y \in B \cap S_2} Q(X, \{y\}) \quad \text{a.s.} \]

Proof. We verify \(Q\) is an RCD by checking \(\mathbb{P}(Y \in B \mid X) = Q(X, B)\) a.s. for each \(B \in \mathcal{B}_2\). Since \(Y\) takes values in the countable set \(S_2\), we have \(\mathbb{P}(X = x) > 0\) for at most countably many \(x\). For any such \(x\): \[ \begin{align*} Q(x, B) &= \sum_{y \in B \cap S_2} Q(x, \{y\})\\ &= \sum_{y \in B \cap S_2} \frac{\mathbb{P}(X = x,\, Y = y)}{\mathbb{P}(X = x)}\\ &= \frac{\mathbb{P}(X = x,\, Y \in B)}{\mathbb{P}(X = x)}\\ &= \mathbb{P}(Y \in B \mid X = x), \end{align*} \] where the third equality uses countable additivity (the events \(\{Y = y\}\) for \(y \in B \cap S_2\) are pairwise disjoint). For \(x\) with \(\mathbb{P}(X = x) = 0\), the value of \(Q(x, \cdot)\) does not affect any a.s. statement since \(\mathbb{P}(X = x) = 0\). Hence \(Q(X, B) = \mathbb{P}(Y \in B \mid X)\) a.s., so \(Q\) is an RCD of \(Y\) given \(X\).

NoteProcedural Order in Discrete Conditioning

When evaluating discrete RCD expressions in practice:

  1. First compute the two-variable deterministic function \(Q(x, \{y\}) = \mathbb{P}(Y = y \mid X = x)\) on \(\{x: \mathbb{P}(X=x) > 0\}\).
  2. Then evaluate \(Q(X, \{y\})\) at the random variable \(X\).
  3. Sum over \(y \in B \cap S_2\) to obtain \(\mathbb{P}(Y \in B \mid X) = \sum_{y \in B \cap S_2} Q(X, \{y\})\) a.s.

73.6 Takeaways

TipKey Takeaways

1. RCDs Are the Rigorous Version of “Conditioning on \(X = x\).” A regular conditional distribution (RCD) of \(Y\) given \(X\) is a probability kernel \(Q: S_1 \times \mathcal{B}_2 \to [0,1]\) such that \(\mathbb{P}(Y \in B \mid X) = Q(X, B)\) a.s. for every \(B \in \mathcal{B}_2\). Unlike the naive conditional probability \(\mathbb{P}(Y \in B \mid X = x)\), which requires \(\mathbb{P}(X = x) > 0\), the kernel \(Q(x, \cdot)\) is defined simultaneously for all \(x\) with a single measurability condition, making it well-suited for continuous random variables.

2. RCD \(\Longleftrightarrow\) Disintegration of the Joint Distribution. A kernel \(Q\) is an RCD of \(Y\) given \(X\) if and only if \(\mu_{(X,Y)} = \mu_X \otimes Q\) (the semi-direct product measure from Lecture 33.1). The forward direction follows by testing on rectangles using the tower property; the reverse follows from the generalized Fubini theorem. The two conditions are therefore interchangeable.

3. Computing Conditional Expectations from the RCD. When \(\mu_{(X,Y)} = \mu_X \otimes Q\), the conditional expectation of any jointly measurable \(f\) is given by \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X, Y) \mid X = x] = \int_{S_2} f(x, y) \, Q(x, dy), \] for any \(f\) that is bounded or \(L^1(\mu_{(X,Y)})\). The proof verifies the two conditions for conditional expectation: (i) \(\sigma(X)\)-measurability (automatic since \(g(x) = \int f(x,y) Q(x,dy)\) is \(\mathcal{B}_1\)-measurable), and (ii) the integral characterization \(\mathbb{E}[g(X)h(X)] = \mathbb{E}[f(X,Y)h(X)]\) for all bounded \(h\), which follows from Fubini applied to \(\mu_X \otimes Q\). The \(L^1\) case is obtained by truncation and \(L^1\)-contractivity of conditional expectation.

4. Explicit Constructions of RCDs.

  • Density case: If \((X,Y)\) has joint density \(f_{X,Y}\) with respect to \(\nu_1 \otimes \nu_2\), set \(Q(x, B) = \int_B f_{Y|X}(y|x) \nu_2(dy)\). This is an RCD because \((\mu_X \otimes Q)(A \times B) = \int_A \int_B f_{X,Y}(x,y) \nu_2(dy) \nu_1(dx) = \mu_{(X,Y)}(A \times B)\) by Fubini, where the key cancellation is \(f_{Y|X}(y|x) \cdot f_X(x) = f_{X,Y}(x,y)\) on \(\{f_X > 0\}\).
  • Discrete case: If \(Y\) takes values in a countable set \(S_2\), set \(Q(x, \{y\}) = \mathbb{P}(Y = y \mid X = x)\) for \(\mathbb{P}(X = x) > 0\). Verification uses the countable additivity of conditional probability: \(Q(x, B) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(X = x, Y \in B)}{\mathbb{P}(X = x)} = \mathbb{P}(Y \in B \mid X = x)\).

5. Existence and Uniqueness. An RCD of \(Y\) given \(X\) need not exist for arbitrary measurable spaces, but is guaranteed to exist whenever \((S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\) is a Standard Borel space (measure-isomorphic to a Borel subset of a Polish space). When it exists and \(\mathcal{B}_2\) is countably generated, the RCD is unique \(\mu_X\)-almost surely: if \(Q\) and \(\widetilde{Q}\) are both RCDs, then \(\mu_X \otimes Q = \mu_X \otimes \widetilde{Q}\), and the uniqueness theorem from Lecture 33.1 gives \(Q(x, \cdot) = \widetilde{Q}(x, \cdot)\) for \(\mu_X\)-a.e. \(x\).
