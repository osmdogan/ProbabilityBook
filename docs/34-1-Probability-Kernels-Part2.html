74.1 Probability Kernels and Bounded Measurable Functions

Let \((S_1, \mathcal{B}_1)\) and \((S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\) be measurable spaces, and let \(Q: S_1 \times \mathcal{B}_2 \to [0, 1]\) be a probability kernel. Recall that

  • For each fixed \(x \in S_1\), \(Q(x, \cdot)\) is a probability measure on \((S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\).
  • For each fixed \(B \in \mathcal{B}_2\), \(Q(\cdot, B)\) is a \(\mathcal{B}_1\)-measurable function on \(S_1\).
  • We can integrate bounded measurable functions against probability kernels: \[ \int f(y) \, Q(x, dy), \] where \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\) is a bounded \(\mathcal{B}_2\)-measurable function. This integral is well-defined for each fixed \(x \in S_1\) because \(Q(x, \cdot)\) is a probability measure. The resulting function of \(x \in S_1\) is \(\mathcal{B}_1\)-measurable and \[ \left|\int f(y) \, Q(x, dy)\right|\le \int |f(y)| \, Q(x, dy) \le \sup_{y \in S_2} |f(y)| = \|f\|_\infty. \]

Given any bounded, \(\mathcal{B}_2\)-measurable function \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\), we can integrate \(f\) against \(Q(x, \cdot)\) for each fixed \(x \in S_1\): \[ (L_Q f)(x) := \int_{S_2} f(y) \, Q(x, dy) \quad \forall x \in S_1. \] The resulting function \(L_Q f: S_1 \to \mathbb{R}\) is a new \(\mathcal{B}_1\)-measurable function of \(x \in S_1\). Furthermore, this operation preserves boundedness: \[ \begin{align*} |(L_Q f)(x)| &= \left| \int_{S_2} f(y) \, Q(x, dy) \right| \\ &\le \int_{S_2} |f(y)| \, Q(x, dy) \\ &\le \sup_{y \in S_2} |f(y)| \\ &= \|f\|_\infty \quad \forall x \in S_1. \end{align*} \]

Definition 74.1 (Markov Operator) Equip the space of bounded measurable functions \(\mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\) with the supremum norm: \[ \|f\|_\infty := \sup_{x \in S} |f(x)|. \] For a probability kernel \(Q\) from \((S_1, \mathcal{B}_1)\) to \((S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\), the linear mapping \[ L_Q: \mathcal{B}_b(S_2, \mathcal{B}_2) \to \mathcal{B}_b(S_1, \mathcal{B}_1), \quad f \mapsto L_Q f \] defined by \((L_Q f)(x) = \int_{S_2} f(y) \, Q(x, dy)\) is called the Markov operator (or transition operator) associated with \(Q\). It satisfies \(\|L_Q f\|_\infty \le \|f\|_\infty\).

Example 74.1 Let \(S_1=S_2=\mathbb R\) and define the probability kernel \[ Q(x,dy)=\phi(y-x)\,dy, \] where \(\phi\) is the standard normal density. Thus, \(Q(x,\cdot)=N(x,1)\). In probabilistic language, \(Y\mid X=x\sim N(x,1)\). The associated Markov operator is \[ (L_Qf)(x) = \int_{\mathbb R}f(y)\phi(y-x)\,dy. \] So the Markov operator takes a function \(f\) and returns its conditional expectation under the transition distribution \(Q(x,\cdot)\).

Now take \(f(y)=y^2\). Since \(Y\mid X=x\sim N(x,1)\), \[ \begin{aligned} (L_Qf)(x) &=\int_{\mathbb R}y^2\phi(y-x)\,dy\\ &=\mathbb E[Y^2\mid X=x]\\ &=x^2+1. \end{aligned} \] Thus, \(L_Q(y^2)(x)=x^2+1\). This is a nice illustration of why \(L_Q\) is closely connected to conditional expectation.

As another example, consider an indicator function: \(f(y)=\mathbf 1_{\{y\leq a\}}\). Then \[ \begin{aligned} (L_Qf)(x) &= \int_{\mathbb R} \mathbf 1_{\{y\leq a\}} \phi(y-x)\,dy\\ &= \int_{-\infty}^a\phi(y-x)\,dy\\ &= \Phi(a-x). \end{aligned} \] Hence \(L_Q\mathbf 1_{(-\infty,a]}(x)=\Phi(a-x)\). But this has an immediate probabilistic interpretation: \(L_Q\mathbf 1_{\{y\leq a\}}(x) = P(Y\leq a\mid X=x)\). So the Markov operator turns an event indicator into its conditional probability.

74.2 Axiomatic Properties of Markov Operators

In the following proposition, we summarize the structural properties of the Markov operator \(L_Q\) associated with a probability kernel \(Q\).

Proposition 74.1 (Properties of the Associated Markov Operator) Let \(Q\) be a probability kernel from \((S_1, \mathcal{B}_1)\) to \((S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\). The associated Markov operator \(L_Q: \mathcal{B}_b(S_2, \mathcal{B}_2) \to \mathcal{B}_b(S_1, \mathcal{B}_1)\) satisfies the following structural properties:

  1. Linearity: \(L_Q(\alpha f + \beta g) = \alpha L_Q(f) + \beta L_Q(g)\) for all \(\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(f, g \in \mathcal{B}_b(S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\).
  2. Preservation of Constants (Normalization): \(L_Q(\mathbb{I}_{S_2}) = \mathbb{I}_{S_1}\).
  3. Positivity (Monotonicity): If \(f \ge 0\), then \(L_Q(f) \ge 0\). Consequently, if \(f \le g\), then \(L_Q(f) \le L_Q(g)\).
  4. Boundedly Monotone / Pointwise Continuity: If \(f_n \to f\) boundedly (i.e., \(f_n(y) \to f(y)\) pointwise and \(\sup_n \|f_n\|_\infty < \infty\)), then \(L_Q f_n \to L_Q f\) boundedly on \(S_1\).

Proof (Proof of Proposition 74.1).

  1. Linearity: Follows directly from the linearity of integration with respect to the integrand: \[ \begin{aligned} (L_Q(\alpha f + \beta g))(x) &= \int_{S_2} (\alpha f(y) + \beta g(y)) \, Q(x, dy) \\ &= \alpha (L_Q f)(x) + \beta (L_Q g)(x). \end{aligned} \]
  2. Normalization: Since \(Q(x, \cdot)\) is a probability measure for each \(x \in S_1\): \[ (L_Q \mathbb{I}_{S_2})(x) = \int_{S_2} 1 \, Q(x, dy) = Q(x, S_2) = 1 = \mathbb{I}_{S_1}(x) \quad \forall x \in S_1. \]
  3. Positivity: If \(f(y) \ge 0\) for all \(y \in S_2\), integrating a non-negative function against a non-negative probability measure yields \((L_Q f)(x) = \int_{S_2} f(y) \, Q(x, dy) \ge 0\).
  4. Boundedly Monotone Continuity: Suppose \(f_n \to f\) pointwise with \(\|f_n\|_\infty \le M < \infty\) for all \(n \in \mathbb{N}\). For each fixed \(x \in S_1\), we have \(|f_n(y)| \le M\) for all \(y \in S_2\), and therefore \[ \int_{S_2} M \, Q(x, dy) = M \, Q(x, S_2) = M < \infty. \] Hence the constant function \(M\) is integrable with respect to the probability measure \(Q(x,\cdot)\), so the Dominated Convergence Theorem applies and yields \[ (L_Q f_n)(x) = \int_{S_2} f_n(y) \, Q(x, dy) \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} \int_{S_2} f(y) \, Q(x, dy) = (L_Q f)(x). \] Since this holds for every \(x \in S_1\), the convergence is pointwise on \(S_1\). Moreover, \[ \|L_Q f_n\|_\infty \le \|f_n\|_\infty \le M, \] so the convergence \(L_Q f_n \to L_Q f\) is bounded.

Remark 74.1 (Implication of Positivity). If a linear operator \(L: \mathcal{B}_b(S_2, \mathcal{B}_2) \to \mathcal{B}_b(S_1, \mathcal{B}_1)\) satisfies positivity (\(f \ge 0 \implies L f \ge 0\)) and normalization (\(L \mathbb{I} = \mathbb{I}\)), then for any \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\): \[ -|f| \le f \le |f| \implies -L(|f|) \le L f \le L(|f|) \implies |L f| \le L(|f|). \] Because \(|f| \le \|f\|_\infty \mathbb{I}_{S_2}\), monotonicity implies: \[ |L f| \le L(|f|) \le L(\|f\|_\infty \mathbb{I}_{S_2}) = \|f\|_\infty L(\mathbb{I}_{S_2}) = \|f\|_\infty \mathbb{I}_{S_1}. \] Hence \(\|L f\|_\infty \le \|f\|_\infty\) for every \(f\), so \(\|L\|_\infty \le 1\). Since \(L\mathbb{I}_{S_2} = \mathbb{I}_{S_1}\), we also have \(\|L\|_\infty = 1\). Thus every positive normalized linear operator is automatically a contraction.

74.3 Duality: Bijection Between Probability Kernels and Markov Operators

In the next proposition, we show that the mapping \(Q \mapsto L_Q\) is a bijection between probability kernels and Markov operators. This is a duality result: every probability kernel defines a Markov operator, and conversely, every Markov operator arises from a unique probability kernel.

Proposition 74.2 (Representation Theorem for Markov Operators) Let \((S, \mathcal{B})\) be a measurable space. Let \(L: \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B}) \to \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\) be an operator satisfying:

  1. \(L\) is linear.
  2. \(L(\mathbb{I}_S) = \mathbb{I}_S\).
  3. \(L f \ge 0\) whenever \(f \ge 0\).
  4. If \(f_n \to f\) boundedly in \(\mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\), then \(L f_n \to L f\) boundedly in \(\mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\).

Then there exists a unique probability kernel \(Q\) over \((S, \mathcal{B})\) such that \(L = L_Q\). Explicitly, \(Q\) is given by: \[ Q(x, B) := (L \mathbb{I}_B)(x) \quad \forall x \in S, \; B \in \mathcal{B}. \]

Proof (Proof of Proposition 74.2). We construct \(Q(x, B) := (L \mathbb{I}_B)(x)\) and check the defining properties of a probability kernel:

  1. Measurability: For every fixed \(B \in \mathcal{B}\), \(\mathbb{I}_B \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\). By definition of the domain/codomain of \(L\), \(Q(\cdot, B) = L(\mathbb{I}_B)(\cdot) \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\), so \(x \mapsto Q(x, B)\) is \(\mathcal{B}\)-measurable.
  2. Probability Measure Properties for \(Q(x, \cdot)\):
    • Normalization: \(Q(x, S) = (L \mathbb{I}_S)(x) = 1\) by Property 1.
    • Non-negativity: For any \(B \in \mathcal{B}\), \(\mathbb{I}_B \ge 0 \implies Q(x, B) = (L \mathbb{I}_B)(x) \ge 0\) by Property 2.
    • Countable Additivity: Let \((B_k)_{k=1}^\infty \subseteq \mathcal{B}\) be pairwise disjoint sets, and let \(B = \bigcup_{k=1}^\infty B_k\). Define \(f_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \mathbb{I}_{B_k} = \mathbb{I}_{\bigcup_{k=1}^n B_k}\). Then \(f_n \uparrow \mathbb{I}_B\) boundedly as \(n \to \infty\). By Property 3 (boundedly monotone continuity) and linearity of \(L\): \[ \begin{align*} Q(x, B) &= (L \mathbb{I}_B)(x) = \lim_{n \to \infty} (L f_n)(x)\\ & = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^n (L \mathbb{I}_{B_k})(x) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty Q(x, B_k) \quad \forall x \in S. \end{align*} \] Thus \(Q(x, \cdot)\) is a probability measure on \((S, \mathcal{B})\) for every fixed \(x \in S\).
  3. Verification of \(L = L_Q\): We need to show \(L f = L_Q f = \int_S f(y) \, Q(\cdot, dy)\) for all \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\).
    • For indicator functions \(f = \mathbb{I}_B\): \(\int_S \mathbb{I}_B(y) \, Q(x, dy) = Q(x, B) = (L \mathbb{I}_B)(x)\).
    • By linearity, \(L f = L_Q f\) for all simple functions \(f = \sum_{i=1}^m c_i \mathbb{I}_{B_i}\).
    • For an arbitrary bounded measurable \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\), there exists a sequence of simple functions \(f_n\) such that \(f_n \to f\) boundedly. Applying Property 3 to both \(L\) and \(L_Q\) yields \(L f = \lim_{n \to \infty} L f_n = \lim_{n \to \infty} L_Q f_n = L_Q f\).

Thus, the mapping \(Q \mapsto L_Q\) defines a bijection between probability kernels and Markov operators: \[ \begin{align*} &Q \mapsto L(f) = \int_S f(y) \, Q(\cdot, dy).\\ & L \mapsto Q(\cdot, B) = (L \mathbb{I}_B)(\cdot). \end{align*} \]

74.4 Composition of Probability Kernels

The algebraic correspondence between probability kernels and linear operators allows us to define the product or composition of probability kernels via operator composition.

Corollary 74.1 (Kernel Composition via Operator Composition) Let \(Q_1\) be a probability kernel from \((S_1, \mathcal{B}_1)\) to \((S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\), and let \(Q_2\) be a probability kernel from \((S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\) to \((S_3, \mathcal{B}_3)\). Then there exists a unique probability kernel \(Q\) from \((S_1, \mathcal{B}_1)\) to \((S_3, \mathcal{B}_3)\) such that its associated Markov operator equals the composition of the individual operators: \[ L_Q = L_{Q_1} \circ L_{Q_2}. \] We write \(Q = Q_1 Q_2\) or \(Q = Q_1 \cdot Q_2\). In the special case \(S_1=S_2=S_3=S\), this is the composition of kernels on the same space.

Proof (Proof of Corollary 74.1). Consider the operator \(L := L_{Q_1} \circ L_{Q_2}: \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B}) \to \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\). We verify that \(L\) satisfies properties 0–3 of Proposition 74.2:

  1. Linearity: Composition of linear operators is linear.
  2. Normalization: \((L \mathbb{I}_S) = L_{Q_1}(L_{Q_2} \mathbb{I}_S) = L_{Q_1}(\mathbb{I}_S) = \mathbb{I}_S\).
  3. Positivity: If \(f \ge 0\), then \(g := L_{Q_2}(f) \ge 0\) by positivity of \(L_{Q_2}\). Then \(L f = L_{Q_1}(g) \ge 0\) by positivity of \(L_{Q_1}\).
  4. Boundedly Monotone Continuity: If \(f_n \to f\) boundedly, then \(g_n := L_{Q_2}(f_n) \to g := L_{Q_2}(f)\) boundedly by property 3 of \(L_{Q_2}\). Subsequently, \(L_{Q_1}(g_n) \to L_{Q_1}(g)\) boundedly by property 3 of \(L_{Q_1}\).

By Proposition 74.2, there exists a unique probability kernel \(Q\) such that \(L_Q = L_{Q_1} \circ L_{Q_2}\).

Remark 74.2 (Explicit Integral Formula for Kernel Composition). The kernel corresponding to the composed Markov operator \[ L_{Q_1Q_2}=L_{Q_1}\circ L_{Q_2} \] can be obtained by evaluating the operator at the indicator function \(\mathbb{I}_B\). For \(B\in\mathcal{B}_3\), \[ \begin{aligned} (Q_1Q_2)(x,B) &= (L_{Q_1Q_2}\mathbb{I}_B)(x)\\ &= (L_{Q_1}(L_{Q_2}\mathbb{I}_B))(x)\\ &= \int_{S_2} Q_2(y,B)\,Q_1(x,dy). \end{aligned} \] Thus, the composition of two kernels is given explicitly by \[ (Q_1Q_2)(x,B) =\int_{S_2} Q_2(y,B)\,Q_1(x,dy). \] More generally, for any \(f\in\mathcal{B}_b(S_3,\mathcal{B}_3)\), \[ \begin{aligned} (L_{Q_1Q_2}f)(x) &= (L_{Q_1}(L_{Q_2}f))(x)\\ &= \int_{S_2} \left( \int_{S_3} f(z)\,Q_2(y,dz) \right) Q_1(x,dy). \end{aligned} \] Equivalently, this can be written as the iterated integral \[ (L_{Q_1Q_2}f)(x) = \int_{S_2\times S_3} f(z)\,Q_1(x,dy)\,Q_2(y,dz). \] Thus, kernel composition corresponds to two successive transitions: \[ x \xrightarrow{Q_1} y \xrightarrow{Q_2} z. \]

Example 74.2 (Matrix Multiplication Analogy for Discrete Spaces) When \(S\) is countable and \(\mathcal{B} = \mathcal{P}(S)\), a probability kernel is fully specified by a stochastic matrix \((Q(x, y))_{x, y \in S}\) where \(Q(x, y) = Q(x, \{y\})\).

Evaluating the kernel composition formula (from Remark 74.2) at \(B = \{z\}\) gives the \((x,z)\) entry of the composed kernel: \[ Q(x, z) := Q(x, \{z\}) = \int_S Q_2(y, \{z\}) \, Q_1(x, dy) = \sum_{y \in S} Q_1(x, y) \, Q_2(y, z). \] This is precisely the \((x, z)\) entry of the matrix product \(Q_1 Q_2\), confirming that kernel composition is matrix multiplication. The associated generator then acts on \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\) by: \[ (L_Q f)(x) = \sum_{z \in S} f(z) \, Q(x, z) = \sum_{z \in S} f(z) \sum_{y \in S} Q_1(x, y) \, Q_2(y, z). \]

Example 74.3 (Composition of Two Discrete Kernels) Let \(S=\{1,2,3\}\) and consider two probability kernels \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\) on \(S\), represented by the stochastic matrices \[ Q_1= \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.3 & 0.2\\ 0.1 & 0.6 & 0.3\\ 0.2 & 0.2 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q_2= \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 & 0.2 & 0.1\\ 0.3 & 0.4 & 0.3\\ 0.1 & 0.2 & 0.7 \end{pmatrix}. \] Suppose the process starts at state \(x=1\). The first kernel \(Q_1\) determines the distribution of the intermediate state \(Y\): \(Q_1(1,\cdot)=(0.5,0.3,0.2)\). The second kernel \(Q_2\) then determines the distribution of the final state \(Z\), conditional on \(Y\). For example, the probability of ending at state \(z=3\) is \[ \begin{aligned} (Q_1Q_2)(1,3) &= \sum_{y\in S}Q_1(1,y)Q_2(y,3)\\ &= Q_1(1,1)Q_2(1,3) +Q_1(1,2)Q_2(2,3) +Q_1(1,3)Q_2(3,3)\\ &= (0.5)(0.1)+(0.3)(0.3)+(0.2)(0.7)\\ &=0.28. \end{aligned} \] Thus, \(P(Z=3\mid X=1)=0.28\). The three terms correspond to the three possible intermediate states: \[ \begin{aligned} P(X=1\to Y=1\to Z=3)&=(0.5)(0.1)=0.05,\\ P(X=1\to Y=2\to Z=3)&=(0.3)(0.3)=0.09,\\ P(X=1\to Y=3\to Z=3)&=(0.2)(0.7)=0.14. \end{aligned} \] Adding these mutually exclusive paths gives \[ 0.05+0.09+0.14=0.28. \] More generally, the composition kernel is \[ (Q_1Q_2)(x,z) = \sum_{y\in S}Q_1(x,y)Q_2(y,z). \] Hence, in this example, \[ Q_1Q_2= \begin{pmatrix} 0.40 & 0.26 & 0.34\\ 0.22 & 0.32 & 0.46\\ 0.26 & 0.28 & 0.46 \end{pmatrix}. \] For instance, the first row gives the distribution of \(Z\) after two transitions when \(X=1\): \[ (Q_1Q_2)(1,\cdot)=(0.40,0.26,0.34). \] Thus, kernel composition combines all possible intermediate states, weighting each two-step path by the probability of reaching that intermediate state under \(Q_1\).

74.5 Construction of Finite-Dimensional Distributions on Path Spaces

Probability kernels provide the building block for constructing finite-dimensional joint distributions of Markov processes.

Proposition 74.3 (Finite-Dimensional Path Distribution) Let \((S_0, \mathcal{B}_0), (S_1, \mathcal{B}_1), \dots, (S_n, \mathcal{B}_n)\) be measurable spaces. Let \(\nu \in \mathcal{P}(S_0, \mathcal{B}_0)\) be an initial probability measure, and let \(Q_k\) be a probability kernel from \((S_{k-1}, \mathcal{B}_{k-1})\) to \((S_k, \mathcal{B}_k)\) for \(k = 1, \dots, n\).

Then there exists a unique joint probability measure \(\mu \in \mathcal{P}(S_0 \times S_1 \times \dots \times S_n, \mathcal{B}_0 \otimes \mathcal{B}_1 \otimes \dots \otimes \mathcal{B}_n)\) such that for all bounded measurable functions \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S_0 \times \dots \times S_n)\): \[ \begin{aligned} \int f \, d\mu &= \int_{S_0} \int_{S_1} \dots \int_{S_n} f(x_0, x_1,\dots, x_n) \, Q_n(x_{n-1}, dx_n)\\ & \dots Q_1(x_0, dx_1) \, \nu(dx_0). \end{aligned} \]

Proof (Proof of Proposition 74.3). We construct \(\mu\) iteratively using kernels lifted to product spaces. For \(k \ge 2\), define the lifted kernel \[ \widetilde Q_k\big((x_0,\dots,x_{k-1}), A\big) := Q_k(x_{k-1}, A), \qquad A \in \mathcal{B}_k. \] This is a probability kernel from \((S_0\times\cdots\times S_{k-1}, \mathcal{B}_0\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{B}_{k-1})\) to \((S_k,\mathcal{B}_k)\).

Then the construction proceeds as follows:

  1. Define \(\mu_1 = \nu \otimes Q_1 \in \mathcal{P}(S_0 \times S_1, \mathcal{B}_0 \otimes \mathcal{B}_1)\).
  2. Define \(\mu_2 = \mu_1 \otimes \widetilde Q_2 \in \mathcal{P}(S_0 \times S_1 \times S_2, \mathcal{B}_0 \otimes \mathcal{B}_1 \otimes \mathcal{B}_2)\).
  3. Continuing inductively, set \[ \mu_k = \mu_{k-1} \otimes \widetilde Q_k \in \mathcal{P}(S_0 \times \cdots \times S_k), \qquad k=1,\dots,n. \] The resulting measure \(\mu := \mu_n\) is the desired finite-dimensional distribution.

To verify the integral identity, start with product functions \[ f(x_0,\dots,x_n) = f_0(x_0) f_1(x_1) \cdots f_n(x_n), \] where each \(f_i\) is bounded and measurable. Applying Fubini/Tonelli successively gives \[ \begin{aligned} \int f \, d\mu &= \int_{S_0} f_0(x_0) \left( \int_{S_1} f_1(x_1) \dots \left( \int_{S_n} f_n(x_n) \, Q_n(x_{n-1}, dx_n) \right) \dots Q_1(x_0, dx_1) \right) \nu(dx_0) \\ &= \int_{S_0} f_0(x_0) \Big( L_{Q_1} \big( f_1 \cdot L_{Q_2}( \dots (f_{n-1} \cdot L_{Q_n} f_n)\dots ) \big) \Big) (x_0) \, \nu(dx_0). \end{aligned} \] This is the claimed iterated-integral representation on tensor-product functions.

To extend from product functions to arbitrary bounded measurable \(f\), define \[ \mathcal{C} := \left\{E \in \mathcal{B}_0\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{B}_n : \int \mathbf{1}_E \, d\mu = \int \mathbf{1}_E \, d\mu' \right\}, \] where \(\mu'\) is the measure defined by the right-hand side of the formula. One checks that \(\mathcal{C}\) is a \(\lambda\)-system, and it contains the class of finite rectangles (equivalently, cylinder sets) because the identity has already been verified on tensor-product functions. Since the rectangles form a \(\pi\)-system generating the product \(\sigma\)-algebra, Dynkin’s \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) theorem yields \(\mathcal{C} = \mathcal{B}_0\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{B}_n\). Thus the two finite measures agree on all measurable sets, and therefore on all bounded measurable functions by standard measure-theoretic extension.

74.6 Takeaways

  1. Markov generators encode kernels. Every probability kernel \(Q\) from \((S_1, \mathcal{B}_1)\) to \((S_2, \mathcal{B}_2)\) induces a bounded linear operator \(L_Q: \mathcal{B}_b(S_2) \to \mathcal{B}_b(S_1)\), \((L_Q f)(x) = \int f \, dQ(x, \cdot)\), satisfying four structural properties: linearity, normalization (\(L_Q \mathbb{I} = \mathbb{I}\)), positivity (\(f \ge 0 \Rightarrow L_Q f \ge 0\)), and continuity under bounded pointwise convergence (via DCT). These properties imply the contraction bound \(\|L_Q\|_\infty \le 1\).

  2. Representation theorem (duality). The four properties characterize Markov generators completely. Every operator \(L: \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B}) \to \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\) satisfying them arises from a unique probability kernel, recovered by the formula \(Q(x, B) = (L \mathbb{I}_B)(x)\). The countable additivity of \(Q(x, \cdot)\) follows from the bounded-convergence property of \(L\) applied to the partial sums \(\sum_{k=1}^n \mathbb{I}_{B_k} \uparrow \mathbb{I}_B\).

  3. Composition of kernels. The product \(Q = Q_1 Q_2\) is the unique kernel whose generator is \(L_Q = L_{Q_1} \circ L_{Q_2}\). Explicitly, \((Q_1 Q_2)(x, B) = \int_S Q_2(y, B) \, Q_1(x, dy)\). For countable \(S\) this reduces to matrix multiplication: \((Q_1 Q_2)(x, z) = \sum_{y \in S} Q_1(x, y) \, Q_2(y, z)\).

  4. Path-space distributions. Given an initial distribution \(\nu \in \mathcal{P}(S_0)\) and transition kernels \(Q_k\) from \(S_{k-1}\) to \(S_k\) (\(k = 1, \dots, n\)), there exists a unique probability measure \(\mu\) on the product space \(S_0 \times \cdots \times S_n\) satisfying the iterated-integral formula \(\int f \, d\mu = \int_{S_0} \cdots \int_{S_n} f(x_0, \dots, x_n) \, Q_n(x_{n-1}, dx_n) \cdots Q_1(x_0, dx_1) \, \nu(dx_0)\). This provides the foundation for constructing finite-dimensional distributions of Markov processes.
