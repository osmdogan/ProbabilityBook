76  Lecture 35.1: Stochastic Processes

76.1 Mathematical Foundations of Stochastic Processes

Definition 76.1 (Stochastic Process) Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) be a probability space, and let \((S, \mathcal{B}_S)\) be a measurable space (referred to as the state space). Let \(T\) be an index set. When the process is time-indexed, \(T\) is typically equipped with an order \(\le\). Examples include:

  • \(\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}\) or \(\mathbb{Z}_+\) (discrete time),
  • \([0, b]\) or \([0, \infty)\) (continuous time).

A stochastic process is a collection of random variables indexed by \(T\), denoted by: \[ \{X_t\}_{t \in T}, \] where for each \(t \in T\), \(X_t: (\Omega, \mathcal{F}) \to (S, \mathcal{B}_S)\) is a measurable mapping.

76.2 Finite-Dimensional Distributions

The fundamental finite-dimensional statistical behavior of a stochastic process is captured by its finite-dimensional distributions (f.d.d.s).

Definition 76.2 (Finite-Dimensional Distributions) For any nonempty finite subset \(A \subseteq T\), choose an enumeration \(A=\{t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n\}\). The finite-dimensional distribution associated with this ordered list is the joint probability measure \(\mu_A\) on \((S^n, \mathcal{B}_S^{\otimes n})\) defined by the law of \((X_{t_1},\dots,X_{t_n})\). If \(T\) is ordered, we use the increasing order on \(A\) by convention: \[ \begin{align*} \mu_A &:= \mathrm{Law}(X_{t_1}, X_{t_2}, \dots, X_{t_n})\\ & = \mathbb{P} \circ (X_{t_1}, X_{t_2}, \dots, X_{t_n})^{-1}. \end{align*} \tag{76.1}\] Explicitly, for any measurable set \(B \in \mathcal{B}_S^{\otimes n}\): \[ \mu_A(B) = \mathbb{P}\left( (X_{t_1}, X_{t_2}, \dots, X_{t_n}) \in B \right). \]

Remark 76.1 (Limitations of Finite-Dimensional Distributions). The finite-dimensional distributions of an existing process are consistent and uniquely determine its law on \((S^T, \bigotimes_{t\in T}\mathcal{B}_S)\), the product sigma-algebra generated by finite-coordinate cylinders. Conversely, an abstract consistent family of finite-dimensional distributions admits an extension under the hypotheses of the Kolmogorov Extension Theorem; for example, a standard Borel state space is a common sufficient setting. These distributions alone generally do not settle pathwise regularity, such as continuity or boundedness, when \(T\) is uncountable. Sample-path measurability also requires specified measurable or topological structures on the index and state spaces. Appropriate regularity assumptions, such as a càdlàg version in a suitable state space, can provide additional pathwise control.

76.3 Example: Sums of Independent Random Variables

Consider \(T = \mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}\). Let \(\{\xi_k\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\) be an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sequence of random vectors taking values in \(\mathbb{R}^d\), with common distribution \(\mu = \mathrm{Law}(\xi_1)\).

Define the process \(\{X_n\}_{n \ge 0}\) by setting \(X_0 = 0\) and: \[ X_n := \sum_{k=1}^n \xi_k, \quad n \ge 1. \tag{76.2}\]

For any finite sequence of time indices \(0 < n_1 < n_2 < \dots < n_m\), the joint law \(\mathrm{Law}(X_{n_1}, X_{n_2}, \dots, X_{n_m})\) can be explicitly derived via the joint distribution of independent increments: \[ (X_{n_1}, X_{n_2} - X_{n_1}, \dots, X_{n_m} - X_{n_{m-1}}). \] For instance, the joint distribution of \((X_1, X_2, X_3)\) is completely determined by the independent components: \[ (X_1, X_2, X_3) = \left(\xi_1, \, \xi_1 + \xi_2, \, \xi_1 + \xi_2 + \xi_3 \right). \] For any bounded measurable function \(g: (\mathbb{R}^d)^3 \to \mathbb{R}\): \[ \mathbb{E}[g(X_1, X_2, X_3)] = \int_{(\mathbb{R}^d)^3} g(y_1, y_1 + y_2, y_1 + y_2 + y_3) \, \mathrm{d}\mu(y_1) \, \mathrm{d}\mu(y_2) \, \mathrm{d}\mu(y_3). \]

As seen in previous discussions on random dynamics, studying the joint asymptotic behavior of \((X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n)\) as \(n \to \infty\) requires systematic conditional structure, leading directly to the concept of filtrations.

76.4 Filtrations and Adapted Processes

Definition 76.3 (Filtration and Filtered Probability Space) Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) be a probability space and \(T\) an ordered index set.

  1. A filtration is an increasing family of \(\sigma\)-subalgebras of \(\mathcal{F}\), denoted by \(\mathbb{F} = \{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \in T}\), such that: \[ \mathcal{F}_s \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t \subseteq \mathcal{F} \quad \text{for all } s, t \in T \text{ with } s \le t. \tag{76.3}\]
  2. A tuple \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \in T}, \mathbb{P})\) equipped with a filtration is called a filtered probability space.

Definition 76.4 (Adapted Process) A stochastic process \(\{X_t\}_{t \in T}\) taking values in \((S, \mathcal{B}_S)\) defined on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \in T}, \mathbb{P})\) is said to be adapted to the filtration \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \in T}\) if, for every \(t \in T\), the random variable \(X_t\) is \(\mathcal{F}_t/\mathcal{B}_S\)-measurable: \[ \sigma(X_t) \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t \quad \text{for all } t \in T. \]

Example 76.1 (Natural Filtration) For any stochastic process \(\{X_t\}_{t \in T}\), its natural filtration \(\{\mathcal{F}_t^X\}_{t \in T}\) is defined by: \[ \mathcal{F}_t^X := \sigma(X_s : s \le t, s \in T). \tag{76.4}\]

  • By construction, \(\{X_t\}_{t \in T}\) is always trivially adapted to its natural filtration \(\mathcal{F}_t^X\).
  • Note that taking \(\mathcal{F}_t = \sigma(X_t)\) does not generally form a filtration because \(\sigma(X_s) \subseteq \sigma(X_t)\) does not necessarily hold for \(s \le t\).
  • When multiple processes are studied simultaneously, a larger, common background filtration \(\mathcal{F}_t\) containing information from all processes is used.

76.5 Markov Property for Sums of Independent Variables

Let \(\{\xi_k\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\) be i.i.d. \(\mathbb{R}^d\)-valued random vectors on a common probability space, and let \(X_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \xi_k\) with \(X_0 = 0\). Set \(\mathcal{F}_0 = \{\varnothing,\Omega\}\) and, for \(n\ge 1\), let \(\mathcal{F}_n = \sigma(\xi_1, \dots, \xi_n) = \sigma(X_1, \dots, X_n)\).

For any \(n \ge k \ge 0\) and any bounded Borel measurable function \(g: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}\): \[ X_n = X_k + \sum_{j=k+1}^n \xi_j. \]

Since \(X_k\) is \(\mathcal{F}_k\)-measurable and the sum \(\sum_{j=k+1}^n \xi_j\) is independent of \(\mathcal{F}_k\), the standard conditional-expectation identity for a measurable function of an \(\mathcal{F}_k\)-measurable variable and an independent random vector gives: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[g(X_n) \mid \mathcal{F}_k] &= \mathbb{E}\left[g\left(X_k + \sum_{j=k+1}^n \xi_j\right) \,\middle|\, \mathcal{F}_k\right] \\ &= h(X_k) \quad \text{a.s.}, \end{aligned} \] where \(h(a) := \mathbb{E}\left[g\left(a + \sum_{j=k+1}^n \xi_j\right)\right]\).

Furthermore, since \(h(X_k)\) is \(\sigma(X_k)\)-measurable, the tower property (using \(\sigma(X_k) \subseteq \mathcal{F}_k\)) gives: \[ \mathbb{E}[g(X_n) \mid X_k] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[g(X_n) \mid \mathcal{F}_k] \mid X_k] = \mathbb{E}[h(X_k) \mid X_k] = h(X_k) \quad \text{a.s.} \] Thus, we obtain the Markov property: \[ \mathbb{E}[g(X_n) \mid \mathcal{F}_k] = \mathbb{E}[g(X_n) \mid X_k] \quad \text{a.s.} \tag{76.5}\]

This process fits precisely into the random dynamics framework \(X_{n+1} = f(X_n, \xi_{n+1})\) where \(f(x, y) = x + y\) and \(X_0 = 0\).

76.6 Special Case: Simple Random Walk

Definition 76.5 (Simple Random Walk) For an integer \(d\geq1\), let \(e_1,\ldots,e_d\) denote the standard coordinate basis vectors of \(\mathbb R^d\). Suppose that \(\{\xi_k\}_{k\geq1}\) are i.i.d. random vectors taking values in \(\{\pm e_1,\ldots,\pm e_d\}\), with \[ \mathbb P(\xi_k=e_j) = \mathbb P(\xi_k=-e_j) = \frac{1}{2d}, \qquad j=1,\ldots,d. \] Starting from \(X_0=0\), define \(X_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\xi_k\) for \(n\geq1\). The process \(\{X_n\}_{n\geq0}\) is called the simple random walk on \(\mathbb Z^d\).

76.7 Arrival, Counting, and Renewal Processes

Definition 76.6 (Arrival Process) On a common probability space, let \(\{X_n\}_{n \ge 0}\) be nonnegative real-valued random variables satisfying \(X_0=0\) and, almost surely, \(X_n\le X_{n+1}\) for every \(n\ge 0\): \[ 0 = X_0 \le X_1 \le X_2 \le \dots \le X_n \le \dots \quad \text{a.s.} \] The sequence \(\{X_n\}_{n \ge 0}\) is called an arrival process, where \(X_n\) represents the arrival time of the \(n\)-th event.

Definition 76.7 (Counting Process) Given an arrival process \(\{X_n\}_{n \ge 0}\), suppose additionally that \(X_1>0\) a.s. and \(X_n\to\infty\) a.s. The associated finite-valued counting process \(\{N_t\}_{t \ge 0}\) is defined by: \[ N_t := \sup \{ n \ge 0 : X_n \le t \}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{I}_{\{X_n\leq t\}}, \quad t \ge 0. \tag{76.6}\] \(N_t\) counts the total number of arrivals that have occurred up to and including time \(t\), and \(N_0=0\) a.s. Without the condition \(X_n\to\infty\) a.s., the same formula defines an extended-valued count that may be infinite. For every integer \(n\ge 1\), the duality relation is: \[ \{ N_t \ge n \} = \{ X_n \le t \}. \tag{76.7}\]

  • For an integer \(n\ge 1\), the statement \(N_t\geq n\) means that at least \(n\) arrivals have occurred by time \(t\). Since \(X_n\) is the time of the \(n\)-th arrival, having at least \(n\) arrivals by time \(t\) means precisely that the \(n\)-th arrival has occurred by time \(t\). Therefore \[ N_t\geq n \iff X_n\leq t. \]

Definition 76.8 (Inter-arrival Times and Renewal Process) For an arrival process \(\{X_n\}_{n \ge 0}\), define the inter-arrival times \(\{\xi_n\}_{n \ge 1}\) by: \[ \xi_n := X_n - X_{n-1}, \quad n \ge 1. \tag{76.8}\] Consequently, \(X_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \xi_k\).

  • If the inter-arrival times \(\{\xi_n\}_{n \ge 1}\) are i.i.d. finite, strictly positive random variables, then \(\{X_n\}_{n \ge 0}\) is called a renewal process. In this case, \(X_n\to\infty\) a.s.: for some \(\varepsilon>0\), \(\mathbb{P}(\xi_1\ge\varepsilon)>0\), so independence and the second Borel–Cantelli lemma imply that infinitely many inter-arrival times are at least \(\varepsilon\).
  • The counting process \(\{N_t\}_{t \ge 0}\) associated with a renewal process is called a renewal counting process.
  • The homogeneous Poisson process of rate \(\lambda>0\) is the renewal counting process whose inter-arrival times are i.i.d. exponential random variables with rate \(\lambda\).

76.8 Takeaways

  1. A stochastic process \(\{X_t\}_{t\in T}\) is a family of measurable random variables on a common probability space. Its finite-dimensional distributions encode its law on the product sigma-algebra generated by finite-coordinate cylinders; abstract consistent families require the hypotheses of a suitable extension theorem for existence.
  2. F.d.d.s are insufficient to control pathwise properties (continuity, measurability of paths) when \(T\) is uncountable; additional regularity assumptions — such as càdlàg paths or separable versions — must be imposed separately.
  3. A filtration \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}\) is an increasing family of \(\sigma\)-algebras representing the information accumulated up to time \(t\). A process is adapted if \(X_t\) is \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-measurable for every \(t\); the smallest such filtration is the natural filtration \(\mathcal{F}_t^X = \sigma(X_s : s \le t)\).
  4. The collection \(\{\sigma(X_t)\}_{t\in T}\) of individual \(\sigma\)-algebras need not be increasing and therefore need not form a filtration; the natural filtration accumulates all past information before generating the \(\sigma\)-algebra.
  5. Sums of i.i.d. \(\mathbb{R}^d\)-valued increments satisfy the Markov property for bounded Borel test functions: \(\mathbb{E}[g(X_n)\mid\mathcal{F}_k] = \mathbb{E}[g(X_n)\mid X_k]\) a.s.
  6. For every integer \(n\ge1\), the counting process and arrival times satisfy \(\{N_t\ge n\}=\{X_n\le t\}\). Under the non-explosion condition \(X_n\to\infty\) a.s., the count is finite at every finite time. I.i.d. strictly positive inter-arrival times define a renewal process; i.i.d. exponential inter-arrivals yield a homogeneous Poisson process.
