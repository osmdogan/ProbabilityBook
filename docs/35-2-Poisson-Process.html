77  Lecture 35.2: Poisson Process

77.1 Review of the Exponential Distribution

The exponential distribution serves as the foundational building block for renewal and Poisson processes.

Definition 77.1 (Exponential Distribution) A random variable \(\xi\) is said to follow an exponential distribution with rate parameter \(\lambda > 0\), denoted \(\xi \sim \operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)\), if its tail probability is given by: \[ \mathbb{P}(\xi > t) = e^{-\lambda t}, \quad t \ge 0. \tag{77.1}\]

The exponential distribution frequently models waiting times because it is the unique continuous distribution possessing the memoryless property. Specifically, for any \(t, s \ge 0\): \[ \begin{align*} \mathbb{P}(\xi > t + s \mid \xi > s) &= \frac{\mathbb{P}(\xi > t + s)}{\mathbb{P}(\xi > s)}\\ &= \frac{e^{-\lambda(t+s)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} \\ &= e^{-\lambda t} \\ &= \mathbb{P}(\xi > t). \end{align*} \tag{77.2}\]

Rearranging Equation 77.2 yields the functional equation: \[ \mathbb{P}(\xi > t + s) = \mathbb{P}(\xi > t) \mathbb{P}(\xi > s). \tag{77.3}\]

77.2 Characterization via Differential Equations

Let \(f(t) := \mathbb{P}(\xi > t)\) for a continuous cumulative distribution function with \(f: [0, \infty) \to [0, 1]\). Assuming \(f\) is differentiable almost everywhere, we differentiate Equation 77.3 with respect to \(s\) at \(s = 0\): \[ \begin{align*} &f(t + h) - f(t) = f(t)(f(h) - 1) \\ &\implies \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{f(t+h) - f(t)}{h} \\ &= f(t) \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{f(h) - 1}{h}. \end{align*} \tag{77.4}\]

This leads directly to the ordinary differential equation: \[ f'(t) = -\lambda f(t), \tag{77.5}\] where \(\lambda = -\lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{f(h)-1}{h} > 0\). Solving Equation 77.5 with initial condition \(f(0) = 1\) uniquely determines \(f(t) = e^{-\lambda t}\).

77.3 Construction of the Standard Poisson Process

Let \(\{\xi_k\}_{k=1}^\infty\) be a sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) \(\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)\) random variables representing inter-arrival times.

Define the arrival times \(\{X_n\}_{n \ge 0}\) by \(X_0 = 0\) and: \[ X_n := \sum_{k=1}^n \xi_k, \quad n \ge 1. \tag{77.6}\]

The associated renewal counting process \(\{N_t\}_{t \ge 0}\) is defined by: \[ N_t := \sup \{ n \ge 0 : X_n \le t \} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{I}_{\{X_n \le t\}}, \quad t \ge 0. \tag{77.7}\]

The event that \(N_t = n\) corresponds precisely to the interval of arrival times: \[ \{ N_t = n \} = \{ X_n \le t < X_{n+1} \}. \tag{77.8}\]

Definition 77.2 (Standard Poisson Process) This renewal counting process \(\{N_t\}_{t \ge 0}\) is called the standard Poisson process of rate \(\lambda\).

77.4 Finite-Dimensional Distributions of the Poisson Process

To understand the finite-dimensional distributions of \(\{N_t\}_{t \ge 0}\), it is first useful to compute the joint distributions of the arrival times \(\{X_n\}_{n \ge 1}\).

Notice that the arrival times satisfy \(X_n > X_{n-1} > \dots > X_1 > 0\) almost surely. Consequently, the state space of \((X_1, \dots, X_n)\) is restricted to a simplex rather than the full Euclidean space \(\mathbb{R}^n\).

Definition 77.3 (Arrival Simplex Domains) For \(t > 0\), define the ordered simplex domains:

  • \(D_n(t) := \{ (x_1, \dots, x_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n : 0 < x_1 < x_2 < \dots < x_n \le t \}\).
  • \(\widetilde{D}_n :=\cup_{t>0}D_n(t) = \{ (x_1, \dots, x_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n : 0 < x_1 < x_2 < \dots < x_n \}\).

Lemma 77.1 (Lebesgue Measure of the Simplex) The Lebesgue measure of \(D_n(t)\) is given by: \[ \operatorname{Leb}(D_n(t)) = \frac{t^n}{n!}. \tag{77.9}\]

Proof (Proof of Lemma 77.1). Write the measure as the iterated integral over the nested inequalities \(0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n \le t\): \[ \operatorname{Leb}(D_n(t)) = \int_0^t \int_0^{x_n} \cdots \int_0^{x_2} 1 \, \mathrm{d}x_1 \, \mathrm{d}x_2 \cdots \mathrm{d}x_{n-1} \, \mathrm{d}x_n. \] Define \(I_k(s) := \operatorname{Leb}(D_k(s))\) for \(k \ge 1\) and \(s \ge 0\). We prove \(I_k(s) = s^k / k!\) by induction on \(k\).

Base case (\(k = 1\)): \[ I_1(s) = \int_0^s \mathrm{d}x_1 = s = \frac{s^1}{1!}. \]

Inductive step: Suppose \(I_{k-1}(x) = x^{k-1}/(k-1)!\) for all \(x \ge 0\). The outermost integral peels off the \(x_k\)-integration, with the inner \((k-1)\) integrals giving \(I_{k-1}(x_k)\): \[ I_k(s) = \int_0^s I_{k-1}(x_k) \, \mathrm{d}x_k = \int_0^s \frac{x_k^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} \, \mathrm{d}x_k = \frac{1}{(k-1)!} \cdot \frac{s^k}{k} = \frac{s^k}{k!}. \]

Applying the result at \(k = n\) and \(s = t\) gives \(\operatorname{Leb}(D_n(t)) = I_n(t) = t^n / n!\).

Proof (Alternative proof of Lemma 77.1). The cube \((0,t)^n\) can be partitioned, up to a Lebesgue-null set, into the \(n!\) regions corresponding to the possible strict orderings of \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\). By symmetry of Lebesgue measure each region has the same measure, so \[ \operatorname{Leb}(D_n(t)) =\frac{\operatorname{Leb}((0,t)^n)}{n!}= \frac{t^n}{n!}. \]

Lemma 77.2 (Joint Density of Arrival Times) If \(g\in\mathcal{B}_b\left(\widetilde{D}_n,\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{D}_n)\right)\) is a bounded measurable function, then: \[ \mathbb{E}\left[g(X_1, \dots, X_n)\right] = \int_{\widetilde{D}_n} g(x_1, \dots, x_n) \lambda^n e^{-\lambda x_n} \, \mathrm{d}x_1 \dots \mathrm{d}x_n. \tag{77.10}\]

Proof (Proof of Lemma 77.2). The inter-arrival times \(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n\) are i.i.d. exponential random variables with parameter \(\lambda\), so their joint density on \(\mathbb{R}_+^n\) is \[ \begin{align*} f_{\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n}(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)&= \prod_{k=1}^n \lambda e^{-\lambda \xi_k}=\lambda^n e^{-\lambda(\xi_1+\cdots+\xi_n)}. \end{align*} \] The arrival times are related to the inter-arrival times by \[ X_k=\sum_{j=1}^k \xi_j, \qquad k=1,\ldots,n. \] Equivalently, the inverse transformation is \[ \xi_1=x_1,\qquad \xi_2=x_2-x_1,\qquad \ldots,\qquad \xi_n=x_n-x_{n-1}. \] Since \(\xi_k>0\) for every \(k\), the corresponding \((x_1,\ldots,x_n)\) satisfy \[ 0<x_1<x_2<\cdots<x_n, \] so the transformation maps \(\mathbb{R}_+^n\) onto \(\widetilde{D}_n\). The Jacobian matrix of the inverse transformation is \[ \frac{\partial(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)} {\partial(x_1,\ldots,x_n)}= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ -1 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ 0 & -1 & 1 & \cdots & 0\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \] This matrix is lower triangular with diagonal entries equal to \(1\), and hence \[ \left|\text{det} \frac{\partial(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)} {\partial(x_1,\ldots,x_n)}\right|=1. \] Moreover, \[ \xi_1+\cdots+\xi_n=x_n. \] Therefore, by the change-of-variables formula, \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[g(X_1,\ldots,X_n)\right] &= \int_{\mathbb{R}_+^n} g(\xi_1,\xi_1+\xi_2,\ldots,\xi_1+\cdots+\xi_n) \lambda^n e^{-\lambda(\xi_1+\cdots+\xi_n)} d\xi_1\cdots d\xi_n\\ &= \int_{\widetilde{D}_n} g(x_1,\ldots,x_n) \lambda^n e^{-\lambda x_n} dx_1\cdots dx_n. \end{aligned} \] This proves the result.

Corollary 77.1 (Poisson Marginal Distribution) For each \(t > 0\) and \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), the counting process variable \(N_t\) follows a Poisson distribution with parameter \(\lambda t\), i.e., \(N_t \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda t)\).

Proof. We compute the probability \(\mathbb{P}(N_t = n)\) using the indicator of the event \(\{X_n \le t < X_{n+1}\}\): \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(N_t = n) &= \mathbb{P}(X_n \le t < X_{n+1})=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}_{\{X_n \le t < X_{n+1}\}}\right] \\ &= \int_{\widetilde{D}_{n+1}} \mathbb{I}_{\{x_n\le t\}}\mathbb{I}_{\{x_{n+1} > t\}} \lambda^{n+1} e^{-\lambda x_{n+1}}\mathrm{d}x_1 \dots \mathrm{d}x_{n+1} \\ &= \int_{D_n(t)} \mathrm{d}x_1 \dots \mathrm{d}x_n\int_t^\infty \lambda^{n+1} e^{-\lambda x_{n+1}} \, \mathrm{d}x_{n+1}. \end{aligned} \] The first integral is the volume of the simplex \(D_n(t)\), which is \(\frac{t^n}{n!}\). The last integral evaluates to \(e^{-\lambda t}\lambda^n\). Multiplying these two factors gives the final result: \[ \mathbb{P}(N_t = n) = e^{-\lambda t} \frac{(\lambda t)^n}{n!}. \] This confirms that \(N_t \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda t)\).

77.5 Order Statistics Property of Arrival Times

Proposition 77.1 (Uniform Order Statistics Property) Let \(n \in \mathbb{N}\) and \(t > 0\). Let \(U_1, \dots, U_n\) be independent and identically distributed \(\operatorname{Unif}(0, t)\) random variables. Then, conditioned on the event \(\{N_t = n\}\), the joint distribution of \((X_1, \dots, X_n)\) is identical to the joint distribution of the order statistics of \((U_1, \dots, U_n)\) (i.e., the values sorted in non-decreasing order).

Proof (Proof of Proposition 77.1). First note that if \(U_1,\ldots,U_n\sim\text{Unif}(0, t)\), then their joint density is \[ f_{U_1,\ldots,U_n}(u_1,\ldots,u_n)=\frac{1}{t^n}\mathbb{I}_{(0,t)^n}(u_1,\ldots,u_n). \] Thus the vector \((U_1,\ldots,U_n)\) is uniformly distributed on the cube \((0,t)^n\). The order statistics \((U_{(1)},\ldots,U_{(n)})\) are obtained by sorting the \(U_i\)’s in non-decreasing order. The joint density of the order statistics is given by \[ f_{U_{(1)},\ldots,U_{(n)}}(u_1,\ldots,u_n)=\frac{n!}{t^n}\mathbb{I}_{D_n(t)}(u_1,\ldots,u_n), \] where the factor of \(n!\) accounts for the \(n!\) possible permutations of the \(U_i\)’s that yield the same order statistics.

For any bounded measurable function \(f: D_n(t) \to \mathbb{R}\): \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_1, \dots, X_n) \mid N_t = n] = \frac{\mathbb{E}[f(X_1, \dots, X_n)\,\mathbb{I}(X_n \le t < X_{n+1})]}{\mathbb{P}(N_t = n)}. \] For the numerator, apply Lemma 77.2 to \((X_1,\dots,X_{n+1})\) and separate the \(x_{n+1}\)-integration over \((t,\infty)\) (exactly as in the proof of Corollary 77.1): \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_1,\dots,X_n)\,\mathbb{I}(X_n\le t<X_{n+1})] = \lambda^n e^{-\lambda t}\int_{D_n(t)} f(x_1, \dots, x_n) \, \mathrm{d}x_1 \cdots \mathrm{d}x_n. \] Dividing by \(\mathbb{P}(N_t = n) = e^{-\lambda t} \frac{(\lambda t)^n}{n!}\) gives: \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_1, \dots, X_n) \mid N_t = n] = \frac{n!}{t^n} \int_{D_n(t)} f(x_1, \dots, x_n) \, \mathrm{d}x_1 \cdots \mathrm{d}x_n. \] The right-hand side is exactly \(\mathbb{E}[f(U_{(1)}, \dots, U_{(n)})]\), where \(U_{(1)} \le \dots \le U_{(n)}\) are the order statistics of \(n\) independent \(\operatorname{Unif}(0, t)\) variables. Indeed, \((U_{(1)},\dots,U_{(n)})\) has joint density \(\frac{n!}{t^n}\) on \(D_n(t)\) (the \(n!\) comes from symmetry: the \(n!\) possible orderings of \((U_1,\dots,U_n)\) each occupy the same Lebesgue measure \(t^n/n!\) within \((0,t)^n\)). Since the identity holds for every bounded measurable \(f\), the conditional distribution of \((X_1,\dots,X_n)\) given \(N_t=n\) equals the distribution of the order statistics \((U_{(1)},\dots,U_{(n)})\).

77.6 Stochastic Counting Measure and Independent Increments

To study spatial or temporal distributions more generally, we generalize the counting process to a stochastic counting measure.

For any Borel set \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_+)\), define \(N(B)\) as the number of arrival points \(X_n\) falling within \(B\): \[ N(B) := \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mathbb{I}_B(X_n) = N\left(B \cap [0, t]\right) \quad \text{(for bounded sets $B \subset [0, t]$)}. \]

Theorem 77.1 (Independence and Poisson Increments Theorem)  

  1. If \(B_1, \dots, B_k\) form a disjoint partition of an interval \([0, t]\), then the random variables \(N(B_1), \dots, N(B_k)\) are mutually independent.
  2. If \(\operatorname{Leb}(B) < \infty\), then \(N(B) \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda \operatorname{Leb}(B))\).
  3. In particular, for any \(0 \le s < t\), the increment \(N((s, t]) = N_t - N_s\) is independent of \(\mathcal{F}_s\) and follows \(\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda(t - s))\).

Proof (Proof of Theorem 77.1). Parts 1 and 2.

Write \(\ell_j := \operatorname{Leb}(B_j)\) and note \(\sum_{j=1}^k \ell_j = t\). For \(u_1, \dots, u_k \in \mathbb{R}\), define \[ \tilde{u}(x) := \sum_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j}\,\mathbb{I}_{B_j}(x), \quad x \in [0, t]. \] Since the \(B_j\) partition \([0, t]\), exactly one indicator is non-zero for each \(x\), so \(\tilde{u}\) is well-defined. We compute the joint characteristic function \[ \phi(\mathbf{u}) := \mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j N(B_j)}\right]. \]

Step 1: Conditional expectation on \(\{N_t = n\}\).

On the event \(\{N_t = n\} = \{X_n \le t < X_{n+1}\}\), the arrivals that fall in \([0,t]\) are exactly \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) (since \(X_{n+1} > t\)). Because \(B_1, \dots, B_k\) partition \([0,t]\) and every \(X_m \in [0,t]\), the count of arrivals in \(B_j\) is: \[ N(B_j) = \sum_{m=1}^n \mathbb{I}_{B_j}(X_m), \quad j = 1, \dots, k. \] Substituting and rearranging the product: \[ \prod_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j N(B_j)} = \prod_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j \sum_{m=1}^n \mathbb{I}_{B_j}(X_m)} = \prod_{j=1}^k \prod_{m=1}^n e^{i u_j \mathbb{I}_{B_j}(X_m)} = \prod_{m=1}^n \prod_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j \mathbb{I}_{B_j}(X_m)}. \] For each fixed \(m\), the sets \(B_j\) partition \([0,t]\), so \(X_m\) belongs to exactly one set, say \(X_m \in B_{j_0}\). Then \(\mathbb{I}_{B_j}(X_m) = \mathbf{1}_{j=j_0}\), and: \[ \prod_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j \mathbb{I}_{B_j}(X_m)} = e^{i u_{j_0} \cdot 1} \prod_{j \ne j_0} e^{i u_j \cdot 0} = e^{i u_{j_0}}. \] On the other hand, the definition of \(\tilde{u}\) gives: \[ \tilde{u}(X_m) = \sum_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j} \mathbb{I}_{B_j}(X_m) = e^{i u_{j_0}}. \] Therefore \(\prod_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j \mathbb{I}_{B_j}(X_m)} = \tilde{u}(X_m)\) for every \(m\), and: \[ \prod_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j N(B_j)} = \prod_{m=1}^n \tilde{u}(X_m) \quad \text{on } \{N_t = n\}. \] Applying Lemma 77.2 to \((X_1, \dots, X_{n+1})\): \[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j N(B_j)}\,\mathbb{I}(N_t = n)\right] = \int_{\widetilde{D}_{n+1}} \prod_{m=1}^n \tilde{u}(x_m)\cdot\mathbb{I}(x_n \le t < x_{n+1}) \cdot\lambda^{n+1} e^{-\lambda x_{n+1}}\,dx_1\cdots dx_{n+1}. \] The constraint \(x_{n+1} > t\) decouples the \(x_{n+1}\)-integration from the rest: \[ = \lambda^{n+1}\int_{D_n(t)}\prod_{m=1}^n \tilde{u}(x_m)\,dx_1\cdots dx_n \cdot\int_t^\infty e^{-\lambda x_{n+1}}\,dx_{n+1}. \] Since \(\displaystyle\int_t^\infty e^{-\lambda x}\,dx = \frac{e^{-\lambda t}}{\lambda}\), the \(\lambda^{n+1}\) and \(1/\lambda\) cancel to give: \[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j N(B_j)}\,\mathbb{I}(N_t = n)\right] = \lambda^n e^{-\lambda t}\int_{D_n(t)}\prod_{m=1}^n \tilde{u}(x_m)\,dx_1\cdots dx_n. \tag{77.11}\]

Step 2: Evaluating the simplex integral.

The integrand \(\prod_{m=1}^n \tilde{u}(x_m)\) is symmetric in \((x_1, \dots, x_n)\) (each factor depends on a single variable and is unaffected by permuting the others). The simplex \(D_n(t) = \{0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n \le t\}\) is exactly one of the \(n!\) equal-measure regions obtained by partitioning \((0,t)^n\) according to the ordering of the coordinates. By symmetry, the integral over each such region is the same, so \[ \int_{D_n(t)}\prod_{m=1}^n \tilde{u}(x_m)\,dx_1\cdots dx_n = \frac{1}{n!}\int_{(0,t)^n}\prod_{m=1}^n \tilde{u}(x_m)\,dx_1\cdots dx_n. \] The integrand factors over coordinates, so Fubini gives \[ \int_{(0,t)^n}\prod_{m=1}^n \tilde{u}(x_m)\,dx_1\cdots dx_n = \prod_{m=1}^n\int_0^t \tilde{u}(x)\,dx = \left(\int_0^t \tilde{u}(x)\,dx\right)^n. \] Since the \(B_j\) are disjoint, \[ \int_0^t \tilde{u}(x)\,dx = \sum_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j}\int_{B_j}dx = \sum_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j}\ell_j. \] Substituting back into Equation 77.11: \[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j N(B_j)}\,\mathbb{I}(N_t = n)\right] = \frac{\lambda^n e^{-\lambda t}}{n!}\left(\sum_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j}\ell_j\right)^n. \]

Step 3: Summing over \(n \ge 0\).

Since \(\{N_t = n\}_{n \ge 0}\) are disjoint events whose union is the whole sample space, the total expectation decomposes as: \[ \phi(\mathbf{u}) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j N(B_j)}\right] = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j N(B_j)}\,\mathbb{I}(N_t = n)\right]. \] Substituting the result of Steps 1 and 2: \[ = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{\lambda^n e^{-\lambda t}}{n!}\left(\sum_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j}\ell_j\right)^n. \] Set \(c := \lambda\sum_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j}\ell_j\). Since \(|c| \le \lambda\sum_j \ell_j = \lambda t < \infty\), the series converges absolutely and equals \(e^c\) by the Taylor expansion of the exponential: \[ \phi(\mathbf{u}) = e^{-\lambda t}\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{c^n}{n!} = e^{-\lambda t} e^{c} = e^{-\lambda t}\exp\!\left(\lambda\sum_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j}\ell_j\right). \] Since the \(B_j\) partition \([0,t]\), we have \(\sum_{j=1}^k \ell_j = t\), so \(-\lambda t = -\lambda\sum_{j=1}^k \ell_j\). Combining the exponents: \[ \phi(\mathbf{u}) = \exp\!\left(-\lambda\sum_{j=1}^k \ell_j + \lambda\sum_{j=1}^k e^{i u_j}\ell_j\right) = \exp\!\left(\lambda\sum_{j=1}^k \ell_j(e^{i u_j}-1)\right). \] The exponent is a sum of \(k\) independent terms, so the exponential factors into a product: \[ \phi(\mathbf{u}) = \prod_{j=1}^k \exp\!\left(\lambda\ell_j(e^{i u_j}-1)\right). \tag{77.12}\]

Conclusion for Parts 1 and 2. The joint characteristic function \(\phi(\mathbf{u})\) is the product over \(j\) of \(\exp(\lambda\ell_j(e^{iu_j}-1))\), which is exactly the characteristic function of a \(\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda\ell_j)\) random variable. By uniqueness of characteristic functions:

  • (Part 1) \(N(B_1), \dots, N(B_k)\) are mutually independent.
  • (Part 2) Each marginal satisfies \(N(B_j) \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda\ell_j) = \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda\operatorname{Leb}(B_j))\).

Part 3.

Fix \(0 \le s < t\). For any finite partition \(0 < r_1 < \cdots < r_{m-1} < s\) of \([0,s]\), apply Part 1 to the partition \[ \bigl\{[0, r_1],\,(r_1, r_2],\,\dots,\,(r_{m-1}, s],\,(s, t]\bigr\} \] of \([0, t]\). This gives mutual independence of all the increment counts, and in particular shows that \(N((s,t])\) is independent of the finite family \[ \bigl\{N([0,r_1]),\,N((r_1,r_2]),\,\dots,\,N((r_{m-1},s])\bigr\}. \] The \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F}_s = \sigma(N_r : r \le s)\) is generated by all such families (as the partition of \([0,s]\) is refined). By the \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) theorem, \(N((s,t])\) is independent of the entire \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F}_s\). Part 2 applied to \(B = (s,t]\) with \(\operatorname{Leb}((s,t]) = t-s\) gives \(N((s,t]) = N_t - N_s \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda(t-s))\). \(\blacksquare\)

77.7 Takeaways

  1. The exponential distribution \(\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)\) is the unique continuous distribution satisfying the memoryless property \(\mathbb{P}(\xi>t+s\mid\xi>s)=\mathbb{P}(\xi>t)\). The survival function \(f(t)=e^{-\lambda t}\) is the unique differentiable solution to the functional equation \(f(t+s)=f(t)f(s)\) with \(f(0)=1\).
  2. The Poisson process of rate \(\lambda\) is constructed as the renewal counting process \(N_t=\sup\{n\ge0:X_n\le t\}\) built from i.i.d. \(\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)\) inter-arrivals. Arrival times \((X_1,\dots,X_n)\) have joint density \(\lambda^n e^{-\lambda x_n}\) on the ordered simplex \(\widetilde{D}_n\), derived via a change of variables whose Jacobian is \(1\).
  3. The marginal \(N_t\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda t)\) follows by integrating the joint density: the volume of \(D_n(t)\) contributes \(t^n/n!\) and the exponential tail over \((t,\infty)\) contributes \(\lambda^n e^{-\lambda t}\), together yielding \(e^{-\lambda t}(\lambda t)^n/n!\).
  4. Conditioned on \(N_t=n\), the \(n\) arrival times \((X_1,\dots,X_n)\) have joint density \(n!/t^n\) on \(D_n(t)\), which is the density of the order statistics of \(n\) independent \(\operatorname{Unif}(0,t)\) random variables.
  5. For disjoint Borel sets \(B_1,\dots,B_k\) partitioning \([0,t]\), the counts \(N(B_1),\dots,N(B_k)\) are mutually independent with \(N(B_j)\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda\operatorname{Leb}(B_j))\). The proof factorizes the joint characteristic function by symmetrizing the simplex integral and exponentiating the resulting Taylor series.
  6. The increment \(N_t-N_s\) is independent of \(\mathcal{F}_s=\sigma(N_r:r\le s)\) for all \(0\le s<t\), and follows \(\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda(t-s))\). This independence follows from applying the disjoint-partition result to any partition that includes \((s,t]\), then extending to all of \(\mathcal{F}_s\) via the \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) theorem.
