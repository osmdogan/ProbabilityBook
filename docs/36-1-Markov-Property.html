78  Lecture 36.1: The Markov Property

78.1 The Markov Property

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) be a probability space equipped with a filtration \((\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \in T}\), where the index set \(T\) is either \(\mathbb{N}\) (discrete time) or \(\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\) (continuous time). Let \((S, \mathcal{B})\) be a measurable state space.

Definition 78.1 (Adapted Stochastic Process) A stochastic process \(X = (X_t)_{t \in T}\) taking values in \((S, \mathcal{B})\) is adapted to the filtration \((\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \in T}\) if \(X_t\) is \((\mathcal{F}_t, \mathcal{B})\)-measurable for every \(t \in T\).

Definition 78.2 (The Markov Property) An adapted stochastic process \(X = (X_t)_{t \in T}\) satisfies the Markov property with respect to the filtration \((\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \in T}\) if, for every bounded measurable function \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\) and for all \(s, t \in T\) with \(s \le t\), \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] \quad \text{a.s.} \tag{78.1}\]

Remark 78.1. Intuitively, the definition says: Once we know the present state \(X_s\), knowing the entire past up to time \(s\) gives us no additional information about the future state \(X_t\).

Consider the left-hand side: \(\mathbb E[f(X_t)\mid\mathcal F_s]\). \(\mathcal F_s\) represents everything we know from the past and present up to time \(s\). Thus \(\mathbb E[f(X_t)\mid\mathcal F_s]\) is our best prediction of \(f(X_t)\) using the entire history up to time \(s\).

Consider the right-hand side: \(\mathbb E[f(X_t)\mid X_s]\). Here we throw away the entire history and retain only the current state \(X_s\). Thus \(\mathbb E[f(X_t)\mid X_s]\) is our best prediction of \(f(X_t)\) based solely on the current state.

The Markov property says these two predictions are identical.So, schematically, \[ \underbrace{\text{Past history}}_{\mathcal F_s} \longrightarrow \underbrace{X_s}_{\text{present}} \longrightarrow \underbrace{X_t}_{\text{future}}. \] The past can affect the future only through the information contained in the present state.

Remark 78.2. Why do we use \(f(X_t)\) rather than just \(X_t\)? This is important. Saying \[ \mathbb E[X_t\mid\mathcal F_s] = \mathbb E[X_t\mid X_s] \] only says that the conditional mean of \(X_t\) depends on the past through \(X_s\). That is not enough to characterize the Markov property. By requiring the equality for every bounded measurable \(f\), \[ \mathbb E[f(X_t)\mid\mathcal F_s] = \mathbb E[f(X_t)\mid X_s], \] we are effectively saying that the entire conditional distribution of \(X_t\) given the past is determined by \(X_s\). For example, taking \(f(x)=\mathbf 1_A(x)\) gives \[ \mathbb P(X_t\in A\mid\mathcal F_s) = \mathbb P(X_t\in A\mid X_s) \] for every measurable set \(A\). So an even more intuitive formulation is: Given the present state \(X_s\), the conditional distribution of the future state \(X_t\) does not depend on the history before \(s\).

Proposition 78.1 (Equivalent Characterization via Factorization) An adapted process \((X_t)_{t \in T}\) satisfies Equation 78.1 if and only if for every \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\) and all \(s \le t\), there exists a measurable function \(F \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\) such that \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = F(X_s) \quad \text{a.s.} \tag{78.2}\]

Proof. \((\Longrightarrow)\) Suppose Equation 78.1 holds. Since \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s]\) is measurable with respect to \(\sigma(X_s)\), the Doob–Dynkin Lemma guarantees the existence of a measurable function \(F \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\) such that \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] = F(X_s)\) almost surely. Thus, Equation 78.2 holds.

\((\Longleftarrow)\) Conversely, suppose there exists \(F \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\) such that \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = F(X_s)\) a.s. By taking conditional expectations with respect to \(\sigma(X_s)\) on both sides and using the tower property (noting that \(\sigma(X_s) \subseteq \mathcal{F}_s\)), we obtain: \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] = \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] \mid X_s \right] = \mathbb{E}[F(X_s) \mid X_s] = F(X_s) \quad \text{a.s.} \] Comparing this with Equation 78.2 yields \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s]\) a.s.

Remark 78.3. Let \(\mathcal{F}_s^X := \sigma(X_r : r \le s)\) denote the natural filtration generated by \(X\). Since \(X\) is adapted, \(\mathcal{F}_s^X \subseteq \mathcal{F}_s\). If the Markov property holds for \((\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \in T}\), then by applying the tower property: \[ \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s^X] &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] \mid \mathcal{F}_s^X \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] \mid \mathcal{F}_s^X \right]\\ &= \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] \quad \text{a.s.} \end{align*} \] The last step holds because \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s]\) is \(\sigma(X_s)\)-measurable, and \(\sigma(X_s) \subseteq \mathcal{F}_s^X\) (since \(X_s\) is \(\mathcal{F}_s^X\)-measurable), so \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s]\) is already \(\mathcal{F}_s^X\)-measurable and conditioning on \(\mathcal{F}_s^X\) leaves it unchanged. Thus, the Markov property with respect to a general filtration implies the Markov property with respect to the process’s natural filtration.

78.2 Discrete-Time Formulation and Induction

When the time index set \(T = \mathbb{N}\) is discrete, the Markov property can be stated in terms of one-step transitions.

Proposition 78.2 (One-Step Characterization in Discrete Time) Let \(T = \mathbb{N}\). The process \((X_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}\) satisfies the Markov property if and only if for every \(n \in \mathbb{N}\) and every \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\), \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_{n+1}) \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = \mathbb{E}[f(X_{n+1}) \mid X_n] \quad \text{a.s.} \tag{78.3}\]

Proof. The forward implication (\(\Longrightarrow\)) is immediate by setting \(s = n\) and \(t = n+1\) in Equation 78.1.

For the reverse implication (\(\Longleftarrow\)), we proceed by induction on \(n \ge k\) for a fixed base time \(k \in \mathbb{N}\).

  • Base Case (\(n = k+1\)): By assumption Equation 78.3, \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_{k+1}) \mid \mathcal{F}_k] = \mathbb{E}[f(X_{k+1}) \mid X_k]\) a.s.

  • Induction Hypothesis: Assume that for some \(n > k\), for all \(g \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\), \[ \mathbb{E}[g(X_n) \mid \mathcal{F}_k] = \mathbb{E}[g(X_n) \mid X_k] \quad \text{a.s.}\]

  • Inductive Step: Consider \(f(X_{n+1})\). By Equation 78.3 and Proposition 78.1, there exists \(q_{n+1} \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\) such that: \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_{n+1}) \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = q_{n+1}(X_n) \quad \text{a.s.} \] Using the tower property of conditional expectation since \(\mathcal{F}_k \subseteq \mathcal{F}_n\): \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[f(X_{n+1}) \mid \mathcal{F}_k] &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_{n+1}) \mid \mathcal{F}_n] \mid \mathcal{F}_k \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[q_{n+1}(X_n) \mid \mathcal{F}_k]. \end{aligned} \] Applying the induction hypothesis to \(g = q_{n+1}\), we get: \[ \mathbb{E}[q_{n+1}(X_n) \mid \mathcal{F}_k] = \mathbb{E}[q_{n+1}(X_n) \mid X_k] \quad \text{a.s.} \] Recombining these equalities gives: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[f(X_{n+1}) \mid \mathcal{F}_k] &= \mathbb{E}[q_{n+1}(X_n) \mid X_k] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_{n+1}) \mid \mathcal{F}_n] \mid X_k \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[f(X_{n+1}) \mid X_k] \quad \text{a.s.}, \end{aligned} \] which completes the induction step.

78.3 Generalization to Future \(\sigma\)-Fields

The Markov property is often interpreted as stating that “the future depends on the past only through the present.” We now formalize this for arbitrary functions of the future.

Definition 78.3 (Future \(\sigma\)-Field) Given a stochastic process \((X_t)_{t \in T}\) and a fixed time \(s \in T\), the future \(\sigma\)-field \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X\) (or \(\mathcal{F}_{[s,\infty)}^X\)) is defined by: \[ \mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X := \sigma(X_t : t \ge s). \] (Note: As \(s\) increases, \(s \mapsto \mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X\) forms a reverse filtration: if \(s_1 \le s_2\), then \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge s_2}^X \subseteq \mathcal{F}_{\ge s_1}^X\).)

Theorem 78.1 (Markov Property for General Future Random Variables) Let \((X_t)_{t \in T}\) be an adapted Markov process. Then for any \(s \in T\) and any bounded measurable random variable \(Y \in \mathcal{B}_b(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X)\), \[ \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_s] \quad \text{a.s.} \tag{78.4}\]

Remark 78.4. The original Markov property says: To predict a future state \(X_t\), the entire past can be replaced by the current state \(X_s\). Theorem 78.1 says: To predict anything that depends on the entire future path, the entire past can still be replaced by the current state \(X_s\).

To prove Theorem 78.1, we use a monotone class argument (\(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) theorem / Functional Dynkin System Theorem).

Lemma 78.1 (Multiplicative System Generation) Let \(s \in T\) be fixed. Define the set of finite cylindrical cylinder-type random variables: \[ \mathbb{M} := \left\{ \prod_{i=1}^n g_i(X_{t_i}) : n \in \mathbb{N}, \, s \le t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_n, \, g_i \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B}) \right\}. \] Then \(\mathbb{M}\) is a multiplicative system (i.e., closed under pointwise multiplication) that generates the future \(\sigma\)-field \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X\).

Proof (Proof of Lemma 78.1). Part 1: \(\mathbb{M}\) is closed under multiplication. Let \(A_1, A_2 \in \mathbb{M}\). Then \(A_1 = \prod_{i=1}^n g_i(X_{t_i})\) and \(A_2 = \prod_{j=1}^m h_j(X_{r_j})\). The product \(A_1 A_2\) is a finite product of bounded measurable evaluations at times in \([s,\infty)\), which can be sorted chronologically. Thus \(A_1 A_2 \in \mathbb{M}\).

Part 2: \(\sigma(\mathbb{M}) = \mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X\).

\((\subseteq)\): Every element of \(\mathbb{M}\) is a finite product \(\prod_{i=1}^n g_i(X_{t_i})\) with each \(t_i \ge s\). Each factor \(g_i(X_{t_i})\) is \(\sigma(X_{t_i})\)-measurable, hence measurable with respect to \(\sigma(X_t : t \ge s) = \mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X\). A finite product of \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X\)-measurable functions is \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X\)-measurable, so every element of \(\mathbb{M}\) is \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X\)-measurable, and therefore \(\sigma(\mathbb{M}) \subseteq \mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X\).

\((\supseteq)\): It suffices to show that every generator of \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X\) lies in \(\sigma(\mathbb{M})\). Fix any \(t \ge s\) and \(B \in \mathcal{B}\). Since \(\mathbf{1}_B \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\), taking \(n = 1\), \(t_1 = t\), \(g_1 = \mathbf{1}_B\) gives: \[ \mathbf{1}_B(X_t) = \mathbf{1}_{\{X_t \in B\}} \in \mathbb{M}. \] Hence \(\{X_t \in B\} = \{\omega : \mathbf{1}_B(X_t(\omega)) = 1\} \in \sigma(\mathbb{M})\). The collection \(\{\{X_t \in B\} : t \ge s,\, B \in \mathcal{B}\}\) generates \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X = \sigma(X_t : t \ge s)\), so \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X \subseteq \sigma(\mathbb{M})\).

Lemma 78.2 (Monotone Class Setup) Define the functional class \(\mathbb{H}\) as: \[ \mathbb{H} := \left\{ Y \in \mathcal{B}_b(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X) : \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_s] \text{ a.s.} \right\}. \] Then \(\mathbb{H}\) is a vector space, contains the constant function \(1\), and is closed under bounded monotone convergence.

Proof (Proof of Lemma 78.2).

  1. Vector Space: Follows directly from the linearity of conditional expectation.
  2. Contains \(1\): \(\mathbb{E}[1 \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = 1 = \mathbb{E}[1 \mid X_s]\) a.s.
  3. Closed under Bounded Convergence: If \((Y_k)_{k=1}^\infty \subset \mathbb{H}\) with \(Y_k \to Y\) pointwise and \(\vert{}Y_k\vert{} \le C < \infty\), then by the Conditional Dominated Convergence Theorem: \[ \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \lim_{k\to\infty} \mathbb{E}[Y_k \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \lim_{k\to\infty} \mathbb{E}[Y_k \mid X_s] = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_s] \quad \text{a.s.} \]

Proof. Proof of Theorem 78.1. By functional Dynkin system theorem / monotone class theorem, if \(\mathbb{M} \subseteq \mathbb{H}\), then \(\mathbb{H}\) contains all bounded \(\sigma(\mathbb{M})\)-measurable functions, so \(\mathbb{H} = \mathcal{B}_b(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X)\).

It remains to verify \(\mathbb{M} \subseteq \mathbb{H}\). Take \(Y = \prod_{i=1}^n g_i(X_{t_i}) \in \mathbb{M}\) with \(s \le t_1 < \dots < t_n\). Using successive conditioning (tower property): \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{F}_s] &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \left( \prod_{i=1}^{n-1} g_i(X_{t_i}) \right) g_n(X_{t_n}) \;\middle\vert{}\; \mathcal{F}_s \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{E}\left[ \left( \prod_{i=1}^{n-1} g_i(X_{t_i}) \right) g_n(X_{t_n}) \;\middle\vert{}\; \mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}} \right] \;\middle\vert{}\; \mathcal{F}_s \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \left( \prod_{i=1}^{n-1} g_i(X_{t_i}) \right) \mathbb{E}[g_n(X_{t_n}) \mid \mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}] \;\middle\vert{}\; \mathcal{F}_s \right]. \end{aligned} \] The factoring in the last line is justified because, for each \(i \le n-1\), we have \(t_i \le t_{n-1}\), so \(g_i(X_{t_i})\) is \(\mathcal{F}_{t_i} \subseteq \mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}\)-measurable by adaptedness. Hence the product \(\prod_{i=1}^{n-1} g_i(X_{t_i})\) is \(\mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}\)-measurable and bounded, so it can be taken out of \(\mathbb{E}[\cdot \mid \mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}]\) by the “taking out what is known” property. By the Markov property at \(t_{n-1}\), \(\mathbb{E}[g_n(X_{t_n}) \mid \mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}] = h(X_{t_{n-1}})\) for some \(h \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\). Substituting this reduces the expression to a product of \(n-1\) terms: \[ \mathbb{E}\left[ \left( \prod_{i=1}^{n-2} g_i(X_{t_i}) \right) \cdot \tilde{g}_{n-1}(X_{t_{n-1}}) \;\middle\vert{}\; \mathcal{F}_s \right], \quad \text{where } \tilde{g}_{n-1} = g_{n-1} \cdot h. \] Repeating this process backward iteratively eliminates terms \(t_{n-1}, \dots, t_1\), ultimately yielding: \[ \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = F(X_s) \quad \text{a.s.} \] for some \(F \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\). Taking conditional expectations with respect to \(X_s\) shows \(F(X_s) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_s]\) a.s., so \(Y \in \mathbb{H}\).

78.4 Conditional Independence and the Markov Property

78.5 Conditional Independence

Definition 78.4 (Conditional Independence of \(\sigma\)-Fields) Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) be a probability space and \(\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{B}, \mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}\) sub-\(\sigma\)-fields. We say that \(\mathcal{A}\) and \(\mathcal{B}\) are conditionally independent given \(\mathcal{G}\) (denoted \(\mathcal{A} \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathcal{B} \mid \mathcal{G}\)) if for all \(A \in \mathcal{A}\) and \(B \in \mathcal{B}\), \[ \mathbb{P}(A \cap B \mid \mathcal{G}) = \mathbb{P}(A \mid \mathcal{G}) \mathbb{P}(B \mid \mathcal{G}) \quad \text{a.s.} \tag{78.5}\]

Proposition 78.3 (Equivalent Characterizations) The following statements are equivalent:

  1. \(\mathcal{A} \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathcal{B} \mid \mathcal{G}\).
  2. For all \(B \in \mathcal{B}\), \(\mathbb{P}(B \mid \mathcal{G} \vee \mathcal{A}) = \mathbb{P}(B \mid \mathcal{G})\) a.s., where \(\mathcal{G} \vee \mathcal{A}\) is the smallest \(\sigma\)-field containing both \(\mathcal{G}\) and \(\mathcal{A}\).
  3. For all bounded \(\mathcal{A}\)-measurable random variables \(Z\) and bounded \(\mathcal{B}\)-measurable random variables \(Y\), \[ \mathbb{E}[Z Y \mid \mathcal{G}] = \mathbb{E}[Z \mid \mathcal{G}] \, \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{G}] \quad \text{a.s.} \]

Example 78.1 (Counterintuitive Example on Conditional Expectation) Let \(X, Y\) be i.i.d. Rademacher random variables: \(\mathbb{P}(X = 1) = \mathbb{P}(X = -1) = 1/2\). Set \(Z = XY\).

Part 1: \(X\) and \(Y\) are not conditionally independent given \(Z\).

The pair \((X, Y)\) is uniform on \(\{-1,1\}^2\), giving the joint distribution of \((X, Y, Z)\):

Since \(\mathbb{P}(Z = 1 \mid X = 1) = \mathbb{P}(Y = 1) = 1/2 = \mathbb{P}(Z = 1)\), the variable \(Z\) is independent of \(X\) (and similarly of \(Y\)) individually.

However, conditioning on \(Z\) breaks this: given \(Z = 1\), the only possible outcomes are \((X=1,Y=1)\) and \((X=-1,Y=-1)\), each with conditional probability \(1/2\). Therefore: \[ \begin{align*} &\mathbb{P}(X=1 \mid Z=1) = \tfrac{1}{2},\\ &\mathbb{P}(Y=1 \mid Z=1) = \tfrac{1}{2}, \\ &\mathbb{P}(X=1,\, Y=1 \mid Z=1) = \tfrac{1}{2}. \end{align*} \] Since \(\tfrac{1}{2} \neq \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\), the variables \(X\) and \(Y\) are not conditionally independent given \(Z\). In fact, given \(Z\), the identity \(Y = ZX\) holds deterministically, so \(X\) and \(Y\) are maximally dependent. This shows that marginal independence (\(X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y\)) does not imply conditional independence (\(X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z\)).

Part 2: Conditioning on the event \(\{X = Y\}\) vs. a \(\sigma\)-field.

The event \(\{X = Y\} = \{X=1,Y=1\} \cup \{X=-1,Y=-1\}\) has \(\mathbb{P}(X=Y) = 1/2\).

Using the definition of conditional expectation given an event: \[ \mathbb{E}[X \mid X = Y] = \frac{\mathbb{E}[X \,\mathbf{1}_{\{X=Y\}}]}{\mathbb{P}(X=Y)} = \frac{1 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} + (-1) \cdot \tfrac{1}{4}}{\tfrac{1}{2}} = 0. \] On the other hand: \[ \mathbb{E}[XY \mid X = Y] = \mathbb{E}[X^2 \mid X = Y] = 1, \] since \(X^2 = 1\) always. Thus \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid X=Y] \ne \mathbb{E}[XY \mid X=Y]\), even though \(X = Y\) on the conditioning event. This is not a contradiction: the formula \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid X=Y] = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X=Y]\) does hold (both equal \(0\)), but equality of random variables on an event does not transfer inside conditional expectations when the conditioning is on that same event.

This illustrates why in the definition of the Markov property and conditional independence, we always condition on a \(\sigma\)-field (such as \(\sigma(X_s)\)) rather than on a specific event.

78.6 The Poetic Reinterpretation of the Markov Property

The Markov property can be phrased intuitively as: “Given (conditional on) the present, the past and the future are independent.”

Theorem 78.2 (Markov Property as Conditional Independence) Let \((X_t)_{t \in T}\) be an adapted process on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \in T}, \mathbb{P})\). Then \((X_t)_{t \in T}\) satisfies the Markov property if and only if for each \(s \in T\), the past \(\sigma\)-field \(\mathcal{F}_s\) and the future \(\sigma\)-field \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X\) are conditionally independent given \(X_s\): \[ \mathcal{F}_s \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X \,\mid \sigma(X_s). \tag{78.6}\]

  • Note that \(\sigma(X_t: t \le s )\subseteq\mathcal{F}_s\) and \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X=\sigma(X_t: t \ge s)\). Thus, given \(\sigma(X_s)\), the past and future are independent.

Proof. \((\Longrightarrow)\) Assume the Markov property holds. Let \(Z \in \mathcal{B}_b(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_s)\) and \(Y \in \mathcal{B}_b(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X)\). We evaluate \(\mathbb{E}[Z Y \mid \sigma(X_s)]\): Using the tower property and the fact that \(Z\) is \(\mathcal{F}_s\)-measurable: \[ \mathbb{E}[Z Y \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = Z \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{F}_s]. \] By Theorem 78.1, \(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_s]\) a.s. Substituting this yields: \[ \mathbb{E}[Z Y \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = Z \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_s] \quad \text{a.s.} \] Now take conditional expectations on both sides with respect to \(\sigma(X_s) \subseteq \mathcal{F}_s\): \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[Z Y \mid \sigma(X_s)] &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{E}[Z Y \mid \mathcal{F}_s] \;\middle\vert{}\; \sigma(X_s) \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\left[ Z \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_s] \;\middle\vert{}\; \sigma(X_s) \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[Z \mid \sigma(X_s)] \, \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \sigma(X_s)] \quad \text{a.s.}, \end{aligned} \] where \(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_s]\) was pulled out of the conditional expectation since it is \(\sigma(X_s)\)-measurable. This establishes \(\mathcal{F}_s \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X \mid \sigma(X_s)\).

\((\Longleftarrow)\) Conversely, assume \(\mathcal{F}_s \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X \mid \sigma(X_s)\). Let \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\) and \(t > s\). Take \(Y = f(X_t) \in \mathcal{B}_b(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X)\). For any \(A \in \mathcal{F}_s\), note that \(\mathbb{I}_A\) is bounded \(\mathcal{F}_s\)-measurable and \(f(X_t)\) is bounded \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X\)-measurable. By Proposition 78.3 (condition 3) with \(\mathcal{A} = \mathcal{F}_s\), \(\mathcal{B} = \mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X\), \(\mathcal{G} = \sigma(X_s)\): \[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}_A f(X_t) \mid X_s] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}_A \mid X_s]\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] = \mathbb{P}(A \mid X_s) \, \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s]. \] Taking expectations via the tower property: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}_A f(X_t)] &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}_A f(X_t) \mid X_s] \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{P}(A \mid X_s) \, \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}_A \mid X_s] \cdot \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{I}_A \cdot \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] \;\middle\vert{}\; X_s \right] \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{I}_A \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] \right], \end{aligned} \] where the fourth equality uses that \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s]\) is \(\sigma(X_s)\)-measurable, so by the “taking out what is known” property, \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}_A \mid X_s] \cdot \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}_A \cdot \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] \mid X_s]\). Since this holds for every \(A \in \mathcal{F}_s\), by definition of conditional expectation with respect to \(\mathcal{F}_s\), we conclude: \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] \quad \text{a.s.} \] This is precisely the Markov property Equation 78.1.

78.7 Takeaways

  1. The Markov property formalises memorylessness.
    An adapted process \((X_t)\) is Markov if \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s]\) a.s. for all \(s \le t\) and bounded measurable \(f\). By Proposition 78.1, this is equivalent to the existence of a measurable \(F\) with \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = F(X_s)\) a.s. — the conditional expectation of any future functional depends on the past only through the present state value.

  2. The natural filtration is the smallest that works.
    If the Markov property holds for some filtration \((\mathcal{F}_t)\), it automatically holds for the natural filtration \(\mathcal{F}_t^X = \sigma(X_r : r \le t)\). This follows from the tower property and the \(\sigma(X_s)\)-measurability of \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s]\). The Markov property is thus a property of the process itself, not of the choice of filtration.

  3. In discrete time, one-step condition implies the full property.
    By Proposition 78.2, checking \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_{n+1}) \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = \mathbb{E}[f(X_{n+1}) \mid X_n]\) for consecutive times suffices. The extension to all \(s \le t\) is proved by induction: the Doob–Dynkin factorization \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_{n+1}) \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = q_{n+1}(X_n)\) enables the tower property to be applied backwards in time.

  4. The Markov property extends to arbitrary future functionals.
    By Theorem 78.1, for any bounded \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X\)-measurable \(Y\): \(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_s]\) a.s. The proof uses the monotone class theorem (functional Dynkin system): the class \(\mathbb{H}\) of random variables satisfying the identity is a vector space closed under bounded pointwise limits, and the multiplicative system \(\mathbb{M}\) of finite products \(\prod g_i(X_{t_i})\) generates \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X\) and is contained in \(\mathbb{H}\) by backward induction via the Markov property.

  5. Conditional independence is the structural characterisation.
    By Theorem 78.2, the Markov property is equivalent to \(\mathcal{F}_s \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X \mid \sigma(X_s)\) for every \(s\): given the present state \(X_s\), the past \(\sigma\)-field \(\mathcal{F}_s\) and the future \(\sigma\)-field \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge s}^X\) are conditionally independent. The \((\Longrightarrow)\) direction uses Theorem 78.1 to compute \(\mathbb{E}[ZY \mid \sigma(X_s)] = \mathbb{E}[Z \mid \sigma(X_s)]\,\mathbb{E}[Y \mid \sigma(X_s)]\); the \((\Longleftarrow)\) direction uses Proposition 78.3 (condition 3) and the tower property to recover \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s]\).

  6. Marginal independence does not imply conditional independence.
    The Rademacher example shows that \(X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y\) (marginal) does not prevent \(X\) and \(Y\) from being maximally dependent given \(Z = XY\). This illustrates why conditioning on a \(\sigma\)-field (such as \(\sigma(X_s)\)) is structurally distinct from marginal independence, and why the Markov property must be stated in terms of \(\sigma\)-fields rather than events.
