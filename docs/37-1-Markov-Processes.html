80  Lecture 37.1: Markov Processes

80.1 Foundations of Markov Processes

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) be a probability space and let \(T \subseteq [0, \infty)\) or \(T \subseteq \mathbb{Z}\) be the parameter set (time domain).

  • A filtration \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \in T}\) is a family of sub-\(\sigma\)-algebras of \(\mathcal{F}\) with \(\mathcal{F}_s \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t\) whenever \(s \le t\). Let \((S, \mathcal{B})\) be a measurable state space, and write \(\mathcal{B}_b(S) = \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\) for the bounded \(\mathcal{B}\)-measurable functions \(S \to \mathbb{R}\).

  • A probability kernel on \((S, \mathcal{B})\) is a map \(q : S \times \mathcal{B} \to [0, 1]\) such that \(q(x, \cdot)\) is a probability measure on \((S, \mathcal{B})\) for every \(x \in S\), and \(x \mapsto q(x, B)\) is \(\mathcal{B}\)-measurable for every \(B \in \mathcal{B}\).

  • Its associated operator acts on \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\) by \[ (Qf)(x) = \int_S f(y) \, q(x, dy), \] and \(Qf \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\) with \(\|Qf\|_\infty \le \|f\|_\infty\).

  • The composition of two kernels is the probability kernel \((q q')(x, B) = \int_S q(x, dy) \, q'(y, B)\), whose operator is \(Q Q'\).

Definition 80.1 (Adapted Process) A stochastic process \(X = \{X_t\}_{t \in T}\) defined on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) taking values in \((S, \mathcal{B})\) is said to be adapted to the filtration \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \in T}\) if \(X_t : \Omega \to S\) is \((\mathcal{F}_t, \mathcal{B})\)-measurable for each \(t \in T\).

Definition 80.2 (Markov Property) An adapted stochastic process \(X = \{X_t\}_{t \in T}\) on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \in T}, \mathbb{P})\) satisfies the Markov property (with respect to \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}\)) if, for every \(s, t \in T\) with \(s < t\) and every \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\), we have: \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] \quad \mathbb{P}\text{-almost surely.} \tag{80.1}\]

  • Suppose that \((S, \mathcal{B})\) is a standard Borel space (e.g., a Polish space equipped with its Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra). Then, for all \(s < t\) in \(T\), there exists a regular conditional distribution of \(X_t\) given \(X_s\), i.e. a probability kernel \(q_{s,t}\) on \((S, \mathcal{B})\) such that \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] = \int_S f(y) \, q_{s,t}(X_s, dy) = (Q_{s,t} f)(X_s) \quad \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.} \tag{80.2}\] for all \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\), where \(Q_{s,t}\) is the operator of \(q_{s,t}\).

  • So \(Q_{s,t}\) is the operator that takes a function of the future state \(X_t\) and produces its conditional expectation as a function of the current state \(X_s\): \[ \boxed{ \mathbb E[f(X_t)\mid X_s] = (Q_{s,t}f)(X_s). } \]

  • Existence holds for any random element \(X_t\) with values in a standard Borel space (the Markov property is not needed), and the kernel is unique only up to \(\mu_{X_s}\)-null sets, where \(\mu_{X_s} = \text{Law}(X_s)\). Throughout, “the” kernel \(q_{s,t}\) means a fixed choice of such a version.

80.2 Derivation of Transition Operators and the Chapman–Kolmogorov Property

We now investigate the properties of the transition operators \(Q_{s,t}\).

Proposition 80.1 (Chapman–Kolmogorov Equations Hold Almost Everywhere) Let \(X\) be adapted and satisfy the Markov property, let \((S, \mathcal{B})\) be standard Borel, and let \(q_{s,t}\) (\(s < t\)) be regular conditional distributions as in Equation 80.2. Then for all \(r < s < t\) in \(T\) and every \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\), \[ Q_{r,t} f = Q_{r,s} (Q_{s,t} f) \quad \mu_{X_r}\text{-almost everywhere.} \tag{80.3}\] Moreover, there is a \(\mu_{X_r}\)-null set \(N\) (depending on \(r,s,t\) but not on \(B\)) such that \[ q_{r,t}(x, B) = \int_S q_{r,s}(x, dy) \, q_{s,t}(y, B), \tag{80.4}\] for all \(x \in S \setminus N\) and all \(B \in \mathcal{B}\).

Proof (Proof of Proposition 80.1). Since \(X\) is adapted and the filtration is increasing, \(\sigma(X_r) \subseteq \mathcal{F}_r \subseteq \mathcal{F}_s\). The function \(Q_{s,t} f\) is bounded and measurable, so by the tower property, the Markov property, and Equation 80.2 applied to \(f\) at times \((s,t)\) and to \(Q_{s,t}f\) at times \((r,s)\): \[ \begin{aligned} (Q_{r,t} f)(X_r) &= \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_r] \\ &= \mathbb{E} \Big[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] \;\Big|\; X_r \Big] \\ &= \mathbb{E} \Big[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] \;\Big|\; X_r \Big] \\ &= \mathbb{E} \Big[ (Q_{s,t} f)(X_s) \;\Big|\; X_r \Big] \\ &= \big(Q_{r,s}(Q_{s,t} f)\big)(X_r) \quad \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.} \end{aligned} \tag{80.5}\] Put \(h = Q_{r,t} f - Q_{r,s}(Q_{s,t} f)\), a bounded measurable function on \(S\). Then \(\mu_{X_r}(h \neq 0) = \mathbb{P}(h(X_r) \neq 0) = 0\), which is Equation 80.3.

(The left-hand side does not depend on the version chosen for \(Q_{s,t}f\): since \(\mu_{X_s} = \mu_{X_r} q_{r,s}\), two versions that agree \(\mu_{X_s}\)-a.e. have images under \(Q_{r,s}\) that agree \(\mu_{X_r}\)-a.e.)

For the kernel form, the difficulty is that Equation 80.3 only gives, for each fixed \(B\), a null set \(N_B\) outside of which equality holds for that \(B\); since \(\mathcal{B}\) is uncountable, naively taking \(N = \bigcup_{B \in \mathcal{B}} N_B\) could fail to be null. The standard Borel hypothesis repairs this, by letting us reduce to countably many \(B\)’s before taking the union, and then use Dynkin’s theorem to recover all of \(\mathcal B\).

  • A countable generating \(\pi\)-system. Since \((S,\mathcal{B})\) is standard Borel, it is Borel-isomorphic to a Polish space with its Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra, which has a countable base \(\{U_n\}_{n \ge 1}\) of open sets. Let \(\mathcal{C}\) be the collection of all finite intersections \(U_{n_1} \cap \cdots \cap U_{n_k}\) together with \(S\) itself. Then \(\mathcal{C}\) is countable, closed under finite intersections (a \(\pi\)-system), and \(\sigma(\mathcal{C}) = \mathcal{B}\).

  • One null set per \(B \in \mathcal{C}\), then a countable union. For each \(B \in \mathcal{C}\), apply Equation 80.3 with \(f = \mathbf{1}_B\): by Remark 80.1 this produces a \(\mu_{X_r}\)-null set \(N_B\) such that \(q_{r,t}(x,B) = (q_{r,s}q_{s,t})(x,B)\) for all \(x \notin N_B\). Set \[ N := \bigcup_{B \in \mathcal{C}} N_B. \] Because \(\mathcal{C}\) is countable, \(N\) is a countable union of \(\mu_{X_r}\)-null sets, so \(\mu_{X_r}(N) \le \sum_{B \in \mathcal{C}} \mu_{X_r}(N_B) = 0\). Crucially, \(N\) was built by exhausting all of \(\mathcal{C}\) in advance, so it no longer depends on \(B\).

  • Agreement on \(\mathcal{C}\), for a fixed \(x\). Fix any \(x \in S \setminus N\). Since \(N = \bigcup_{B \in \mathcal{C}} N_B\), this single \(x\) avoids every \(N_B\) simultaneously, so \[ q_{r,t}(x,B) = (q_{r,s}q_{s,t})(x,B) \qquad \text{for all } B \in \mathcal{C}. \] (This is the key swap of quantifiers: having fixed \(x\) first, we now let \(B\) range over all of \(\mathcal{C}\), rather than fixing \(B\) first and asking for a.e. \(x\).)

  • Extension from \(\mathcal{C}\) to \(\mathcal{B}\) via Dynkin. For this fixed \(x\), the set functions \(\nu_1(B) := q_{r,t}(x,B)\) and \(\nu_2(B) := (q_{r,s}q_{s,t})(x,B) = \int_S q_{s,t}(y,B)\,q_{r,s}(x,dy)\) are both probability measures on \((S,\mathcal{B})\): \(\nu_1\) because \(q_{r,t}(x,\cdot)\) is a probability measure, and \(\nu_2\) because \(q_{s,t}(y,\cdot)\) is countably additive in \(B\) for each \(y\) with total mass \(1\), so integrating term by term against \(q_{r,s}(x,dy)\) (monotone convergence) shows \(\nu_2\) is countably additive with \(\nu_2(S) = 1\). Since \(\nu_1 = \nu_2\) on the \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal{C}\) and both are finite measures, Dynkin’s \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) theorem gives \(\nu_1 = \nu_2\) on \(\sigma(\mathcal{C}) = \mathcal{B}\), i.e. \[ q_{r,t}(x,B) = (q_{r,s}q_{s,t})(x,B) \qquad \text{for all } B \in \mathcal{B}. \]

As \(x \in S \setminus N\) was arbitrary, this is Equation 80.4, with \(N\) depending only on \(r,s,t\) and not on \(B\).

Remark 80.1. For \(f=\mathbf 1_B\), \[ Q_{r,t}\mathbf 1_B(x) = q_{r,t}(x,B), \] while \[ \begin{aligned} Q_{r,s}(Q_{s,t}\mathbf 1_B)(x) &= \int_S Q_{s,t}\mathbf 1_B(y)\,q_{r,s}(x,dy)\\ &= \int_S q_{s,t}(y,B)\,q_{r,s}(x,dy)\\ &=(q_{r,s}q_{s,t})(x,B). \end{aligned} \] Thus, \(Q_{r,t}\mathbf 1_B = Q_{r,s} (Q_{s,t} \mathbf 1_B)\) gives \(q_{r,t}(x,B) = (q_{r,s}q_{s,t})(x,B)\) for \(\mu_{X_r}\)-a.e. \(x\), for each fixed \(B\).

The standard Borel assumption allows this conclusion to be strengthened: there exists a single \(\mu_{X_r}\)-null set \(N\) such that the equality holds for every \(x\notin N\) and every \(B\in\mathcal B\). Indeed, choose a countable generating \(\pi\)-system for \(\mathcal B\), take the union of the corresponding null sets, and use Dynkin’s \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) theorem to extend the equality from the generating \(\pi\)-system to all of \(\mathcal B\).

Integrating Equation 80.3 against \(\mu_{X_r}\) gives only the weaker identity \(\int Q_{r,t} f \, d\mu_{X_r} = \int Q_{r,s} Q_{s,t} f \, d\mu_{X_r}\) (equality of the laws of \(X_t\) computed via both kernels), which does not characterize Equation 80.4: with \(S = \{0,1\}\), \(\mu\) uniform, \(q_{r,t} = I\) and \(q_{r,s} q_{s,t}\) equal to the swap matrix, both sides agree for all \(f\) although the kernels differ everywhere. The correct joint-law version tests against functions of both coordinates: for all bounded measurable \(g, f\), \[ \int_S g(x) \, (Q_{r,t} f)(x) \, \mu_{X_r}(dx) = \int_S g(x) \, \big(Q_{r,s} Q_{s,t} f\big)(x) \, \mu_{X_r}(dx), \tag{80.6}\] i.e. \(\mu_{X_r} \otimes q_{r,t} = \mu_{X_r} \otimes (q_{r,s} q_{s,t})\) as measures on \(S \times S\).

Remark 80.2 (The Chapman–Kolmogorov Equation). The key idea is that to calculate the probability of being in \(B\) at time \(t\), given the state \(x\) at time \(r\), you can condition on the intermediate state at time \(s\) and average over all possible intermediate states. The Chapman–Kolmogorov equation expresses this idea mathematically: \[ q_{r,t}(x,B)=\int_S q_{s,t}(y,B)\,q_{r,s}(x,dy). \]

  • Left-hand side: \(q_{r,t}(x,B)\) is the probability of being in \(B\) at time \(t\), starting from state \(x\) at time \(r\).

  • First kernel on the right: \(q_{r,s}(x,dy)\) is the distribution of the intermediate state \(X_s\), given \(X_r=x\).

  • Second kernel on the right: \(q_{s,t}(y,B)\) is the probability of reaching \(B\) at time \(t\), starting from state \(y\) at time \(s\).

The integral averages the last quantity over the distribution of the intermediate state \(y\).

Intuitively, Proposition 80.1 says something very simple: If \(X\) is Markov, then predicting the future from the present can be done in stages: first predict the intermediate state, and then predict the later state from that intermediate state. Suppose \(r<s<t\). Think of the process as \(X_r \longrightarrow X_s \longrightarrow X_t\).

The kernel \(q_{r,t}(x,\cdot)\) describes the distribution of \(X_t\) when the process is currently at \(x\) at time \(r\). The Chapman–Kolmogorov result says that this distribution can be obtained by:

Move from \(x\) at time \(r\) to the possible states \(y\) at time \(s\) according to \(q_{r,s}(x,dy)\). From each such \(y\), move to the possible states at time \(t\) according to \(q_{s,t}(y,dz)\). Average over all possible intermediate states \(y\). In symbols, \[ q_{r,t}(x,C) = \int_S q_{s,t}(y,C)\,q_{r,s}(x,dy), \] for \(\mu_{X_r}\)-almost every \(x\).

Why is this a Markov property? The crucial idea is that once we know \(X_s\), the earlier state \(X_r\) contains no additional information about \(X_t\). Schematically, \(X_r \quad\longrightarrow\quad X_s \quad\longrightarrow\quad X_t\). Thus, to predict \(X_t\) from \(X_r\), we do not need to carry the entire history from \(r\) to \(s\). We can simply pass through the intermediate state \(X_s\). For a function \(f\), the same idea appears even more clearly: \[ \boxed{ Q_{r,t}f = Q_{r,s}(Q_{s,t}f) \qquad \mu_{X_r}\text{-a.e.} } \] Here \(Q_{s,t}f(y)\) is the expected value of \(f(X_t)\) if the process is at \(y\) at time \(s\). Then \(Q_{r,s}\) averages those conditional expectations over the possible values of \(X_s\) given \(X_r=x\). So it is essentially the tower property combined with the Markov property: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[f(X_t)\mid X_r] &= \mathbb E\left[ \mathbb E[f(X_t)\mid X_s] \mid X_r \right]\\ &= \mathbb E\left[ (Q_{s,t}f)(X_s) \mid X_r \right]\\ &= (Q_{r,s}Q_{s,t}f)(X_r). \end{aligned} \] A useful interpretation of the operators is the following. You can think of \(Q_{r,t}\) as a prediction operator: \[ f \quad\xrightarrow{\quad Q_{r,t}\quad}\quad \text{expected value of }f(X_t)\text{ given }X_r. \] Then Chapman–Kolmogorov says that prediction over a long interval is equivalent to successive prediction over shorter intervals: \[ \boxed{ \text{prediction from }r\text{ to }t = \text{prediction from }r\text{ to }s \;\circ\; \text{prediction from }s\text{ to }t. } \] That is the fundamental intuition behind the semigroup/transition-operator viewpoint. One subtlety the proposition captures especially well is that, because regular conditional distributions are only defined up to null sets, this equality is initially an almost-everywhere statement, rather than necessarily a pointwise statement for every \(x\in S\).

80.3 Markov Transition Kernels and Markov Processes

Definition 80.3 (Markov Transition Kernels and Operators) A collection of probability kernels \(\{q_{s,t}\}_{s \le t,\; s,t \in T}\) on \((S, \mathcal{B})\) (with corresponding operators \(\{Q_{s,t}\}_{s \le t}\) acting on \(\mathcal{B}_b(S)\)) is called a family of Markov transition kernels (operators) if:

  1. For all \(s \in T\) and all \(x \in S\), \(q_{s,s}(x, \cdot) = \delta_x(\cdot)\) (or \(Q_{s,s} = \text{Id}\)).
  2. For all \(r \le s \le t\) in \(T\) and all \(x \in S\), the Chapman–Kolmogorov equations hold pointwise: \[ Q_{r,t} = Q_{r,s} Q_{s,t}, \tag{80.7}\] which is explicitly written in kernel form as: \[ q_{r,t}(x, B) = \int_S q_{r,s}(x, dy) \, q_{s,t}(y, B) \quad \forall B \in \mathcal{B}, \, \forall x \in S. \tag{80.8}\]

(The operator and kernel forms are equivalent by the standard monotone class argument: kernels are determined by their action on indicators, and operators by their action on \(\mathcal{B}_b(S)\).)

Definition 80.4 (Markov Process) Given a filtered probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \in T}, \mathbb{P})\) and a state space \((S, \mathcal{B})\), an adapted stochastic process \(X = \{X_t\}_{t \in T}\) is a Markov process (with respect to \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}\)) if it satisfies the Markov property (Equation 80.1) and there exists a family of Markov transition operators \(\{Q_{s,t}\}_{s \le t}\) in the sense of Definition 80.3 such that: \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] = (Q_{s,t} f)(X_s) \quad \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.} \quad \forall s \le t \text{ in } T, \, \forall f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S). \tag{80.9}\]

(For \(s = t\), Equation 80.9 is trivial since \(f(X_s)\) is \(\sigma(X_s)\)-measurable.)

Example 80.1 (Why Both Clauses of Definition 80.4 Are Needed) Let \(Z_0, Z_1\) be i.i.d. uniform on \(\{0,1\}\), set \(Z_2 = Z_0 \oplus Z_1\) (addition modulo \(2\)), and put \(X_0 = Z_0\), \(X_1 = Z_1\), \(X_2 = Z_2\) on \(T = \{0,1,2\}\) with \(\mathcal{F}_t = \sigma(X_0, \dots, X_t)\). Then \((Z_0, Z_1, Z_2)\) are pairwise independent, so in particular \(X_1\) is independent of \(X_0\), and, given \(X_1 = y\), \(X_2 = Z_0 \oplus y\) with \(Z_0\) independent of \(X_1\).

Take \(q_{0,1}(x, \cdot)\) to be uniform on \(\{0,1\}\) (not depending on \(x\)) and \(q_{1,2}(y, \{z\}) = \tfrac12\) for \(z \in \{0,1\}\) (not depending on \(y\)). Both reproduce Equation 80.9: \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_1) \mid X_0] = \tfrac12(f(0)+f(1)) = (Q_{0,1}f)(X_0)\) since \(X_1 \perp X_0\), and \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_2) \mid X_1] = \tfrac12(f(0)+f(1)) = (Q_{1,2}f)(X_1)\) since \(X_2 = X_1 \oplus Z_0\) with \(Z_0 \perp X_1\) uniform. Composing, \((q_{0,1}q_{1,2})(x, \cdot)\) is again uniform on \(\{0,1\}\) for every \(x\), which coincides with \(q_{0,2}(x,\cdot) := \mathrm{Law}(X_2 \mid X_0 = x)\) (also uniform on \(\{0,1\}\), since \(X_2 = X_0 \oplus Z_1\) with \(Z_1 \perp X_0\) uniform). So Chapman–Kolmogorov holds pointwise, and this family satisfies Equation 80.9 at every pair \((s,t)\).

Yet \(X\) is not Markov: \(X_2 = X_0 \oplus X_1\) is a deterministic, non-constant function of \(\mathcal{F}_1 = \sigma(X_0, X_1)\), so \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_2) \mid \mathcal{F}_1] = f(X_0 \oplus X_1)\) depends on \(X_0\) for non-constant \(f\), whereas \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_2) \mid X_1] = \tfrac12(f(0)+f(1))\) does not; these disagree, so Equation 80.1 fails at \((s,t) = (1,2)\). Hence a Chapman–Kolmogorov-consistent transition family satisfying Equation 80.9 is not enough to make \(X\) a Markov process — Definition 80.4 imposes the Markov property as a genuinely separate requirement.

80.4 Independent Increment Processes

In this section \(S = \mathbb{R}^d\) with its Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra (more generally, a measurable group), so that addition and subtraction are measurable.

Definition 80.5 (Independent Increment Process) Let \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}\) be a filtration. An adapted process \(X = \{X_t\}_{t \in T}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) has independent increments (with respect to \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}\)) if, for all \(s < t\) in \(T\), the increment \(X_t - X_s\) is independent of \(\mathcal{F}_s\). When no filtration is mentioned, \(\mathcal{F}_t = \mathcal{F}_t^X = \sigma(X_u : u \le t)\).

Remark 80.3 (On the Definition). In particular, for \(t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n\) the increments \(X_{t_1} - X_{t_0}, \dots, X_{t_n} - X_{t_{n-1}}\) are independent. This weaker property alone is not sufficient for the Markov property, because it says nothing about the initial value: with \(T = \{0,1\}\), \(X_0 = \pm 1\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, and \(X_1 = 2X_0\), there is a single increment \(X_1 - X_0 = X_0\), which is trivially “independent”, yet it is not independent of \(\mathcal{F}_0^X = \sigma(X_0)\).

If \(T\) has a least element \(t_*\), the definition above is equivalent to: for all \(t_* < t_1 < \dots < t_n\) the random variables \(X_{t_*}, X_{t_1} - X_{t_*}, X_{t_2} - X_{t_1}, \dots, X_{t_n} - X_{t_{n-1}}\) are independent. Thus random walks require \(X_0\) independent of the steps, and Poisson processes (with \(X_0 = 0\)) are examples.

Proposition 80.2 (Independent Increment Processes are Markovian) Let \(X = \{X_t\}_{t \in T}\) be an \(\mathbb{R}^d\)-valued process with independent increments with respect to \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}\). Then \(X\) is a Markov process with respect to \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}\), and the transition kernel is given by: \[ q_{s,t}(x, B) = \mathbb{P}\left( x + X_t - X_s \in B \right), \, s \le t, \; x \in \mathbb{R}^d, \, B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d), \] and this family satisfies the Chapman–Kolmogorov equations at every \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\).

Proof (Proof of Proposition 80.2).

  • Step 0 (a \(\sigma\)-algebra form of the freezing lemma). We will repeatedly use the following generalization of Proposition 71.2: if \(Z\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable, \(W\) is independent of \(\mathcal{G}\) (not merely of \(Z\)), and \(\phi \in \mathcal{B}_b(\mathbb{R}^d \times \mathbb{R}^d)\), then \[ \mathbb{E}[\phi(Z,W) \mid \mathcal{G}] = \psi(Z) \text{ a.s.}, \qquad \psi(z) := \mathbb{E}[\phi(z,W)], \] and consequently, taking total expectation, \(\mathbb{E}[\phi(Z,W)] = \mathbb{E}[\psi(Z)]\). Indeed, for any bounded \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable \(h\), the pair \((Z,h)\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable, hence independent of \(W\) (since \(W \perp \mathcal{G}\)); applying Fubini–Tonelli to the product measure \(\mu_{(Z,h)} \otimes \mu_W\), exactly as in the proof of Proposition 71.2 but with \(h\) in place of the test function there, gives \(\mathbb{E}[\phi(Z,W) h] = \mathbb{E}[\psi(Z) h]\) for every such \(h\), which is the defining property of \(\mathbb{E}[\phi(Z,W) \mid \mathcal{G}] = \psi(Z)\).

  • Step 1 (the kernel is well defined and measurable). Fix \(s \le t\), let \(\Delta = X_t - X_s\) with law \(\mu_\Delta\), and fix \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\). Then \(q_{s,t}(x,B) = \mathbb{P}(x + \Delta \in B) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{1}_B(x+z) \, \mu_\Delta(dz)\). For fixed \(x\), \(B \mapsto q_{s,t}(x,B)\) is the law of \(x + \Delta\), hence a probability measure. For fixed \(B\), the map \((x,z) \mapsto \mathbf{1}_B(x+z)\) is the indicator of the preimage of \(B\) under the continuous (hence Borel) addition map, so it is jointly measurable and nonnegative; by Tonelli’s theorem, \(x \mapsto \int \mathbf{1}_B(x+z)\,\mu_\Delta(dz) = q_{s,t}(x,B)\) is Borel measurable. So \(q_{s,t}\) is a probability kernel.

  • Step 2 (Markov property and Equation 80.9). Fix \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(\mathbb{R}^d)\) and \(s \le t\), and let \(\Delta = X_t - X_s\), so \(X_t = X_s + \Delta\) with \(X_s\) being \(\mathcal{F}_s\)-measurable (as \(X\) is adapted). If \(s = t\), then \(\Delta = 0\) a.s., so \(H(x) := \mathbb{E}[f(x+\Delta)] = f(x)\), and since \(f(X_t) = f(X_s)\) is already \(\sigma(X_s)\)-measurable, \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = f(X_s) = H(X_s)\) trivially — no independence is needed in this degenerate case. If \(s < t\), then by definition of independent increments \(\Delta\) is independent of \(\mathcal{F}_s\); the map \(\phi(x,z) = f(x+z)\) is bounded and jointly measurable (addition is continuous, \(f\) is Borel), so Step 0 with \(\mathcal{G} = \mathcal{F}_s\), \(Z = X_s\), \(W = \Delta\) gives \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = H(X_s), \qquad H(x) = \mathbb{E}[f(x+\Delta)] = \int f(y) \, q_{s,t}(x,dy). \] In either case \(H(X_s)\) is \(\sigma(X_s)\)-measurable, and \(\sigma(X_s) \subseteq \mathcal{F}_s\), so the tower property gives \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] \mid X_s] = \mathbb{E}[H(X_s) \mid X_s] = H(X_s)\). Comparing the two displays, \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t)\mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s]\), which is Equation 80.1, and \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] = H(X_s) = (Q_{s,t}f)(X_s)\), which is Equation 80.9.

  • Step 3 (Definition 80.3, part 1). Taking \(s=t\) in Step 1, \(\Delta = X_s - X_s = 0\) a.s., so \(q_{s,s}(x,B) = \mathbb{P}(x \in B) = \mathbf{1}_B(x)\), i.e. \(q_{s,s}(x,\cdot) = \delta_x\).

  • Step 4 (Definition 80.3, part 2: pointwise Chapman–Kolmogorov). Fix \(r \le s \le t\), \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\), \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\). Let \(Y := x + (X_s - X_r)\) and \(\Delta_2 := X_t - X_s\); by Step 1 applied to \((r,s)\), \(Y\) has law \(q_{r,s}(x,\cdot)\). Since \(X\) is adapted and \(\mathcal{F}_r \subseteq \mathcal{F}_s\), both \(X_r\) and \(X_s\) (hence \(X_s - X_r\), and hence \(Y\)) are \(\mathcal{F}_s\)-measurable; and \(\Delta_2\) is independent of \(\mathcal{F}_s\) (trivially if \(s=t\), as in Step 2; by definition of independent increments if \(s < t\)). By Step 1 applied to \((s,t)\), \(y \mapsto q_{s,t}(y,B)\) is bounded and measurable, so by the change-of-variables formula (law of the unconscious statistician) applied to the law \(q_{r,s}(x,\cdot)\) of \(Y\), \[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} q_{r,s}(x,dy)\, q_{s,t}(y,B) = \mathbb{E}[q_{s,t}(Y,B)]. \] Now apply Step 0, in its total-expectation form, with \(\mathcal{G} = \mathcal{F}_s\), \(Z = Y\), \(W = \Delta_2\), \(\phi(y,w) = \mathbf{1}_B(y+w)\): since \(q_{s,t}(Y,B) = \psi(Y)\) with \(\psi(y) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_B(y+\Delta_2)]\), \[ \mathbb{E}[q_{s,t}(Y,B)] = \mathbb{E}[\psi(Y)] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_B(Y + \Delta_2)] = \mathbb{P}(Y + \Delta_2 \in B). \] Since \(Y + \Delta_2 = x + (X_s - X_r) + (X_t - X_s) = x + (X_t - X_r)\), this equals \(\mathbb{P}(x + (X_t-X_r) \in B) = q_{r,t}(x,B)\). Hence \[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} q_{r,s}(x,dy)\, q_{s,t}(y,B) = q_{r,t}(x,B) \] for every \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and every \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\) — i.e. Equation 80.8 holds pointwise, not merely \(\mu_{X_r}\)-almost everywhere as in the general setting of Proposition 80.1.

80.5 Discrete-Time Parameterization and Discrete State Spaces

Let us now focus on discrete time \(T = \mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}\). The family of transition operators is \(\{Q_{m,n} : 0 \le m \le n < \infty\}\).

By the (pointwise) Chapman–Kolmogorov equations and induction on \(n - m\): \[ Q_{m,n} = Q_{m,m+1} Q_{m+1,m+2} \cdots Q_{n-1,n}. \tag{80.10}\]

Hence, in discrete time, a transition family is determined by the one-step operators \(\{Q_{m,m+1} : m \in \mathbb{N}\}\). Conversely, any sequence of probability kernels \(\{q_{m,m+1}\}_{m \in \mathbb{N}}\), together with \(q_{m,m} = \delta_x\) and \(q_{m,n}\) defined by composition, satisfies Definition 80.3. In particular, in discrete time the pointwise requirement of Definition 80.3 is automatic once the one-step kernels are chosen (for \(S\) standard Borel, with one-step kernels taken as regular conditional distributions).

80.6 Discrete State Space

If \(S\) is countable (equipped with the discrete \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{B} = 2^S\)), write \(q_{m,n}(x, y) := q_{m,n}(x, \{y\})\), so that \[ q_{m,m+1}(x, B) = \sum_{y \in B} q_{m,m+1}(x, y), \tag{80.11}\] where \(q_{m,m+1}(x, y)\) is a regular conditional distribution of \(X_{m+1}\) given \(X_m\), characterized by \[ \begin{aligned} q_{m,m+1}(x, y) &= \mathbb{P}(X_{m+1} = y \mid X_m = x) \\ &= \frac{\mathbb{P}(X_{m+1} = y, X_m = x)}{\mathbb{P}(X_m = x)}, \end{aligned} \tag{80.12}\] whenever \(\mathbb{P}(X_m = x) > 0\). For \(x\) with \(\mathbb{P}(X_m = x) = 0\) the row \(q_{m,m+1}(x, \cdot)\) is not determined by the process; it may be any probability vector, and the pointwise convention of Definition 80.3 simply fixes one such choice.

In this framework, for \(n \ge m + 2\) the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation represents a sum over intermediate states (“sum over paths”): \[ \begin{aligned} &q_{m,n}(x, y) = \sum_{x_{m+1} \in S} \sum_{x_{m+2} \in S} \dots \\ &\sum_{x_{n-1} \in S} q_{m,m+1}(x, x_{m+1}) q_{m+1,m+2}(x_{m+1}, x_{m+2}) \cdots q_{n-1,n}(x_{n-1}, y). \end{aligned} \tag{80.13}\] (For \(n = m+1\) there are no intermediate sums, and for \(n = m\) one has \(q_{m,m}(x,y) = \mathbf{1}_{\{x = y\}}\).) The order of the countable sums is immaterial since all terms are nonnegative.

80.7 Finite-Dimensional Distributions

Let \((X_t)_{t \in T}\) be a Markov process (in the sense of Definition 80.4) with transition kernels \(\{q_{s,t}\}_{s \le t}\). Fix arbitrary ordered time points \(t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n\) in \(T\), and let \(\nu_0 = \text{Law}(X_{t_0})\) be the initial law. We write \(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_0}[h] := \int_S h \, d\nu_0\) for \(h \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\).

The measure on the right of Equation 80.14 below is the probability measure on \((S^{n+1}, \mathcal{B}^{\otimes(n+1)})\) defined by iterated integration of the kernels: \[ A \mapsto \int \nu_0(dx_0) \int q_{t_0,t_1}(x_0, dx_1) \cdots \int q_{t_{n-1},t_n}(x_{n-1}, dx_n) \, \mathbf{1}_A(x_0, \dots, x_n). \]

Proposition 80.3 (Finite-Dimensional Distributions) The joint distribution \(\text{Law}(X_{t_0}, X_{t_1}, \dots, X_{t_n})\) on \(S^{n+1}\) is given by: \[ \begin{aligned} &\text{Law}(X_{t_0}, X_{t_1}, \dots, X_{t_n})(dx_0, dx_1, \dots, dx_n) \\ &= \nu_0(dx_0) q_{t_0, t_1}(x_0, dx_1) q_{t_1, t_2}(x_1, dx_2) \cdots q_{t_{n-1}, t_n}(x_{n-1}, dx_n). \end{aligned} \tag{80.14}\] Equivalently, for all bounded measurable functions \(f_0, f_1, \dots, f_n \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\): \[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}\left[ \prod_{i=0}^n f_i(X_{t_i}) \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{\nu_0} \left[ f_0 Q_{t_0, t_1} \left( f_1 Q_{t_1, t_2} \left( \cdots (f_{n-1} Q_{t_{n-1}, t_n} f_n) \cdots \right) \right) \right], \end{aligned} \tag{80.15}\] where for \(n = 0\) the right-hand side is read as \(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_0}[f_0]\).

Proof (Proof of Proposition 80.3). We prove Equation 80.15 by induction on \(n\), simultaneously for all time points \(t_0 < \dots < t_n\) and all bounded measurable \(f_0, \dots, f_n\).

Base Case (\(n=0\)): \(\mathbb{E}[f_0(X_{t_0})] = \int_S f_0 \, d\nu_0 = \mathbb{E}_{\nu_0}[f_0]\) by definition of \(\nu_0\).

Inductive Step: Assume Equation 80.15 holds for \(n\) (all tuples of \(n+1\) times and all bounded measurable functions). Consider \(n+2\) time points \(t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n < t_{n+1}\). Since \(X\) is adapted and the filtration is increasing, \(\prod_{i=0}^n f_i(X_{t_i})\) is bounded and \(\mathcal{F}_{t_n}\)-measurable, so by the tower property and “taking out what is known”: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E} \left[ \prod_{i=0}^{n+1} f_i(X_{t_i}) \right] &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \prod_{i=0}^{n+1} f_i(X_{t_i}) \;\middle|\; \mathcal{F}_{t_n} \right] \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \left( \prod_{i=0}^n f_i(X_{t_i}) \right) \mathbb{E} \left[ f_{n+1}(X_{t_{n+1}}) \;\middle|\; \mathcal{F}_{t_n} \right] \right]. \end{aligned} \]

By the Markov property at \((t_n, t_{n+1})\) and Equation 80.9: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[f_{n+1}(X_{t_{n+1}}) \mid \mathcal{F}_{t_n}] &= \mathbb{E}[f_{n+1}(X_{t_{n+1}}) \mid X_{t_n}] \\ &= (Q_{t_n, t_{n+1}} f_{n+1})(X_{t_n}) \quad \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.} \end{aligned} \tag{80.16}\]

Define \(g_n \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\) as: \[ g_n(x) := f_n(x) \cdot (Q_{t_n, t_{n+1}} f_{n+1})(x). \tag{80.17}\]

Substituting Equation 80.16 and Equation 80.17 gives: \[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \prod_{i=0}^{n+1} f_i(X_{t_i}) \right] = \mathbb{E} \left[ \left( \prod_{i=0}^{n-1} f_i(X_{t_i}) \right) g_n(X_{t_n}) \right] \tag{80.18}\] (for \(n = 0\) the empty product is \(1\)). By the induction hypothesis applied to the \(n+1\) times \(t_0 < \dots < t_n\) and the functions \(f_0, \dots, f_{n-1}, g_n\), the right-hand side equals \[ \mathbb{E}_{\nu_0} \left[ f_0 Q_{t_0, t_1} \left( f_1 Q_{t_1, t_2} \left( \cdots (f_{n-1} Q_{t_{n-1}, t_n} g_n) \cdots \right) \right) \right]. \] Replacing \(g_n\) with \(f_n Q_{t_n, t_{n+1}} f_{n+1}\) yields \[ \mathbb{E}_{\nu_0} \left[ f_0 Q_{t_0, t_1} \left( f_1 Q_{t_1, t_2} \left( \cdots \Big( f_{n-1} Q_{t_{n-1}, t_n} (f_n Q_{t_n, t_{n+1}} f_{n+1}) \Big) \cdots \right) \right) \right], \] which is Equation 80.15 for \(n+1\).

From Equation 80.15 to Equation 80.14: Unwinding the nested expression by iterated integration of the kernels shows that its right-hand side equals \(\int \prod_i f_i \, d\pi\), where \(\pi\) is the iterated-kernel measure defined before the proposition. Taking \(f_i = \mathbf{1}_{B_i}\), the laws \(\text{Law}(X_{t_0}, \dots, X_{t_n})\) and \(\pi\) agree on measurable rectangles \(B_0 \times \dots \times B_n\). These form a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\mathcal{B}^{\otimes(n+1)}\), so the two probability measures coincide by Dynkin’s \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) theorem. \(\blacksquare\)

The proof uses only the Markov property and Equation 80.9; it does not depend on which version of the kernels is used, since different versions differ only on null sets of the relevant marginal laws.

80.8 Takeaways

  • Markov property. An adapted process is Markov with respect to \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}\) if conditioning on the whole past \(\mathcal{F}_s\) is the same as conditioning on the present \(X_s\), for every bounded measurable test function.
  • Transition kernels. On a standard Borel state space, \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid X_s] = (Q_{s,t}f)(X_s)\) for a probability kernel \(q_{s,t}\) that is unique only \(\mu_{X_s}\)-almost everywhere.
  • Chapman–Kolmogorov. The Markov property implies \(Q_{r,t} = Q_{r,s}Q_{s,t}\) only almost everywhere with respect to \(\mu_{X_r}\), with exceptional sets depending on \((r,s,t)\). For this reason a Markov process is defined via a transition family satisfying Chapman–Kolmogorov at every point.
  • Both clauses are needed. A Chapman–Kolmogorov family satisfying Equation 80.9 does not make a process Markov; Example 80.1 (the pairwise independent triple \(X_2 = X_0 \oplus X_1\)) shows that the Markov property is a genuinely separate condition on the joint law.
  • Independent increments. An \(\mathbb{R}^d\)-valued process whose increments are independent of the past (including the initial value) is Markov with the translation kernel \(q_{s,t}(x,B) = \mathbb{P}(x + X_t - X_s \in B)\). Independence of the increments among themselves is not enough.
  • Discrete time. The one-step kernels determine everything via \(Q_{m,n} = Q_{m,m+1} \cdots Q_{n-1,n}\), and the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation becomes a sum over intermediate states (matrix multiplication for countable \(S\)).
  • Finite-dimensional distributions. The law of \((X_{t_0}, \dots, X_{t_n})\) is \(\nu_0(dx_0)\,q_{t_0,t_1}(x_0,dx_1)\cdots q_{t_{n-1},t_n}(x_{n-1},dx_n)\). Hence the initial law and the transition kernels determine the law of the process on the product \(\sigma\)-algebra.
