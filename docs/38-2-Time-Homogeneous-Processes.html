83  Lecture 38.2: Time-Homogeneous Markov Processes

83.1 Introduction and Motivation

In time-homogeneous processes, transition kernels depend on time \(s\) and \(t\) only through the time elapsed \(t - s\). We illustrate this via two standard motivating examples before formalizing operator semigroups and transition dynamics.

Example 83.1 (Poisson Process Transition Kernel) Consider a Poisson process \((N_t)_{t \ge 0}\) with intensity parameter \(\lambda > 0\). The transition kernel \(q_{s,t}(n, B)\) for \(s \le t\), \(n \in \mathbb{N}_0\), and \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{N}_0)\) is given by \[ q_{s,t}(n, B) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbf{1}_B(n + N_t - N_s) \right] = \sum_{m \in B} \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda(t - s))(\{m - n\}). \] Since the distribution of \(N_t - N_s\) is \(\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda(t - s))\), \(q_{s,t}\) depends on \(s\) and \(t\) solely through the difference \(t - s\), so \(q_{s,t} = q_{0, t-s}\).

Example 83.2 ((Pre-) Brownian Motion Transition Kernel) Consider a (pre-) Brownian motion \((B_t)_{t \ge 0}\). Its transition kernel \(q_{s,t}(x, A)\) for \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(A \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) is \[ q_{s,t}(x, A) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbf{1}_A(x + B_t - B_s) \right]. \] Because \(B_t - B_s \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t - s)\), the transition kernel \(q_{s,t}\) depends on \(s, t\) only through \(t - s\).

83.2 Time Homogeneity and One-Parameter Semigroups

Throughout, \(T\) denotes the totally ordered index set with least element \(0\) fixed in Lecture 38.1 (e.g. \(T = [0,\infty)\) or \(T = \mathbb{N}_0\)), so that for \(s \le t \in T\) the difference \(t - s\) and, for \(s,t\ge0\), the sum \(s+t\) again lie in \(T\). We also recall from Lectures 37.1–38.1 that any family of Markov transition operators \(\{Q_{s,t}\}_{s \le t \in T}\) on \((S,\mathcal{B}(S))\) is assumed to satisfy the (general, not-yet-homogeneous) Chapman-Kolmogorov equations \[ Q_{r,s} Q_{s,t} = Q_{r,t}, \quad \forall r \le s \le t \in T. \tag{83.1}\]

Definition 83.1 (Time-Homogeneous Markov Transition Operators) Let \(\{Q_{s,t}\}_{s \le t \in T}\) be a collection of Markov transition operators on \((S, \mathcal{B}(S))\) satisfying Equation 83.1. The collection is called time-homogeneous if \[ Q_{s,t} = Q_{0, t-s} =: Q_{t-s}, \quad \forall s \le t \in T. \tag{83.2}\]

Under time homogeneity, the general Chapman-Kolmogorov equation Equation 83.1 forces the single-index family \((Q_r)_{r\ge0}\) to satisfy the semigroup law: substituting \(Q_{r,s}=Q_{s-r}\), \(Q_{s,t}=Q_{t-s}\), and \(Q_{r,t}=Q_{t-r}\) into Equation 83.1 gives \[ Q_{s-r} Q_{t-s} = Q_{r,s} Q_{s,t} \overset{\text{(CK)}}{=} Q_{r,t} = Q_{t-r} = Q_{(s-r) + (t-s)}, \] where (CK) marks the use of the general Chapman-Kolmogorov equation Equation 83.1 — the only non-notational step in the chain, since the remaining equalities are either the time-homogeneity substitution or the trivial arithmetic identity \((s-r)+(t-s)=t-r\). Writing \(a := s-r \ge 0\) and \(b := t - s \ge 0\) (which range over all of \(T\cap[0,\infty)\) as \(r,s,t\) vary), we obtain \(Q_a Q_b = Q_{a+b}\). In other words, time homogeneity together with the general Chapman-Kolmogorov equations implies (rather than merely relabels) the one-parameter semigroup property defined next.

Definition 83.2 (One-Parameter Transition Semigroup) A collection \((Q_t)_{t \in T, t \ge 0}\) of Markov transition operators on \(\mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B}(S))\) (i.e. bounded linear operators with \(Q_t f \ge 0\) whenever \(f \ge 0\), and \(Q_t \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}\)) satisfying \[ Q_s Q_t = Q_{s+t} \quad \text{and} \quad Q_0 = \mathrm{Id} \tag{83.3}\] is called a 1-parameter (transition) semigroup.

Definition 83.3 (Time-Homogeneous Markov Process) A Markov process \((X_t)_{t \in T}\) with transition operators \(\{Q_{s,t}\}_{s \le t \in T}\) is called a time-homogeneous Markov process if \(\{Q_{s,t}\}\) is time-homogeneous in the sense of Definition 83.1. By the computation above, the induced single-index family \((Q_t)_{t \ge 0} := (Q_{0,t})_{t \ge 0}\) then automatically forms a 1-parameter semigroup in the sense of Definition 83.2, which we call the transition semigroup of the process.

Remark 83.1 (Intuitive Interpretation). By definition of the transition operators (Lecture 38.1) together with time homogeneity, for every \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\) and \(s \le t \in T\) we have the exact identity \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = (Q_{t-s} f)(X_s), \] or equivalently, shifting by \(s\): \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_{s+t}) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = (Q_t f)(X_s). \] (Theorem 83.1 below gives the rigorous generalization of this identity to arbitrary bounded measurable functionals of the whole future path, not just single-time functionals \(f(X_t)\).) Informally, \(Q_t\) determines the state distribution at time \(t\) given \(X_0\). For \(X_{t+s}\) given \(X_0\), the evolution decomposes via \(Q_tQ_s=Q_sQ_t\) (the two operators commute since \(Q_aQ_b=Q_{a+b}=Q_bQ_a\)), corresponding to evaluating a new process \(\tilde{X}_t\) given \(X_s\).

83.3 Canonical Process Notation and Measures on Path Space

For any time-homogeneous Markov process, all finite-dimensional distributions are uniquely determined by:

  1. The initial distribution \(\nu = \mathrm{Law}(X_0) \in \mathcal{P}(S, \mathcal{B})\);
  2. The transition semigroup \((Q_t)_{t \in T}\).

Thinking of the process as a measure on the path space \(S^T\), we fix the transition semigroup \((Q_t)_{t \in T}\) and consider the family of probability measures \(\{\mathbb{P}^\nu\}_{\nu \in \mathcal{P}(S,\mathcal{B})}\) corresponding to different starting distributions \(\nu\).

Notation:

  • Let \(X_\cdot = (X_t)_{t \in T}\) denote the canonical process ensemble.
  • For a bounded, measurable functional \(F \in \mathcal{B}_b(S^T, \mathcal{B}^{\otimes T})\), \(\mathbb{E}^\nu[F(X_\cdot)]\) denotes the expected value of \(F(X_\cdot)\) under the law where \(X_0 \sim \nu\).
  • In the Dirac case where \(\nu = \delta_x\) for \(x \in S\), we write \(\mathbb{E}^x\) and \(\mathbb{P}^x\). \(\mathbb{P}^x\) is the law of the process started at \(x\). The superscript \(x\) in \(\mathbb{E}^x\) specifies the initial condition: under \(\mathbb{P}^x = \mathbb{P}^{\delta_x}\), the process begins at \(x\) with certainty (\(X_0 = x\) almost surely).
  • \(g(x) = \mathbb{E}^x[F(X_\cdot)]\) is simply: start the process at \(x\), run it forward in time, apply \(F\) to the resulting path, and take the expected value. Doing this for every \(x \in S\) produces a function \(g : S \to \mathbb{R}\). It tells you, for each possible starting state, how large \(F\) is expected to be along the path.
  • Before you can write “\(g(X_t)\)” in the following theorem, we need \(g\) to be a measurable function \(S \to \mathbb{R}\). Otherwise, composing \(g\) with the random variable \(X_t\) doesn’t produce a well-defined random variable. Statement 1 in Theorem 83.1 guarantees exactly this.
  • Statement 2 of the following theorem is: \(\mathbb{E}^{\nu_0}[F(X_{t+\cdot}) \mid X_t] = g(X_t), \quad \mathbb{P}^{\nu_0}\text{-a.s.}\) This should be read as: to compute the conditional expectation of any path functional of the future trajectory, you don’t need to know the full past — you just evaluate \(g\) at the current state. The entire job of the conditional expectation is done by the single function \(g\) evaluated at one point.

83.4 Main Shift-Invariance and Conditional Expectation Theorem

Theorem 83.1 (Shift Property and Measurability of Markov Expectations) Let \(X_\cdot\) be a time-homogeneous Markov process taking values in a measurable state space \((S, \mathcal{B}(S))\) with transition semigroup \((Q_t)_{t \ge 0}\). Let \(F \in \mathcal{B}_b(S^T, \mathcal{B}^{\otimes T})\). Then:

  1. The mapping \(S \ni x \mapsto \mathbb{E}^x[F(X_\cdot)]\) is \(\mathcal{B}(S) / \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\)-measurable.
  2. For all \(t \ge 0\) and any initial distribution \(\nu_0 \in \mathcal{P}(S, \mathcal{B}(S))\), \[ \mathbb{E}^{\nu_0}\left[ F(X_{t+\cdot}) \mid \mathcal{F}_t \right] = \mathbb{E}^{\nu_0}\left[ F(X_{t+\cdot}) \mid X_t \right] = \mathbb{E}^{X_t}[F(X_\cdot)], \quad \mathbb{P}^{\nu_0}\text{-a.s.} \tag{83.4}\] where \(g(x) := \mathbb{E}^x[F(X_\cdot)]\) and \(g(X_t) = \mathbb{E}^{X_t}[F(X_\cdot)]\).

Remark 83.2 (What the theorem actually says).

  1. At the level of a single observable \(f(X_{s+t})\), time homogeneity already gives you the familiar identity \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_{s+t}) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = (Q_t f)(X_s)\). The shift property theorem lifts this to the entire future path at once: instead of just \(f(X_{s+t})\), you can take any bounded measurable functional \(F\) of the whole future trajectory \((X_{t+s})_{s \geq 0}\) — say, the supremum of the path, a stopping-time functional, a running integral — and the identity still holds. The conclusion is: \[ \mathbb{E}^{\nu_0}\!\left[F(X_{t+\cdot}) \mid \mathcal{F}_t\right] = \mathbb{E}^{X_t}[F(X_\cdot)], \quad \mathbb{P}^{\nu_0}\text{-a.s.} \]

  2. Statement 1 (measurability) is a prerequisite, not just a bonus. Before you can write \(\mathbb{E}^{X_t}[F(X_\cdot)]\) — i.e., evaluate the map \(x \mapsto \mathbb{E}^x[F(X_\cdot)]\) at the random variable \(X_t\) — you need to know that this map is measurable, otherwise the composition isn’t a well-defined random variable. The theorem guarantees this first, making the right-hand side of the equation meaningful. Notice also that measurability holds for any measurable transition kernel family and does not itself require time homogeneity.

  3. Statement 2 is where time homogeneity plays its role. The first equality (conditioning on \(\mathcal{F}_t\) is the same as conditioning on \(X_t\)) is just the Markov property. The second equality — that the conditional expectation equals \(\mathbb{E}^{X_t}[F(X_\cdot)]\) — is genuinely using time homogeneity. Because the transition kernels from time \(t\) onward coincide with those of a fresh process started at \(X_t\) from time \(0\), the law of the future path \((X_{t+s})_{s \geq 0}\) given \(X_t = x\) is exactly the same as the law of \((X_s)_{s \geq 0}\) under \(\mathbb{P}^x\).

  4. The restart interpretation: The theorem is the rigorous statement of: a time-homogeneous Markov process restarts from scratch at any fixed time \(t\), with the current state \(X_t\) as the new initial condition, and this holds not just for observations at a single future time, but for the entire future path at once. \(\mathbb{E}^{X_t}[F(X_\cdot)]\) should literally be read as “run a fresh copy of the process starting at \(X_t\), and apply \(F\) to that copy’s trajectory.” The theorem says this equals the conditional expectation of \(F\) applied to the actual time-shifted path.

83.5 Proof of the Main Theorem

Proof (Step 1: Reduction to Local \(\sigma\)-Algebras). Recall \(\mathcal{F}_t\) denotes a filtration to which \((X_t)_{t\in T}\) is adapted and Markov, and \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge t}^X := \sigma(X_u : u \ge t)\) denotes the future \(\sigma\)-algebra from time \(t\) onward (Lecture 36.1). Observe that \(F(X_{t+\cdot}) \in \mathcal{B}(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_{\ge t}^X)\), since \(F(X_{t+\cdot})\) is a (pointwise) limit of functions depending only on finitely many coordinates \(X_u\), \(u \ge t\). As established in Theorem 78.1 (Lecture 36.1), if \(Y\) is a bounded \(\mathcal{F}_{\ge t}^X\)-measurable random variable, then by the Markov property: \[ \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_t]. \] Applying this to \(Y = F(X_{t+\cdot})\) directly yields the first equality in Equation 83.4: \[ \mathbb{E}^{\nu_0}\left[ F(X_{t+\cdot}) \mid \mathcal{F}_t \right] = \mathbb{E}^{\nu_0}\left[ F(X_{t+\cdot}) \mid X_t \right]. \]

83.5.1 Step 2: Proof for Cylinder Functions

It remains to prove the second equality in Equation 83.4, \(\mathbb{E}^{\nu_0}[F(X_{t+\cdot}) \mid X_t] = \mathbb{E}^{X_t}[F(X_\cdot)]\), together with statement 1, for general \(F \in \mathcal{B}_b(S^T, \mathcal{B}^{\otimes T})\). We reduce to cylinder functions via Dynkin’s Multiplicative Systems Theorem (Theorem 29.1, Lecture 14.1), exactly as in the proof of Theorem 78.1 (Lecture 36.1):

  • Let \(\mathbb{M}\) be the collection of functions \[ F(w) = f_0(w(t_0)) f_1(w(t_1)) \cdots f_n(w(t_n)), \tag{83.5}\] where \(0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n\) and \(f_j \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\) for \(j = 0, \dots, n\). As in Lemma 78.1 (Lecture 36.1), \(\mathbb{M}\) is closed under pointwise products (merge coincident time points and multiply the corresponding factors) and \(\sigma(\mathbb{M}) = \mathcal{B}^{\otimes T}\): taking \(n=0\) recovers every generator \(\{X_0 \in A\}\), and padding with \(f_0 \equiv 1\) recovers every generator \(\{X_t \in A\}\) for \(t > 0\), \(A \in \mathcal{B}\). Hence \(\mathbb{M}\) is a multiplicative system generating \(\mathcal{B}^{\otimes T}\).
  • Let \(\mathbb{H} := \{F \in \mathcal{B}_b(S^T, \mathcal{B}^{\otimes T}) : \text{statements 1 and 2 hold for } F\}\). \(\mathbb{H}\) is a vector space (both statements are linear in \(F\)) containing the constant function \(1\) (both sides of statement 2 reduce to \(1\), and \(x \mapsto 1\) is trivially measurable). \(\mathbb{H}\) is closed under bounded convergence: if \(F_k \in \mathbb{H}\), \(|F_k| \le C\), and \(F_k \to F\) pointwise, then for each fixed \(x\), \(g_k(x) := \mathbb{E}^x[F_k(X_\cdot)] \to \mathbb{E}^x[F(X_\cdot)] =: g(x)\) by (ordinary) bounded convergence under \(\mathbb{P}^x\), so \(g\) is measurable as a pointwise limit of measurable functions (statement 1), and \(g_k(X_t) \to g(X_t)\) everywhere on \(\Omega\); combined with the Conditional Dominated Convergence Theorem applied to \(F_k(X_{t+\cdot}) \to F(X_{t+\cdot})\) (boundedly), the identities \(\mathbb{E}^{\nu_0}[F_k(X_{t+\cdot}) \mid X_t] = g_k(X_t)\) pass to the limit to give \(\mathbb{E}^{\nu_0}[F(X_{t+\cdot}) \mid X_t] = g(X_t)\) a.s. (statement 2).

By Theorem 29.1, if \(\mathbb{M} \subseteq \mathbb{H}\) then \(\mathbb{H} = \mathcal{B}_b(S^T, \mathcal{B}^{\otimes T})\). It therefore suffices to establish statements 1 and 2 for cylinder functions \(F \in \mathbb{M}\) of the form Equation 83.5.

83.5.1.1 Proof of Statement 1 (Measurability)

For \(F\) given by Equation 83.5: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}^x[F(X_\cdot)] &= \mathbb{E}^x\left[ f_0(X_{t_0}) f_1(X_{t_1}) \cdots f_n(X_{t_n}) \right] \\ &= \int \delta_x(\mathrm{d}x_0) \int q_{t_0, t_1}(x_0, \mathrm{d}x_1) \cdots \int q_{t_{n-1}, t_n}(x_{n-1}, \mathrm{d}x_n) f_0(x_0) \cdots f_n(x_n) \\ &= f_0(x) \left( Q_{t_0, t_1} \left( f_1 \cdots Q_{t_{n-1}, t_n} f_n \right) \right)(x). \end{aligned} \] Because transition operators map bounded measurable functions to bounded measurable functions, \(x \mapsto \mathbb{E}^x[F(X_\cdot)]\) is a composition of measurable functions, hence \(\mathcal{B}(S)/\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\)-measurable. \(\checkmark\)

83.5.1.2 Proof of Statement 2 (Conditional Shift Property)

We wish to show that \(\mathbb{E}^{\nu_0}[F(X_{t+\cdot}) \mid X_t] = g(X_t)\) \(\mathbb{P}^{\nu_0}\)-a.s., where \(g(x) := \mathbb{E}^x[F(X_\cdot)]\).

For \(F(X_{t+\cdot}) = f_0(X_t) f_1(X_{t + t_1}) \cdots f_n(X_{t + t_n})\), let \(h \in \mathcal{B}_b(S, \mathcal{B})\) be an arbitrary test function. Evaluating the joint expectation yields: \[ \begin{aligned} I &:= \mathbb{E}^{\nu_0}\left[ h(X_t) F(X_{t+\cdot}) \right] = \mathbb{E}^{\nu_0}\left[ h(X_t) f_0(X_t) f_1(X_{t+t_1}) \cdots f_n(X_{t+t_n}) \right] \\ &= \int \nu_0(\mathrm{d}x_{-1}) \int q_{0, t}(x_{-1}, \mathrm{d}x_0) \int q_{t, t+t_1}(x_0, \mathrm{d}x_1) \cdots \\ & \quad \quad \cdots \int q_{t+t_{n-1}, t+t_n}(x_{n-1}, \mathrm{d}x_n) h(x_0) f_0(x_0) f_1(x_1) \cdots f_n(x_n). \end{aligned} \]

By time homogeneity (the initial kernel \(q_{0,t} = q_t\), and \(q_{t+t_{j-1}, t+t_j} = q_{0, t_j - t_{j-1}} = q_{t_j - t_{j-1}}\) for \(j = 1, \dots, n\)) and using the marginal distribution \(\nu_t(\mathrm{d}x_0) := \int \nu_0(\mathrm{d}x_{-1}) q_t(x_{-1}, \mathrm{d}x_0) = \mathrm{Law}(X_t)\), we rewrite \(I\) as: \[ \begin{aligned} I &= \int \nu_t(\mathrm{d}x_0) h(x_0) f_0(x_0) \int q_{t_1}(x_0, \mathrm{d}x_1) \cdots \int q_{t_n - t_{n-1}}(x_{n-1}, \mathrm{d}x_n) f_1(x_1) \cdots f_n(x_n) \\ &= \int \nu_t(\mathrm{d}x_0) h(x_0) \underbrace{\mathbb{E}^{x_0}\left[ f_0(X_0) f_1(X_{t_1}) \cdots f_n(X_{t_n}) \right]}_{= \mathbb{E}^{x_0}[F(X_\cdot)] = g(x_0)} \\ &= \int \nu_t(\mathrm{d}x_0) h(x_0) g(x_0) \\ &= \mathbb{E}^{\nu_0}\left[ h(X_t) \mathbb{E}^{X_t}[F(X_\cdot)] \right]. \end{aligned} \] Since \(\mathbb{E}^{\nu_0}[h(X_t) F(X_{t+\cdot})] = \mathbb{E}^{\nu_0}[h(X_t) g(X_t)]\) holds for all bounded measurable test functions \(h\), and \(g(X_t)\) is \(\sigma(X_t)\)-measurable, by definition of conditional expectation we conclude: \[ \mathbb{E}^{\nu_0}\left[ F(X_{t+\cdot}) \mid X_t \right] = g(X_t) = \mathbb{E}^{X_t}[F(X_\cdot)], \quad \mathbb{P}^{\nu_0}\text{-a.s.} \quad \blacksquare \]

83.6 Takeaways

  • Time homogeneity collapses \(\{Q_{s,t}\}\) to a semigroup. The reduction \(Q_{s,t} = Q_{0,t-s}\) (Definition 83.1), combined with the general Chapman-Kolmogorov equations Equation 83.1 that any transition family already satisfies, implies — rather than merely renames — the one-parameter semigroup law \(Q_sQ_t=Q_{s+t}\), \(Q_0=\mathrm{Id}\) (Definition 83.2). A time-homogeneous Markov process is exactly a Markov process whose transition operators reduce this way.
  • The canonical family \(\{\mathbb{P}^\nu\}_{\nu}\). Fixing the semigroup \((Q_t)_{t\ge0}\), all finite-dimensional distributions — and hence, via the Kolmogorov Extension Theorem, a canonical probability measure \(\mathbb{P}^\nu\) on path space — are determined by the initial law \(\nu\) alone; \(\mathbb{P}^x := \mathbb{P}^{\delta_x}\) is the Dirac/single-starting-point case.
  • The shift property (Theorem 83.1) is the rigorous form of “restarting the process at \(X_t\).” Statement 1 (measurability of \(x \mapsto \mathbb{E}^x[F(X_\cdot)]\)) holds for any measurable transition kernel family and does not use time homogeneity. Statement 2 — that conditioning the shifted path functional \(F(X_{t+\cdot})\) on the whole past \(\mathcal{F}_t\) is the same as conditioning on \(X_t\) alone, and equals \(\mathbb{E}^{X_t}[F(X_\cdot)]\) — genuinely uses time homogeneity (to identify the post-\(t\) kernels with the original ones) and is the basis for the strong Markov property developed in later lectures.
  • Proof technique: reduce to cylinder functions via the Multiplicative Systems Theorem. As in Theorem 78.1 (Lecture 36.1), the argument is carried out first for finite products \(f_0(w(t_0))\cdots f_n(w(t_n))\) of bounded measurable functions — a multiplicative system generating \(\mathcal{B}^{\otimes T}\) — and then extended to all of \(\mathcal{B}_b(S^T,\mathcal{B}^{\otimes T})\) by checking that the class of functionals satisfying statements 1–2 is a vector space, containing \(1\), closed under bounded convergence.
