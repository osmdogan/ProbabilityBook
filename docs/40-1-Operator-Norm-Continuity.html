86  Lecture 40.1: Operator Norm Continuity

86.1 Introduction to Markov Generators and Continuity

Given a family of Markov transition operators \(\{Q_t\}_{t \ge 0}\) on the bounded measurable functions \(\mathcal{B}(S, \mathcal{B})\) over a state space \(S\), the generator \(A\) (should it exist) is defined as the linear operator: \[ A f := \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} Q_t f \right\vert{}_{t=0} = \lim_{t \to 0^+} \frac{Q_t f - f}{t}. \tag{86.1}\]

If \(Q_t\) is differentiable at \(t = 0\), it must also be continuous at \(t = 0\). There are multiple notions of operator continuity available (e.g., pointwise/strong continuity, operator norm continuity). The strongest notion—operator norm continuity—yields the most concrete analytical properties.

86.2 Operator Norms and Bounded Operators

Definition 86.1 (Bounded Operator and Operator Norm) Let \((B, \Vert{}\cdot\Vert{})\) be a normed space (e.g., \(B = \mathcal{B}(S, \mathcal{B})\) equipped with the supremum norm \(\Vert{}f\Vert{}_\infty := \sup_{x \in S} \vert{}f(x)\vert{}\)). For a linear operator \(A: B \to B\), its operator norm \(\Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}}\) is defined as: \[ \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} := \sup_{f \neq 0} \frac{\Vert{}A f\Vert{}}{\Vert{}f\Vert{}} = \sup_{\Vert{}f\Vert{} \le 1} \Vert{}A f\Vert{}. \tag{86.2}\]

\(A\) is called a bounded linear operator if \(\Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} < \infty\), which is equivalent to \(A\) being Lipschitz continuous: \[ \Vert{}A f\Vert{} \le \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \Vert{}f\Vert{} \quad \forall f \in B. \tag{86.3}\]

86.3 Submultiplicativity of the Operator Norm

Lemma 86.1 (Composition of Bounded Linear Operators) If \(A, T: B \to B\) are bounded linear operators, then their composition \(A T\) is also bounded, and: \[ \Vert{}A T\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \Vert{}T\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}}. \tag{86.4}\]

Proof (Proof of Lemma 86.1). Fix \(f \in B\), \(f \neq 0\). If \(T f = 0\), then \(\Vert{}A T f\Vert{} = \Vert{}A 0\Vert{} = 0 \le \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \Vert{}T\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \Vert{}f\Vert{}\) trivially. If \(T f \neq 0\), then: \[ \frac{\Vert{}A T f\Vert{}}{\Vert{}f\Vert{}} = \frac{\Vert{}A(T f)\Vert{}}{\Vert{}T f\Vert{}} \cdot \frac{\Vert{}T f\Vert{}}{\Vert{}f\Vert{}}. \] By definition of the operator norm: \[ \frac{\Vert{}A(T f)\Vert{}}{\Vert{}T f\Vert{}} \le \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \quad \implies \quad \frac{\Vert{}A T f\Vert{}}{\Vert{}f\Vert{}} \le \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \cdot \frac{\Vert{}T f\Vert{}}{\Vert{}f\Vert{}} \le \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \Vert{}T\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}}. \] In either case \(\Vert{}A T f\Vert{} \le \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \Vert{}T\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \Vert{}f\Vert{}\). Taking the supremum over all \(f \neq 0\) yields \(\Vert{}A T\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \Vert{}T\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}}\).

86.4 Operator Powers and Matrix Exponentials

Corollary 86.1 (Powers and Exponentials of Operators) If \(A\) is a bounded linear operator, then \(A^n\) is bounded for every \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), with: \[ \Vert{}A^n\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}}^n. \tag{86.5}\]

Furthermore, on a Banach space \(B\), the operator exponential defined by the power series: \[ e^{t A} := \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{t^n}{n!} A^n, \tag{86.6}\] converges in operator norm to a bounded linear operator for all \(t \in \mathbb{R}\), satisfying: \[ \Vert{}e^{t A}\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le e^{\vert{}t\vert{} \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}}} < \infty. \tag{86.7}\]

Proof (Proof of Corollary 86.1).

  1. By induction on Lemma 86.1 (base case \(n=0\): \(\Vert{}A^0\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} = \Vert{}I\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} = 1 = \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}}^0\)), \(\Vert{}A^n\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}}^n\).
  2. Since \((\mathcal{B}(S), \Vert{}\cdot\Vert{}_\infty)\) is a complete normed space (Banach space, as uniform limits of bounded functions are bounded and \(\mathbb{R}\) is complete), a standard result in functional analysis guarantees that the operator algebra \((\mathcal{L}(B), \Vert{}\cdot\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}})\) of bounded linear operators on a Banach space is itself a Banach space: if \((A_n)\) is \(\Vert{}\cdot\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}}\)-Cauchy, then \((A_n f)\) is Cauchy in \(B\) for each fixed \(f\), hence converges to some \(A f\) by completeness of \(B\); one checks \(A\) is linear, bounded, and \(A_n \to A\) in operator norm.
  3. By completeness of \((\mathcal{L}(B), \Vert{}\cdot\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}})\), it suffices to show the series of norms converges (absolute convergence implies convergence in a Banach space): \[ \sum_{n=0}^\infty \left\Vert{} \frac{t^n}{n!} A^n \right\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{\vert{}t\vert{}^n}{n!} \Vert{}A^n\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{\vert{}t\vert{}^n \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}}^n}{n!} = e^{\vert{}t\vert{} \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}}} < \infty. \] Hence the partial sums of \(e^{t A}\) form a Cauchy sequence in \((\mathcal{L}(B), \Vert{}\cdot\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}})\) and converge to a bounded operator, with \(\Vert{}e^{tA}\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le \sum_{n=0}^\infty \Vert{}t^nA^n/n!\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le e^{\vert{}t\vert{}\Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}}}\) by the triangle inequality.

86.5 Discrete Matrix Representations of Operators

When \(S\) is countable, we can represent linear operators on \(\mathcal{B}(S)\) via matrix kernels \(q: S \times S \to \mathbb{R}\): \[ (A f)(i) = \sum_{j \in S} q(i, j) f(j). \tag{86.8}\]

To ensure that \((A f) \in \mathcal{B}(S)\) and \(A\) is bounded, we define the norm: \[ \Vert{}q\Vert{}_\infty := \sup_{i \in S} \sum_{j \in S} \vert{}q(i, j)\vert{}. \tag{86.9}\]

Proposition 86.1 (Equivalence of Row-Sum Norm and Operator Norm) Let \(S\) be countable and \(q: S \times S \to \mathbb{R}\) satisfy \(\Vert{}q\Vert{}_\infty < \infty\). Then \((A f)(i) = \sum_{j \in S} q(i, j) f(j)\) defines a bounded linear operator on \(\mathcal{B}(S)\), and: \[ \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} = \Vert{}q\Vert{}_\infty = \sup_{i \in S} \sum_{j \in S} \vert{}q(i, j)\vert{}. \tag{86.10}\]

Proof (Proof of Proposition 86.1).

  • Upper Bound (\(\Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le \Vert{}q\Vert{}_\infty\)): For any \(f \in \mathcal{B}(S)\) and any \(i \in S\): \[ \begin{aligned} \vert{}(A f)(i)\vert{} &= \left\vert{} \sum_{j \in S} q(i, j) f(j) \right\vert{}\\ & \le \sum_{j \in S} \vert{}q(i, j)\vert{} \vert{}f(j)\vert{} \\ &\le \left( \sum_{j \in S} \vert{}q(i, j)\vert{} \right) \Vert{}f\Vert{}_\infty \\ &\le \Vert{}q\Vert{}_\infty \Vert{}f\Vert{}_\infty. \end{aligned} \] Taking the supremum over \(i \in S\) gives \(\Vert{}A f\Vert{}_\infty \le \Vert{}q\Vert{}_\infty \Vert{}f\Vert{}_\infty\), so \(\Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le \Vert{}q\Vert{}_\infty\).

  • Lower Bound (\(\Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \ge \Vert{}q\Vert{}_\infty\)): Fix \(\varepsilon > 0\) and choose \(i \in S\) such that \(\sum_{j \in S} \vert{}q(i, j)\vert{} \ge \Vert{}q\Vert{}_\infty - \varepsilon\). Define the function \(f_i \in \mathcal{B}(S)\) by setting \(f_i(j) = \operatorname{sgn}(q(i, j))\). Notice \(\Vert{}f_i\Vert{}_\infty \le 1\). Evaluating \((A f_i)(i)\): \[ (A f_i)(i) = \sum_{j \in S} q(i, j) \operatorname{sgn}(q(i, j)) = \sum_{j \in S} \vert{}q(i, j)\vert{} \ge \Vert{}q\Vert{}_\infty - \varepsilon. \] Hence \(\Vert{}A f_i\Vert{}_\infty \ge \Vert{}q\Vert{}_\infty - \varepsilon\). Taking \(\varepsilon \to 0\) yields \(\Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \ge \Vert{}q\Vert{}_\infty\).

Combining both inequalities establishes \(\Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} = \Vert{}q\Vert{}_\infty\).

Caution: Even when \(S\) is countable, not every bounded linear operator on \(\mathcal{B}(S) = \ell^\infty(S)\) can be expressed as such a matrix. This is because the dual space \((\ell^\infty(S))^*\) is strictly larger than \(\ell^1(S)\): it contains singular (finitely additive but not countably additive) functionals, such as Banach limits, that cannot be represented by any row \(q(i, \cdot) \in \ell^1(S)\). Operators built from such functionals (e.g., rank-one operators \(f \mapsto \phi(f) g\) for \(g \in \mathcal{B}(S)\) and \(\phi\) a singular functional) are bounded but admit no matrix representation Equation 86.8.

86.6 Uniformly Continuous Markov Semigroups

The following result shows that operator norm continuity at a single point, combined with the semigroup property, forces the entire semigroup to have the simplest possible structure: The entire semigroup collapses to \(Q_t = e^{tA}\) for a single bounded operator \(A\). This means the infinitely many operators \(\{Q_t\}_{t \geq 0}\) are completely encoded in just one object \(A\), and the time evolution is as explicit as it could possibly be.

Theorem 86.1 (Uniformly Continuous Markov Semigroups) Let \(B = \mathcal{B}(S, \mathcal{B})\) and let \(\{Q_t\}_{t \ge 0} \subset \mathcal{L}(B)\) be a semigroup of bounded linear Markov transition operators, i.e. \[ Q_0 = I, \qquad Q_{s+t} = Q_s Q_t \quad \text{for all } s, t \ge 0 \tag{86.11}\] (the Chapman–Kolmogorov equations). Suppose further that \(Q_t\) is operator norm continuous at \(t = 0\): \[ \lim_{t \to 0^+} \Vert{}Q_t - I\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} = 0. \tag{86.12}\]

Then \(Q_t\) is operator norm continuous for every \(t \ge 0\), and operator norm differentiable on all of \([0, \infty)\) (one-sided at \(t = 0\)). Furthermore, defining the generator \(A := \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} Q_t\right\vert{}_{t=0} = \lim_{t \to 0^+} \frac{Q_t - I}{t}\), we have:

  1. \(\Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} < \infty\) (the generator is a bounded operator).
  2. \(Q_t = e^{t A} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{t^n}{n!} A^n\) for all \(t \ge 0\).
  3. \(Q_t\) satisfies the Kolmogorov Forward and Backward Differential Equations: \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} Q_t = Q_t A = A Q_t, \quad \text{with } Q_0 = I. \tag{86.13}\]

Proof (Proof of Theorem 86.1). Step 1 (A local uniform bound, and continuity on all of \([0,\infty)\)). By Equation 86.12, fix \(\delta > 0\) such that \(\Vert{}Q_s - I\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le 1/2\) for all \(s \in [0, \delta]\); in particular \(M := \sup_{s \in [0,\delta]} \Vert{}Q_s\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le 3/2\). For any \(t \ge 0\), write \(t = n\delta + r\) with \(n \in \mathbb{N}_0\), \(r \in [0, \delta)\); by Equation 87.1 and Lemma 86.1, \(\Vert{}Q_t\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} = \Vert{}Q_\delta^n Q_r\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le M^{n+1} < \infty\). Hence \(\sup_{s \in [0,T]} \Vert{}Q_s\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} < \infty\) for every \(T < \infty\).

For \(t_0 \ge 0\) and \(h \to 0^+\), Equation 87.1 gives \(Q_{t_0+h} - Q_{t_0} = Q_{t_0}(Q_h - I)\), so by Lemma 86.1 and Equation 86.12, \[ \Vert{}Q_{t_0+h} - Q_{t_0}\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le \Vert{}Q_{t_0}\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \Vert{}Q_h - I\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \to 0, \] which is right continuity at every \(t_0 \ge 0\). For \(0 < h < t_0\), similarly \(Q_{t_0} - Q_{t_0 - h} = Q_{t_0 - h}(Q_h - I)\); since \(\Vert{}Q_{t_0-h}\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}}\) stays bounded as \(h \to 0^+\) by the uniform bound on \([0,t_0]\) established above, \(\Vert{}Q_{t_0}-Q_{t_0-h}\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \to 0\) as well: left continuity. Hence \(t \mapsto Q_t\) is operator norm continuous on all of \([0,\infty)\).

Step 2 (Construction of a bounded generator). Since \(s \mapsto Q_s\) is continuous on \([0,\delta]\) (Step 1), the Riemann integral \(S_\delta := \int_0^\delta Q_s \, \mathrm{d}s \in \mathcal{L}(B)\) is well-defined as a limit of Riemann sums in the Banach space \((\mathcal{L}(B), \Vert{}\cdot\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}})\) (cf. Corollary 86.1), and \[ \left\Vert{} \frac{S_\delta}{\delta} - I \right\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} = \left\Vert{} \frac{1}{\delta}\int_0^\delta (Q_s - I) \, \mathrm{d}s \right\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le \frac{1}{\delta}\int_0^\delta \Vert{}Q_s - I\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \, \mathrm{d}s \le \frac{1}{2} < 1. \] The last inequality uses the uniform bound \(\|Q_s - I\|_\text{op} \leq 1/2\) on \([0, \delta]\) from Step 1. Since we’re in a Banach algebra and \(\|S_\delta/\delta - I\|_\text{op} < 1\), the Neumann series \(\sum_{n=0}^\infty (I - S_\delta/\delta)^n\) converges in operator norm and gives \((S_\delta/\delta)^{-1}\). Hence \(S_\delta\) is invertible with a bounded inverse \(S_\delta^{-1}\). Define the bounded operator \[ A := (Q_\delta - I) S_\delta^{-1}. \] For \(h > 0\), since the bounded linear operator \(Q_h\) commutes with the Riemann integral and \(Q_h Q_s = Q_{h+s}\), a change of variables \(u = h+s\) gives \[ \frac{Q_h - I}{h} S_\delta = \frac{1}{h}\left( \int_0^\delta Q_{h+s}\,\mathrm{d}s - \int_0^\delta Q_s\,\mathrm{d}s \right) = \frac{1}{h}\left( \int_\delta^{\delta+h} Q_u\,\mathrm{d}u - \int_0^h Q_u\,\mathrm{d}u \right). \] By continuity of \(u \mapsto Q_u\) at \(u = \delta\) and \(u = 0\) (Step 1), each averaged integral converges in operator norm as \(h \to 0^+\): \(h^{-1}\int_\delta^{\delta+h}Q_u\,\mathrm{d}u \to Q_\delta\) and \(h^{-1}\int_0^h Q_u\,\mathrm{d}u \to Q_0 = I\) (see Remark 86.1). Hence \[ \frac{Q_h-I}{h}S_\delta \to Q_\delta - I \quad \text{in operator norm as } h \to 0^+. \]

Remark 86.1. Banach-space analogue of the fundamental theorem of calculus: if \(u \mapsto Q_u\) is continuous in operator norm at a point \(u_0\), then \[ \frac{1}{h}\int_{u_0}^{u_0+h} Q_u\, du \xrightarrow{h \to 0^+} Q_{u_0}. \] Proof of this fact. Write: \[ \begin{aligned} \left\|\frac{1}{h}\int_{u_0}^{u_0+h} Q_u\, du - Q_{u_0}\right\|_\text{op} &= \left\|\frac{1}{h}\int_{u_0}^{u_0+h}(Q_u - Q_{u_0})\, du\right\|_\text{op} \\ &\leq \frac{1}{h}\int_{u_0}^{u_0+h}\|Q_u - Q_{u_0}\|_\text{op}\, du. \end{aligned} \] The first equality uses the fact that \(\frac{1}{h}\int_{u_0}^{u_0+h} Q_{u_0}\, du = Q_{u_0}\) exactly. The inequality is the triangle inequality for Banach-space-valued integrals. Now fix any \(\varepsilon > 0\). Since \(u \mapsto Q_u\) is operator-norm continuous at \(u_0\), there exists \(\eta > 0\) such that \(|u - u_0| < \eta \implies \|Q_u - Q_{u_0}\|_\text{op} < \varepsilon\). For any \(0 < h < \eta\), every \(u \in [u_0, u_0 + h]\) satisfies \(|u - u_0| \leq h < \eta\), so: \[ \frac{1}{h}\int_{u_0}^{u_0+h}\|Q_u - Q_{u_0}\|_\text{op}\, du < \frac{1}{h}\int_{u_0}^{u_0+h} \varepsilon\, du = \varepsilon. \] Since \(\varepsilon > 0\) was arbitrary, the limit holds.

Right-multiplication by the fixed bounded operator \(S_\delta^{-1}\) is continuous on \(\mathcal{L}(B)\) (Lemma 86.1), so \[ \frac{Q_h-I}{h} = \left[\frac{Q_h-I}{h}S_\delta\right]S_\delta^{-1} \longrightarrow (Q_\delta-I)S_\delta^{-1} = A \] in operator norm as \(h \to 0^+\). This shows the limit defining \(A\) exists in operator norm, and \(A\) is bounded as a product of bounded operators, proving (1).

Step 3 (Differentiability for all \(t \ge 0\) and the Kolmogorov equations). Fix \(t_0 \ge 0\). For \(h > 0\), Equation 87.1 gives \[ \begin{aligned} & \frac{Q_{t_0+h}-Q_{t_0}}{h} = Q_{t_0}\cdot\frac{Q_h-I}{h} \longrightarrow Q_{t_0}A, \\ & \frac{Q_{t_0+h}-Q_{t_0}}{h} = \frac{Q_h-I}{h}\cdot Q_{t_0} \longrightarrow A Q_{t_0} \end{aligned} \] as \(h \to 0^+\), using Step 2 and continuity of left- and right-multiplication by the fixed bounded operator \(Q_{t_0}\) (Lemma 86.1). Hence the right-derivative of \(Q_t\) at \(t_0\) equals \(Q_{t_0}A = AQ_{t_0}\). For \(t_0 > 0\) and \(0 < h < t_0\), \[ \frac{Q_{t_0}-Q_{t_0-h}}{h} = Q_{t_0-h}\cdot\frac{Q_h-I}{h}. \] Since \(Q_{t_0-h}\to Q_{t_0}\) (Step 1) and \((Q_h-I)/h \to A\) (Step 2), and \(\Vert{}Q_{t_0-h}\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}}\) is bounded for small \(h\) (Step 1), multiplication in \(\mathcal{L}(B)\) is jointly continuous there (Lemma 86.1), so the left-derivative at \(t_0\) also equals \(Q_{t_0}A\). Thus \(t \mapsto Q_t\) is differentiable in operator norm on \([0,\infty)\) (one-sided at \(t=0\)), with \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}Q_t = Q_t A = A Q_t, \qquad Q_0 = I, \] proving (3).

Step 4 (\(Q_t = e^{tA}\)). By Corollary 86.1, \(e^{-tA}\) is a well-defined bounded operator for every \(t\), and \(A\) commutes with \(e^{-tA}\) since \(A\) commutes with every power of itself. Define \(F(t) := Q_t e^{-tA} \in \mathcal{L}(B)\). Differentiating the power series for \(e^{-tA}\) term-by-term (justified by the same absolute-convergence argument as in Corollary 86.1) gives \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}e^{-tA} = -Ae^{-tA}\), so by the Leibniz rule for operator-norm-differentiable \(\mathcal{L}(B)\)-valued functions (valid since operator multiplication is bounded bilinear) and Step 3, \[ F'(t) = (Q_tA)e^{-tA} + Q_t(-Ae^{-tA}) = Q_tAe^{-tA} - Q_tAe^{-tA} = 0, \] for all \(t \ge 0\). Since \(F\) is continuously differentiable with \(F' \equiv 0\) on \([0,\infty)\), the fundamental theorem of calculus for Banach-space-valued functions gives \(F(t) - F(0) = \int_0^t F'(s)\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\), i.e., \(F(t) = F(0) = Q_0 e^0 = I\) for all \(t \ge 0\). Hence \(Q_t = e^{tA}\) for all \(t \ge 0\), proving (2).

86.7 Remarks

  1. Uniqueness: The argument in Step 4 of the proof above is in fact a general uniqueness argument: for any bounded operator \(A\), the operator-valued ODE \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}X_t = AX_t\), \(X_0 = I\), has \(X_t = e^{tA}\) as its unique solution among operator-norm-differentiable curves \(t \mapsto X_t \in \mathcal{L}(B)\) (apply Step 4’s computation to \(F(t) := X_t e^{-tA}\) to get \(F'\equiv0\), hence \(X_t = X_0 e^{tA} = e^{tA}\)). In particular, \(Q_t = e^{tA}\) is the unique such solution to Equation 87.5.
  2. Unbounded Generators: Without operator norm continuity (e.g., under the weaker notion of strong/pointwise continuity \(\lim_{t \to 0} \Vert{}Q_t f - f\Vert{}_\infty = 0\)), the generator \(A\) may be unbounded and only densely defined on a proper domain \(\mathcal{D}(A) \subset \mathcal{B}(S)\). The key step of the proof above that fails in this setting is Step 2: the Riemann integral \(S_\delta\) is still well-defined (its integrand is only strongly, not uniformly, continuous), but \(S_\delta/\delta\) need not be close to \(I\) in operator norm, so the Neumann-series argument for invertibility of \(S_\delta\) breaks down, and the generator can no longer be extracted as a bounded operator via this construction.

86.8 Takeaways

  • The operator norm \(\Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} = \sup_{\Vert{}f\Vert{}\le1}\Vert{}Af\Vert{}\) turns boundedness of a linear operator into a Lipschitz condition, and it is submultiplicative: \(\Vert{}AT\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} \le \Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}}\Vert{}T\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}}\) (Lemma 86.1). This single inequality is the engine behind everything else in this lecture: it controls powers \(A^n\), the exponential series \(e^{tA}\), and products/limits of operators such as \(Q_t(Q_h - I)\).
  • On a Banach space \(B\), the space of bounded linear operators \((\mathcal{L}(B), \Vert{}\cdot\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}})\) is itself a Banach space (in fact a Banach algebra), so absolutely convergent operator series converge. This is what makes the operator exponential \(e^{tA} = \sum_{n\ge0} t^nA^n/n!\) well-defined and bounded whenever \(A\) is bounded (Corollary 86.1).
  • For a countable state space \(S\), bounded operators on \(\mathcal{B}(S)\) that arise from a matrix kernel \(q(i,j)\) are exactly characterized by \(\Vert{}A\Vert{}_{\mathrm{op}} = \Vert{}q\Vert{}_\infty\), the supremum of absolute row sums (Proposition 86.1)—but not every bounded operator on \(\mathcal{B}(S)\) arises this way, because \((\ell^\infty(S))^*\) is strictly larger than \(\ell^1(S)\).
  • The central result, Theorem 86.1, shows that operator norm continuity at a single time \(t=0\), together with the semigroup (Chapman–Kolmogorov) property \(Q_{s+t}=Q_sQ_t\), is already enough to force the entire semigroup to collapse to the simplest possible form: \(Q_t = e^{tA}\) for a bounded generator \(A\). This is special to the operator-norm topology—the semigroup property alone does not buy differentiability; continuity at one point is essential, and it must hold in operator norm, not merely strongly.
  • This is also a genuine limitation: most interesting continuous-time Markov chains and diffusions (e.g., Brownian motion, birth-death chains on infinite state spaces) have unbounded generators, so \(Q_t\) is only strongly (pointwise) continuous at \(t=0\), not operator norm continuous. The uniformly continuous case treated here is the technically simplest instance of the general Hille–Yosida theory of \(C_0\)-semigroups, in which the generator is replaced by a closed, densely defined operator \(A: \mathcal{D}(A) \subset B \to B\), and the exponential series is replaced by a more delicate construction (e.g., via the resolvent \((\lambda I - A)^{-1}\)).
