87  Lecture 40.2: Bounded Generators

87.1 Introduction

In continuous-time Markov process theory, the transition operators \((Q_t)_{t \ge 0}\) form a semigroup of bounded linear operators on the space of bounded measurable functions. A central question in the analytical theory of Markov processes is the differentiability of the mapping \(t \mapsto Q_t\) with respect to the operator norm and the existence of a generator operator \(A\).

In these notes, we establish that under uniform operator-norm continuity at \(t = 0^+\), the semigroup \((Q_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is operator-norm differentiable for all \(t \in [0, \infty)\), the generator \(A\) is a bounded operator, and \(Q_t\) can be represented explicitly as an operator exponential \(Q_t = e^{tA}\).

87.2 Main Theorem

Theorem 87.1 (Bounded Generator Theorem) Let \((S, \mathcal{B})\) be a measurable space, and let \((Q_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be a semigroup of Markov transition operators on \(\mathcal{B}_b(S)\), the Banach space of real-valued, bounded, \(\mathcal{B}\)-measurable functions endowed with the supremum norm \(\|f\|_\infty = \sup_{x \in S} |f(x)|\). That is, each \(Q_t\) is a linear, positivity-preserving operator with \(Q_t 1 = 1\), and the family satisfies the Chapman–Kolmogorov equations \[ Q_0 = I, \qquad Q_{s+t} = Q_s Q_t \quad \text{for all } s, t \ge 0. \tag{87.1}\] Suppose further that \((Q_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is operator-norm continuous at \(t = 0^+\): \[ \lim_{t \downarrow 0} \|Q_t - I\|_{\text{op}} = 0, \tag{87.2}\] where \(I\) denotes the identity operator on \(\mathcal{B}_b(S)\).

Then:

  1. The mapping \(t \mapsto Q_t\) is operator-norm differentiable on \([0, \infty)\) (one-sided at \(t = 0\)).

  2. The infinitesimal generator defined by

    \[ A := \lim_{h \downarrow 0} \frac{Q_h - I}{h} = \left. \frac{d}{dt} Q_t \right|_{t=0^+} \tag{87.3}\]

    exists in the operator norm and satisfies \(\|A\|_{\text{op}} < \infty\).

  3. For all \(t \ge 0\), the transition operator is given by the operator exponential

    \[ Q_t = e^{tA} := \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^n}{n!} A^n, \tag{87.4}\]

    where the series converges uniformly in the operator norm on compact subintervals of \([0, \infty)\).

  4. In particular, \((Q_t)_{t \ge 0}\) satisfies the Kolmogorov forward and backward differential equations:

    \[ \frac{d}{dt} Q_t = Q_t A = A Q_t, \quad Q_0 = I. \tag{87.5}\]

87.3 Motivational Example: Poisson Process

Example 87.1 (Discrete State Space Poisson Process) Consider a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda > 0\) on the state space \(S = \mathbb{N}_0 = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}\) equipped with the discrete \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{B} = \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}_0)\). The transition probabilities are given by: \[ q_t(i, j) = \mathbb{P}(X_t = j \mid X_0 = i) = e^{-\lambda t} \frac{(\lambda t)^{j-i}}{(j-i)!} \mathbb{I}_{\{j \ge i\}}, \tag{87.6}\] for \(i, j \in \mathbb{N}_0\). We verify condition (Equation 87.2) directly. Since \(\mathcal{B} = \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}_0)\) is discrete, \(Q_t - I\) is represented by the matrix kernel \(q_t(i,j) - \delta_{ij}\), and by the row-sum operator-norm formula (Proposition 86.1, Lecture 40.1): \[ \begin{aligned} \|Q_t - I\|_{\text{op}} &= \sup_{i \in \mathbb{N}_0} \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} |q_t(i, j) - \delta_{ij}| \\ &= \sup_{i \in \mathbb{N}_0} \left( |q_t(i, i) - 1| + \sum_{j > i} q_t(i, j) \right) \\ &= \sup_{i \in \mathbb{N}_0} \left( |e^{-\lambda t} - 1| + e^{-\lambda t} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(\lambda t)^k}{k!} \right) \\ &= (1 - e^{-\lambda t}) + e^{-\lambda t} (e^{\lambda t} - 1) \\ &= 2(1 - e^{-\lambda t}). \end{aligned} \]

As \(t \downarrow 0\), we have:

\[ \lim_{t \downarrow 0} \|Q_t - I\|_{\text{op}} = \lim_{t \downarrow 0} 2(1 - e^{-\lambda t}) = 0. \]

Thus, the Poisson process semigroup is uniformly operator-norm continuous at \(t = 0\). The infinitesimal generator \(A\) corresponds to the infinite matrix:

\[ A_{ij} = \left. \frac{d}{dt} q_t(i,j) \right|_{t=0^+} = \begin{cases} -\lambda & \text{if } j = i, \\ \lambda & \text{if } j = i+1, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

87.4 Proof of the Main Theorem

We break the proof into several logical steps.

87.4.1 Step 1: Norm Boundedness of \(Q_t\) and Continuity on \([0, \infty)\)

Since \((Q_t)_{t \ge 0}\) are Markov transition operators, they preserve positivity and satisfy \(Q_t 1 = 1\). Consequently, for any \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\), \[ \|Q_t f\|_\infty = \sup_{x \in S} |Q_t f(x)| \le \sup_{x \in S} Q_t |f|(x) \le \|f\|_\infty Q_t 1 = \|f\|_\infty. \] Thus, \(\|Q_t\|_{\text{op}} \le 1\) for all \(t \ge 0\). Now, let \(t \ge 0\). For any \(h > 0\), using the Chapman–Kolmogorov identity \(Q_{t+h} = Q_h Q_t\) (Equation 87.1) and submultiplicativity of the operator norm (cf. Lemma 86.1, Lecture 40.1): \[ \begin{aligned} \|Q_{t+h} - Q_t\|_{\text{op}} &= \|(Q_h - I) Q_t\|_{\text{op}} \\ &\le \|Q_h - I\|_{\text{op}} \|Q_t\|_{\text{op}} \\ &\le \|Q_h - I\|_{\text{op}} \xrightarrow[h \downarrow 0]{} 0. \end{aligned} \] Similarly, for \(t > h > 0\), using \(Q_t - Q_{t-h} = (Q_h - I) Q_{t-h}\) (again from Equation 87.1, since \(Q_t = Q_hQ_{t-h}\)): \[ \begin{aligned} \|Q_t - Q_{t-h}\|_{\text{op}} &= \|(Q_h - I) Q_{t-h}\|_{\text{op}}\\ &\le \|Q_h - I\|_{\text{op}} \|Q_{t-h}\|_{\text{op}} \\ &\le \|Q_h - I\|_{\text{op}} \xrightarrow[h \downarrow 0]{} 0. \end{aligned} \] Hence, \(t \mapsto Q_t\) is operator-norm continuous on \([0, \infty)\). In particular, \(s \mapsto \|I - Q_s\|_{\text{op}}\) is continuous on \([0,\infty)\), and \(\|Q_s\|_{\text{op}} \le 1\) for every \(s \ge 0\); both facts will be used repeatedly below.

87.4.2 Step 2: Equivalence of Global Differentiability and Local Differentiability at \(t=0^+\)

Consider the right difference quotient for \(h > 0\):

\[ \frac{Q_{t+h} - Q_t}{h} = \frac{Q_h - I}{h} Q_t = Q_t \frac{Q_h - I}{h}. \]

Suppose there exists a bounded linear operator \(B \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}_b(S))\) such that \(\lim_{h \downarrow 0} \frac{Q_h - I}{h} = B\) in the operator norm. Then:

\[ \begin{aligned} \left\| \frac{Q_{t+h} - Q_t}{h} - Q_t B \right\|_{\text{op}} &= \left\| Q_t \left( \frac{Q_h - I}{h} - B \right) \right\|_{\text{op}}\\ & \le \|Q_t\|_{\text{op}} \left\| \frac{Q_h - I}{h} - B \right\|_{\text{op}} \\ & \le \left\| \frac{Q_h - I}{h} - B \right\|_{\text{op}} \xrightarrow[h \downarrow 0]{} 0. \end{aligned} \]

so the right-derivative of \(t\mapsto Q_t\) exists at every \(t\ge0\) and equals \(Q_tB\).

For \(t > 0\) we must also check the left difference quotient, since differentiability at an interior point is a two-sided notion. For \(0 < h < t\), Step 1 gives \(Q_t - Q_{t-h} = (Q_h - I)Q_{t-h}\), so \[ \frac{Q_t - Q_{t-h}}{h} = \frac{Q_h - I}{h}\,Q_{t-h}. \] Hence \[ \begin{aligned} \left\| \frac{Q_t - Q_{t-h}}{h} - BQ_t \right\|_{\text{op}} &= \left\| \left(\frac{Q_h - I}{h} - B\right) Q_{t-h} + B(Q_{t-h} - Q_t) \right\|_{\text{op}} \\ &\le \left\| \frac{Q_h - I}{h} - B \right\|_{\text{op}} \|Q_{t-h}\|_{\text{op}} + \|B\|_{\text{op}} \|Q_{t-h} - Q_t\|_{\text{op}} \\ &\le \left\| \frac{Q_h - I}{h} - B \right\|_{\text{op}} + \|B\|_{\text{op}} \|Q_{t-h} - Q_t\|_{\text{op}} \xrightarrow[h \downarrow 0]{} 0, \end{aligned} \] where the first term vanishes by hypothesis and the second vanishes because \(h \mapsto Q_{t-h}\) is operator-norm continuous at \(h=0\) (Step 1) and \(B\) is bounded. Thus the left-derivative at \(t\) also exists and equals \(BQ_t\). Moreover \(Q_tB=BQ_t\): this is immediate from the identity \(\frac{Q_h-I}{h}Q_t = Q_t\frac{Q_h-I}{h}\) established above, which holds for every \(h>0\) (not merely in the limit) and whose two sides converge, as \(h\downarrow0\), to \(BQ_t\) and \(Q_tB\) respectively. Hence the right- and left-derivatives at \(t\) coincide, and \(t\mapsto Q_t\) is (two-sided) differentiable at every \(t>0\).

This shows that \(\frac{d}{dt} Q_t\) exists (two-sided for \(t>0\), one-sided at \(t=0\)) at every point \(t \ge 0\) if and only if \(A = \lim_{h \downarrow 0} \frac{Q_h - I}{h}\) exists at \(t = 0^+\) as a bounded operator, in which case:

\[ \frac{d}{dt} Q_t = Q_t A = A Q_t. \]

87.4.3 Step 3: Gårding’s Regularization Trick

To prove that \(A = \lim_{h \downarrow 0} \frac{Q_h - I}{h}\) exists, it suffices to show that \(t \mapsto Q_t\) is right-differentiable at some \(t \ge 0\). We employ a technique due to Lars Gårding.

Definition 87.1 (Gårding Averaging Operator) For any \(\varepsilon > 0\), define the linear operator \(B_\varepsilon\) on \(\mathcal{B}_b(S)\) by:

\[ B_\varepsilon = \frac{1}{\varepsilon} \int_0^\varepsilon Q_s \, ds, \tag{87.7}\]

meaning that for any \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\) and \(x \in S\):

\[ (B_\varepsilon f)(x) = \frac{1}{\varepsilon} \int_0^\varepsilon (Q_s f)(x) \, ds. \]

This is well defined: for fixed \(f\) and \(x\), the map \(s \mapsto (Q_sf)(x)\) is continuous and bounded by \(\|f\|_\infty\) (Step 1), so the Riemann integral exists and \(|(B_\varepsilon f)(x)| \le \|f\|_\infty\). Measurability of \(x \mapsto (B_\varepsilon f)(x)\) follows because it is the pointwise limit, as \(n \to \infty\), of the Riemann sums \(\frac{\varepsilon}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n (Q_{k\varepsilon/n}f)(x)\), each of which is a finite linear combination of the measurable functions \(Q_{k\varepsilon/n}f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\). Hence \(B_\varepsilon f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\) and \(B_\varepsilon \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}_b(S))\) with \(\|B_\varepsilon\|_{\text{op}} \le 1\).

Applying \(B_\varepsilon\) to \(Q_t\), and using the Chapman–Kolmogorov identity \(Q_s Q_t = Q_{s+t}\) (Equation 87.1), we obtain:

\[ B_\varepsilon Q_t = \frac{1}{\varepsilon} \int_0^\varepsilon Q_s Q_t \, ds = \frac{1}{\varepsilon} \int_0^\varepsilon Q_{s+t} \, ds = \frac{1}{\varepsilon} \int_t^{t+\varepsilon} Q_u \, du. \tag{87.8}\]

By Step 1, \(u \mapsto Q_u\) is continuous in the operator norm on all of \([0,\infty)\), and \(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}_b(S))\) is a Banach space (Corollary 86.1, Lecture 40.1); hence \(t \mapsto \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_t^{t+\varepsilon} Q_u\,du\) is the difference of two indefinite Riemann integrals of a continuous Banach-space-valued function and is therefore differentiable in both directions (two-sided for \(t>0\), one-sided at \(t=0\)) by the vector-valued Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, with derivative:

\[ \frac{d}{dt} \left( B_\varepsilon Q_t \right) = \frac{1}{\varepsilon} [Q_{t+\varepsilon} - Q_t]. \tag{87.9}\]

87.4.4 Step 4: Invertibility of \(B_\varepsilon\) for Small \(\varepsilon > 0\)

To recover the differentiability of \(Q_t\) from \(B_\varepsilon Q_t\), we show that \(B_\varepsilon\) is an invertible operator for sufficiently small \(\varepsilon > 0\).

Define \(T_\varepsilon := I - B_\varepsilon\). We attempt to construct the inverse \(C_\varepsilon := B_\varepsilon^{-1}\) using a Neumann geometric series:

\[ C_\varepsilon = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} T_\varepsilon^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (I - B_\varepsilon)^n. \tag{87.10}\]

If the series converges in operator norm, we have:

\[ \begin{aligned} C_\varepsilon B_\varepsilon &= \lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{n=0}^N T_\varepsilon^n (I - T_\varepsilon) \\ &= \lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{n=0}^N (T_\varepsilon^n - T_\varepsilon^{n+1}) \\ &= \lim_{N \to \infty} (I - T_\varepsilon^{N+1}) = I, \end{aligned} \]

and similarly \(B_\varepsilon C_\varepsilon = I\).

Since \(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}_b(S))\) is a Banach space (Corollary 86.1, Lecture 40.1), the Neumann series converges in operator norm whenever \(\|T_\varepsilon\|_{\text{op}} < 1\): absolute convergence, \(\sum_{n=0}^\infty \|T_\varepsilon^n\|_{\text{op}} \le \sum_{n=0}^\infty \|T_\varepsilon\|_{\text{op}}^n < \infty\), implies convergence in a Banach space. We estimate \(\|T_\varepsilon\|_{\text{op}}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \|I - B_\varepsilon\|_{\text{op}} &= \sup_{\|f\|_\infty = 1} \left\| f - \frac{1}{\varepsilon} \int_0^\varepsilon Q_s f \, ds \right\|_\infty \\ &= \sup_{\|f\|_\infty = 1} \left\| \frac{1}{\varepsilon} \int_0^\varepsilon (f - Q_s f) \, ds \right\|_\infty \\ &\le \frac{1}{\varepsilon} \int_0^\varepsilon \|I - Q_s\|_{\text{op}} \, ds. \end{aligned} \]

By the Mean Value Theorem for integrals, since \(s \mapsto \|I - Q_s\|_{\text{op}}\) is continuous on \([0,\varepsilon]\) (Step 1) and \(\|I - Q_0\|_{\text{op}} = 0\), there exists \(s^*=s^*(\varepsilon) \in [0, \varepsilon]\) such that:

\[ \frac{1}{\varepsilon} \int_0^\varepsilon \|I - Q_s\|_{\text{op}} \, ds = \|I - Q_{s^*(\varepsilon)}\|_{\text{op}}. \]

Since \(0 \le s^*(\varepsilon) \le \varepsilon\), we have \(s^*(\varepsilon) \downarrow 0\) as \(\varepsilon \downarrow 0\); combined with condition (Equation 87.2), i.e. \(\lim_{s \downarrow 0}\|I-Q_s\|_{\text{op}}=0\), this gives \(\lim_{\varepsilon \downarrow 0} \|I - Q_{s^*(\varepsilon)}\|_{\text{op}} = 0\). Thus, for all sufficiently small \(\varepsilon > 0\), we have:

\[ \|T_\varepsilon\|_{\text{op}} = \|I - B_\varepsilon\|_{\text{op}} < 1. \]

Hence, \(B_\varepsilon\) is invertible on \(\mathcal{B}_b(S)\) with continuous inverse \(C_\varepsilon = \sum_{n=0}^\infty (I - B_\varepsilon)^n\).

87.4.5 Step 5: Existence of the Generator \(A\) and Solution of the ODE

Using the invertibility of \(B_\varepsilon\) from Step 4, we write \(Q_t = C_\varepsilon (B_\varepsilon Q_t)\) for every \(t \ge 0\). Setting \(t = 0\) (recall \(Q_0 = I\) by Equation 87.1) and using the fixed, bounded operator \(C_\varepsilon\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{h \downarrow 0} \frac{Q_h - I}{h} &= \lim_{h \downarrow 0} C_\varepsilon \left( \frac{B_\varepsilon Q_h - B_\varepsilon Q_0}{h} \right) \\ &= C_\varepsilon \left[ \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0} (B_\varepsilon Q_t) \right] \\ &= C_\varepsilon \left( \frac{Q_{\varepsilon} - I}{\varepsilon} \right), \end{aligned} \]

where the second equality uses continuity of the bounded linear map \(X \mapsto C_\varepsilon X\) on \(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}_b(S))\) (so the limit can be passed inside \(C_\varepsilon\)), and the third equality is (Equation 87.9) evaluated at \(t=0\). Hence the limit \[ A := \lim_{h \downarrow 0} \frac{Q_h - I}{h} = C_\varepsilon \left( \frac{Q_\varepsilon - I}{\varepsilon} \right) \] exists in operator norm and is bounded, being a composition of the bounded operators \(C_\varepsilon\) and \(\varepsilon^{-1}(Q_\varepsilon - I)\): \[ \|A\|_{\text{op}} \le \|C_\varepsilon\|_{\text{op}} \cdot \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|Q_\varepsilon - I\|_{\text{op}} < \infty. \]

Since \(A\) now exists as a bounded linear operator, Step 2 applies and shows that \(t \mapsto Q_t\) is operator-norm differentiable at every \(t \ge 0\) (two-sided for \(t>0\), one-sided at \(t=0\)), with \[ \frac{d}{dt} Q_t = Q_t A = A Q_t, \qquad Q_0 = I, \tag{87.11}\] proving parts 1, 2, and 4 of the theorem.

Finally, we show part 3, \(Q_t = e^{tA}\). Since \(A\) is bounded, Equation 87.4 defines \(e^{tA}\) as a bounded operator for every \(t\), with the series converging absolutely and uniformly on compact \(t\)-intervals (comparison with \(\sum_n |t|^n\|A\|_{\text{op}}^n/n!=e^{|t|\|A\|_{\text{op}}}\)); in particular \(e^{-tA}\) is well defined and commutes with \(A\) and with \(Q_t\) for every \(t\) (via Equation 87.11 and the fact that \(A\) commutes with every power of itself). Define \(F(t) := Q_t e^{-tA} \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}_b(S))\). Term-by-term differentiation of the (uniformly convergent) power series for \(e^{-tA}\) gives \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{-tA} = -Ae^{-tA}\), so by the Leibniz (product) rule for operator-norm-differentiable \(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}_b(S))\)-valued functions and Equation 87.11, \[ F'(t) = (Q_tA)e^{-tA} + Q_t(-Ae^{-tA}) = Q_tAe^{-tA} - Q_tAe^{-tA} = 0 \quad \text{for all } t \ge 0. \] Since \(F\) is continuously differentiable with \(F'\equiv0\), the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus for Banach-space-valued functions gives \(F(t) = F(0) = Q_0e^{0} = I\) for all \(t\ge0\), i.e. \(Q_t = e^{tA}\). This same computation shows that \(e^{tA}\) is the unique operator-norm-differentiable solution of the Cauchy problem \(\frac{d}{dt}X_t = AX_t\), \(X_0 = I\): applying it to any such solution \(X_t\) in place of \(Q_t\) gives \(X_te^{-tA}\equiv I\), hence \(X_t = e^{tA}\). \(\blacksquare\)

87.5 Concluding Remarks

  1. Scope of Uniform Continuity: The condition \(\lim_{t \downarrow 0} \|Q_t - I\|_{\text{op}} = 0\), together with the Chapman–Kolmogorov property (Equation 87.1), guarantees that the generator \(A\) is a bounded operator.
  2. General Markov Chains vs. General State Spaces: For general continuous-state Markov processes (e.g., Brownian motion), uniform continuity in the operator norm is typically too restrictive, and \(A\) is generally an unbounded, densely defined operator under the weaker notion of strong continuity (\(L^p\) or \(C_0\)-semigroups). By contrast, for continuous-time Markov chains on discrete state spaces with bounded jump rates (i.e. \(\sup_i q_i < \infty\) for the Q-matrix), uniform operator-norm continuity does hold; this can be shown via uniformization (dominating the chain by a Poisson clock of rate \(\Lambda := \sup_i q_i\)), though we do not prove it here. The converse direction — passing from a bounded Q-matrix to operator-norm continuity — is thus the natural converse of Theorem 87.1, not a restatement of it.

87.6 Takeaways

  • A semigroup of Markov transition operators \((Q_t)_{t\ge0}\) (linear, positivity-preserving, \(Q_t1=1\), satisfying \(Q_0=I\) and the Chapman–Kolmogorov law \(Q_{s+t}=Q_sQ_t\)) that is merely continuous at the single point \(t=0\) in the operator norm is automatically continuous and (one-sided/two-sided) differentiable on all of \([0,\infty)\), with a bounded generator \(A\). This is the content of Theorem 87.1, re-proved here by a method different from Lecture 40.1.
  • The key mechanism, due to Gårding, is to replace the semigroup by its short-time averages \(B_\varepsilon = \varepsilon^{-1}\int_0^\varepsilon Q_s\,ds\). Operator-norm continuity at \(t=0\) forces \(B_\varepsilon \to I\) in operator norm as \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\), so \(B_\varepsilon\) is invertible (via a Neumann series) for small \(\varepsilon\); the generator is then extracted algebraically as \(A = C_\varepsilon(Q_\varepsilon - I)/\varepsilon\), where \(C_\varepsilon = B_\varepsilon^{-1}\).
  • Differentiability at an interior point \(t>0\) is a two-sided condition: both the right-quotient \((Q_{t+h}-Q_t)/h\) and the left-quotient \((Q_t-Q_{t-h})/h\) must converge to the same limit as \(h\downarrow0\). Both are controlled by the Chapman–Kolmogorov identity together with the uniform bound \(\|Q_s\|_{\text{op}}\le1\) and the operator-norm continuity of \(s\mapsto Q_s\) established in Step 1.
  • Once \(A\) is known to be bounded, \(Q_t=e^{tA}=\sum_{n\ge0}t^nA^n/n!\) is the unique operator-norm-differentiable solution of \(\frac{d}{dt}X_t=AX_t=X_tA\), \(X_0=I\); uniqueness follows from differentiating \(F(t)=X_te^{-tA}\) and finding \(F'\equiv0\).
  • The discrete-state-space Poisson process illustrates the theorem concretely: the row-sum operator norm gives \(\|Q_t-I\|_{\text{op}}=2(1-e^{-\lambda t})\to0\), so the semigroup is uniformly operator-norm continuous and its generator is the familiar bounded birth generator \(A_{ii}=-\lambda\), \(A_{i,i+1}=\lambda\), \(A_{ij}=0\) otherwise.
  • This “bounded generator” regime is the technically simplest case of semigroup theory. It fails for most continuous-state-space Markov processes (e.g. diffusions), where the generator is only densely defined and unbounded, and one must instead invoke the Hille–Yosida theorem for strongly continuous (\(C_0\)) semigroups — the subject of the general theory this lecture pair (40.1–40.2) specializes.
