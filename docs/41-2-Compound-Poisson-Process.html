89  Lecture 41.2: Compound Poisson Process

89.1 Setting and Standing Assumptions

Throughout, \(S\) is a countable set with the discrete \(\sigma\)-algebra \(2^S\), and \(B(S) = \ell_\infty(S)\) is the Banach space of bounded real-valued functions on \(S\) with the supremum norm \(\|f\|_\infty = \sup_{i \in S} |f(i)|\), as in Lecture 41.1. For a matrix \(\Theta = (\Theta(i,j))_{i,j \in S}\) we write \(\|\Theta\|_{\mathrm{row}} = \sup_{i} \sum_{j} |\Theta(i,j)|\) and, when \(\|\Theta\|_{\mathrm{row}} < \infty\), \(T_\Theta\) for the bounded operator \(T_\Theta f(i) = \sum_j \Theta(i,j) f(j)\); recall \(\|T_\Theta\|_{\mathrm{op}} = \|\Theta\|_{\mathrm{row}}\) (Lemma 88.1). For an operator \(T\) on \(B(S)\) we write \(T(i,j) \coloneqq T(\mathbf{1}_{\{j\}})(i)\) for its matrix entries.

Let \((q_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be a family of transition probability mass functions on \(S\) satisfying conditions 1–4 of Lecture 41.1 (non-negativity, unit row sums, \(q_0(i,j) = \delta_{ij}\), and the Chapman–Kolmogorov equations), and let \(Q_t = T_{q_t}\), so that \((Q_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is a semigroup on \(B(S)\) with \(Q_0 = I\).

A process \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) of \(S\)-valued random variables on a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) is a time-homogeneous Markov chain with transition function \((q_t)\) and initial law \(\mu\) if for all \(k \ge 0\), all \(0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_k\) and all \(i_0, \dots, i_k \in S\), \[ \mathbb{P}(X_{t_0} = i_0, X_{t_1} = i_1, \dots, X_{t_k} = i_k) = \mu(i_0) \prod_{\ell=1}^{k} q_{t_\ell - t_{\ell-1}}(i_{\ell-1}, i_\ell). \tag{89.1}\] In particular, \(\mathbb{P}(X_t = j \mid X_0 = i) = q_t(i,j)\) whenever \(\mathbb{P}(X_0 = i) > 0\); the function \(q_t(i,j)\) itself is defined for all \(i\), independently of \(\mu\).

89.2 The Bounded Generator Case

Suppose \((Q_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is continuous in operator norm at \(0\): \[ \lim_{t \downarrow 0} \|Q_t - I\|_{\mathrm{op}} = 0, \] equivalently (Equation 88.4, Lecture 41.1), \(\lim_{t \downarrow 0} \inf_{i \in S} q_t(i,i) = 1\). By the generator theorem (Theorem 87.1, Lecture 40.2; see also Equation 88.1), the limit \[ A = \lim_{t \downarrow 0} \frac{Q_t - I}{t} \] exists in operator norm, \(A\) is a bounded linear operator on \(B(S)\), and \[ Q_t = e^{tA} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^n}{n!} A^n, \quad t \ge 0, \] with convergence in operator norm.

By Corollary 88.1 (Lecture 41.1), the right derivatives \[ a(i, j) = \left. \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} q_t(i, j) \right|_{t=0^+} = \lim_{t \downarrow 0} \frac{q_t(i, j) - \delta_{ij}}{t} \] exist for all \(i, j \in S\), \(\|a\|_{\mathrm{row}} < \infty\), and \(A\) is represented by the matrix \(a\): \[ (Af)(i) = \sum_{j \in S} a(i, j) f(j), \qquad f \in B(S),\ i \in S. \] (This is not automatic for an arbitrary bounded operator on \(\ell_\infty(S)\), cf. Example 88.1; it holds here because \(A\) is the operator-norm limit of the matrix operators \((Q_t - I)/t\), Proposition 88.1.) Moreover, by Corollary 88.2, \(a(i,j) \ge 0\) for \(i \ne j\), \(a(i,i) \le 0\), \(\sum_{j} a(i,j) = 0\) (absolutely convergent), and \(\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}} = \|a\|_{\mathrm{row}} = 2 \sup_i |a(i,i)| < \infty\).

The small-time expansion holds uniformly in \(i, j \in S\): since \(q_t(i,j) - \delta_{ij} - t a(i,j) = \bigl((e^{tA} - I - tA)\mathbf{1}_{\{j\}}\bigr)(i)\), \[ \begin{aligned} \sup_{i,j \in S} \bigl| q_t(i, j) - \delta_{ij} - t\, a(i, j) \bigr| &\le \|e^{tA} - I - tA\|_{\mathrm{op}} \\ & \le e^{t\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}} - 1 - t\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}} = O(t^2) \quad \text{as } t \downarrow 0. \end{aligned} \] In particular, for \(i \neq j\) and any Markov chain \((X_t)\) with transition function \((q_t)\) and \(\mathbb{P}(X_0 = i) > 0\), \[ \mathbb{P}(X_t = j \mid X_0 = i) = a(i, j)\, t + O(t^2) \quad \text{as } t \downarrow 0. \]

Definition 89.1 (Conservative Q-Matrix with Bounded Rates) A matrix \(a = (a(i,j))_{i,j \in S}\) is a conservative Q-matrix if \(a(i,i) \in (-\infty, 0]\) for all \(i\), \(a(i,j) \ge 0\) for all \(i \ne j\), and \(\sum_{j \in S} a(i,j) = 0\) for all \(i \in S\). It has bounded rates if, in addition, \[ \sup_{i \in S} |a(i, i)| < \infty. \]

Remark 89.1 (Bounded Rates and Operator-Norm Continuity).

  1. If \((q_t)\) satisfies conditions 1–4 and \(\|Q_t - I\|_{\mathrm{op}} \to 0\), its generator matrix \(a\) is a conservative Q-matrix with bounded rates (Corollary 88.2).
  2. Conversely, every conservative Q-matrix \(a\) with bounded rates generates the operator-norm continuous Markov semigroup \(Q_t = e^{tT_a}\), whose generator matrix is \(a\) (Theorem 88.1).
  3. Statement 2 does not by itself say that every transition function whose derivatives at \(0\) form a bounded-rate Q-matrix is operator-norm continuous; that requires an additional argument. For a standard transition function (i.e. \(q_t(i,i) \to 1\) for each \(i\)) the derivatives \(q_i \coloneqq -q'_{ii}(0^+) \in [0, \infty]\) always exist and satisfy \(q_t(i,i) \ge e^{-q_i t}\) (Kolmogorov; see e.g. Chung, Markov Chains with Stationary Transition Probabilities, §II.2, or Anderson, Continuous-Time Markov Chains, Ch. 1). Granting this, if \(\sup_i q_i < \infty\) then \(1 - q_t(i,i) \le q_i t \le t \sup_k q_k\), so \(\inf_i q_t(i,i) \to 1\), i.e. \(\|Q_t - I\|_{\mathrm{op}} \to 0\) by Equation 88.4. We do not prove the inequality \(q_t(i,i) \ge e^{-q_i t}\) here.

Lemma 89.1 (Total Absolute Rate per Row) Let \(a\) be a conservative Q-matrix. Then for every \(i \in S\), \[ \sum_{j \in S} |a(i, j)| = 2 |a(i, i)|, \] and consequently \[ \|a\|_{\mathrm{row}} = \sup_{i \in S} \sum_{j \in S} |a(i, j)| = 2 \sup_{i \in S} |a(i, i)| \in [0, \infty]. \tag{89.2}\] In particular, \(\|a\|_{\mathrm{row}} < \infty\) if and only if \(a\) has bounded rates, in which case \(T_a\) is a bounded operator with \(\|T_a\|_{\mathrm{op}} = 2 \sup_i |a(i,i)|\) (Lemma 88.1).

Proof (Proof of Lemma 89.1). Fix \(i \in S\). Since \(a(i,i)\) is finite and \(\sum_j a(i,j) = 0\), we have \(\sum_{j \ne i} a(i,j) = -a(i,i) = |a(i,i)|\), the series having non-negative terms. Hence \[ \sum_{j \in S} |a(i, j)| = |a(i, i)| + \sum_{j \neq i} a(i, j) = 2 |a(i, i)|. \] Taking the supremum over \(i \in S\) yields Equation 89.2 (both sides possibly \(+\infty\)). Without the bounded-rates assumption the supremum may be infinite: on \(S = \mathbb{N}\), the conservative Q-matrix with \(a(i,i) = -i\), \(a(i,i+1) = i\) and all other entries \(0\) has \(\|a\|_{\mathrm{row}} = \infty\).

89.3 Uniformization (Randomization) Construction

By Equation 89.1, the finite-dimensional distributions of a Markov chain are determined by its initial law \(\mu\) and its transition function; in the bounded case the transition function is \(q_t(i,j) = e^{tA}(i,j)\), so it is determined by the generator matrix \(a\). We now construct such a chain by running a discrete-time Markov chain at the jump times of an independent Poisson process.

89.4 Rescaling and the Stochastic Transition Matrix

Let \(a\) be a conservative Q-matrix with bounded rates, \(A = T_a\), and let \(\lambda\) be a constant with \[ \lambda > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \lambda \ge \sup_{i \in S} |a(i, i)| = \tfrac{1}{2} \|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}, \] where the equality is Equation 89.2 together with Lemma 88.1. Define the matrix \(p\) and the operator \(P\) by \[ p(i,j) = \delta_{ij} + \frac{1}{\lambda} a(i,j), \qquad P = T_p = I + \frac{1}{\lambda} A. \tag{89.3}\] (The second identity holds because \(T_\delta = I\) and \(\Theta \mapsto T_\Theta\) is linear, Lemma 88.1.)

Lemma 89.2 (Properties of \(P\)) Under the above assumptions, \(p\) is a (row-)stochastic matrix, i.e. \(p(i,j) \ge 0\) and \(\sum_{j} p(i,j) = 1\) for all \(i, j \in S\). Consequently \(P\) is a positivity-preserving bounded operator on \(B(S)\) with \(P\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}\) and \(\|P\|_{\mathrm{op}} = 1\).

Proof (Proof of Lemma 89.2).

  1. Non-negativity: For \(i \neq j\), \(p(i, j) = \frac{1}{\lambda} a(i, j) \ge 0\). For \(i = j\), since \(a(i,i) \le 0\), \[ p(i, i) = 1 + \frac{1}{\lambda} a(i, i) = 1 - \frac{|a(i, i)|}{\lambda} \ge 0, \] because \(|a(i,i)| \le \sup_k |a(k,k)| \le \lambda\).
  2. Row sums: For every \(i \in S\), the series \(\sum_j a(i,j)\) converges absolutely (Lemma 89.1), so the sum may be split: \[ \sum_{j \in S} p(i, j) = \sum_{j \in S} \delta_{ij} + \frac{1}{\lambda} \sum_{j \in S} a(i, j) = 1 + 0 = 1. \]
  3. Norm: By 1–2, \(\|p\|_{\mathrm{row}} = \sup_i \sum_j p(i,j) = 1\), so \(\|P\|_{\mathrm{op}} = 1\) by Lemma 88.1; positivity and \(P\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}\) are immediate from 1–2.

By the Kolmogorov extension theorem (Lectures 16.1–16.2), for every probability measure \(\mu\) on \(S\) there exists a discrete-time Markov chain \((Y_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}_0}\) with initial law \(\mu\) and transition matrix \(p\), i.e. \[ \mathbb{P}(Y_0 = i_0, Y_1 = i_1, \dots, Y_n = i_n) = \mu(i_0) \prod_{\ell=1}^{n} p(i_{\ell-1}, i_\ell), \] for \(n \ge 0\) and \(i_0, \dots, i_n \in S\). Summing over intermediate states (all sums have non-negative terms) gives, for \(0 = n_0 \le n_1 \le \dots \le n_k\), \[ \mathbb{P}(Y_0 = i_0, Y_{n_1} = i_1, \dots, Y_{n_k} = i_k) = \mu(i_0) \prod_{\ell=1}^{k} p^{(n_\ell - n_{\ell-1})}(i_{\ell-1}, i_\ell), \tag{89.4}\] where \(p^{(m)}\) is the \(m\)-th matrix power of \(p\) (\(p^{(0)}(i,j) = \delta_{ij}\)). As shown in the proof of Theorem 88.1, \(P^m = T_{p^{(m)}}\), i.e. \(P^m(i,j) = p^{(m)}(i,j)\).

89.5 The Main Uniformization Theorem

For \(\theta \ge 0\) write \(\pi_\theta(m) = e^{-\theta} \theta^m / m!\), \(m \in \mathbb{N}_0\), for the Poisson(\(\theta\)) probability mass function.

Theorem 89.1 (Main Uniformization Theorem) Let \(a\) be a conservative Q-matrix with bounded rates on a countable set \(S\), let \(A = T_a\), and fix \(\lambda > 0\) with \(\lambda \ge \sup_{i \in S} |a(i, i)|\). Let \(p\) and \(P\) be as in Equation 89.3. On a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) let:

  1. \((Y_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}_0}\) be a discrete-time Markov chain on \(S\) with initial law \(\mu\) and transition matrix \(p\);
  2. \((N_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda\) (as defined in Lecture 38.1): \(N_0 = 0\); for \(0 = s_0 < s_1 < \dots < s_k\) the increments \(N_{s_1} - N_{s_0}, \dots, N_{s_k} - N_{s_{k-1}}\) are independent; and \(N_t - N_s \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda(t-s))\) for \(0 \le s < t\). We assume, modifying \(N\) on a null set if necessary, that every path \(t \mapsto N_t(\omega)\) is \(\mathbb{N}_0\)-valued and non-decreasing with \(N_0(\omega) = 0\);
  3. the processes \((Y_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}_0}\) and \((N_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be independent, i.e. \(\sigma(Y_n : n \in \mathbb{N}_0)\) and \(\sigma(N_t : t \ge 0)\) are independent.

Then \(X_t \coloneqq Y_{N_t}\), \(t \ge 0\), defines \(S\)-valued random variables, and \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is a time-homogeneous Markov chain with initial law \(\mu\) and transition function \[ q_t(i,j) = e^{tA}(i,j), \qquad e^{tA} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \pi_{\lambda t}(n) P^n, \] whose generator is \(A = \lambda(P - I)\) and whose generator matrix is \(a\). In particular, for all \(s, t \ge 0\) and \(f \in B(S)\), \[ \mathbb{E}\bigl[f(X_{t+s}) \mid \mathcal{F}^X_t\bigr] = (e^{sA} f)(X_t) \quad \text{a.s.}, \qquad \mathcal{F}^X_t \coloneqq \sigma(X_r : 0 \le r \le t). \]

Proof (Proof of Theorem 89.1).

89.5.1 Step 0: Measurability

Since every \(N_t(\omega) \in \mathbb{N}_0\), for each \(j \in S\) \[ \{X_t = j\} = \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{N}_0} \{Y_n = j\} \cap \{N_t = n\} \in \mathcal{F}, \] so \(X_t\) is a random variable. Also \(X_0 = Y_{N_0} = Y_0\), so \(X_0 \sim \mu\).

89.5.2 Step 1: The Semigroup Representation

By Equation 89.3, \(A = \lambda(P - I) = \lambda P - \lambda I\). Since \(\lambda I\) commutes with \(\lambda P\), \(e^{tA} = e^{-\lambda t I} e^{\lambda t P} = e^{-\lambda t} e^{\lambda t P}\), hence \[ e^{tA} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-\lambda t} \frac{(\lambda t)^n}{n!} P^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \pi_{\lambda t}(n)\, P^n, \tag{89.5}\] the series converging in operator norm because \(\|P^n\|_{\mathrm{op}} \le 1\). Evaluating at \(\mathbf{1}_{\{j\}}\) and at \(i\), \[ e^{tA}(i,j) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \pi_{\lambda t}(n)\, p^{(n)}(i,j). \tag{89.6}\] By Theorem 88.1, \(q_t(i,j) \coloneqq e^{tA}(i,j)\) satisfies conditions 1–4 of Lecture 41.1, \(e^{tA} = T_{q_t}\), and its generator matrix is \(a\).

89.5.3 Step 2: Finite-Dimensional Distributions

Fix \(0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_k\) and \(i_0, \dots, i_k \in S\), and put \(n_0 = 0\). Since every path of \(N\) is \(\mathbb{N}_0\)-valued, non-decreasing, and starts at \(0\), the events \(\{N_{t_1} = n_1, \dots, N_{t_k} = n_k\}\), \(0 \le n_1 \le \dots \le n_k\), partition \(\Omega\), and on such an event \(X_{t_\ell} = Y_{n_\ell}\). By countable additivity and the independence in hypothesis 3, \[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb{P}(X_{t_0} = i_0, \dots, X_{t_k} = i_k) \\ & = \sum_{0 \le n_1 \le \dots \le n_k} \mathbb{P}(Y_0 = i_0, Y_{n_1} = i_1, \dots, Y_{n_k} = i_k)\, \mathbb{P}(N_{t_1} = n_1, \dots, N_{t_k} = n_k). \end{aligned} \] Set \(m_\ell = n_\ell - n_{\ell-1}\); the map \((n_1, \dots, n_k) \mapsto (m_1, \dots, m_k)\) is a bijection from \(\{0 \le n_1 \le \dots \le n_k\}\) onto \(\mathbb{N}_0^k\). By the independence of the increments and their Poisson laws (hypothesis 2), \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(N_{t_1} = n_1, \dots, N_{t_k} = n_k) &= \mathbb{P}\bigl(N_{t_\ell} - N_{t_{\ell-1}} = m_\ell,\ \ell = 1, \dots, k\bigr) \\ & = \prod_{\ell=1}^{k} \pi_{\lambda(t_\ell - t_{\ell-1})}(m_\ell), \end{aligned} \] and by Equation 89.4, \(\mathbb{P}(Y_0 = i_0, Y_{n_1} = i_1, \dots, Y_{n_k} = i_k) = \mu(i_0) \prod_{\ell=1}^{k} p^{(m_\ell)}(i_{\ell-1}, i_\ell)\). Since all terms are non-negative, the sum over \(\mathbb{N}_0^k\) of a product factorizes (Tonelli for counting measure), and by Equation 89.6 \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(X_{t_0} = i_0, \dots, X_{t_k} = i_k) &= \mu(i_0) \prod_{\ell=1}^{k} \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \pi_{\lambda(t_\ell - t_{\ell-1})}(m)\, p^{(m)}(i_{\ell-1}, i_\ell) \\ & = \mu(i_0) \prod_{\ell=1}^{k} q_{t_\ell - t_{\ell-1}}(i_{\ell-1}, i_\ell). \end{aligned} \] This is Equation 89.1, so \((X_t)\) is a time-homogeneous Markov chain with initial law \(\mu\) and transition function \((q_t)\).

89.5.4 Step 3: The Markov Property

Fix \(t \ge 0\), \(s > 0\) (the case \(s = 0\) is trivial) and \(f \in B(S)\). Let \(\mathcal{C}_t\) be the collection consisting of \(\Omega\) and all cylinder events \(C = \{X_{r_1} = j_1, \dots, X_{r_m} = j_m\}\) with \(m \ge 1\), \(r_1, \dots, r_m \in [0, t]\) and \(j_1, \dots, j_m \in S\). Then \(\mathcal{C}_t\) is a \(\pi\)-system (the intersection of two cylinder events is a cylinder event or \(\emptyset = \{X_0 = j\} \cap \{X_0 = j'\}\) for \(j \ne j'\)) generating \(\mathcal{F}^X_t\).

Claim: \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_C f(X_{t+s})] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_C (Q_s f)(X_t)]\) for all \(C \in \mathcal{C}_t\). Writing \(C\) as a countable disjoint union over the values of \(X_0\) and \(X_t\) (and both sides are countably additive in \(C\), being integrals of bounded functions), it suffices to treat \(C = \{X_{r_0} = j_0, \dots, X_{r_m} = j_m\}\) with \(0 = r_0 < r_1 < \dots < r_m = t\) (if \(t = 0\), \(m = 0\)). By Step 2 applied to the times \(r_0 < \dots < r_m < t + s\), \[ \mathbb{P}(C \cap \{X_{t+s} = j\}) = \mathbb{P}(C)\, q_s(j_m, j), \qquad j \in S. \] Multiplying by \(f(j)\) and summing over \(j\) (absolutely convergent, since \(\sum_j \mathbb{P}(C \cap \{X_{t+s} = j\}) = \mathbb{P}(C)\) and \(f\) is bounded), \[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_C f(X_{t+s})] = \mathbb{P}(C)\, (Q_s f)(j_m) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_C (Q_s f)(X_t)]. \] The class of \(C \in \mathcal{F}^X_t\) satisfying the claimed identity is a \(\lambda\)-system (both sides are finite signed measures in \(C\)), so by Dynkin’s \(\pi\)–\(\lambda\) theorem it contains \(\mathcal{F}^X_t\). Since \((Q_s f)(X_t)\) is \(\mathcal{F}^X_t\)-measurable and bounded, this proves \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_{t+s}) \mid \mathcal{F}^X_t] = (Q_s f)(X_t)\) a.s. with \(Q_s = e^{sA}\).

89.6 Filtration Construction

The process \(X\) is Markov with respect to its natural filtration by Theorem 89.1. The following result, which is formulated in Driver’s lecture notes (Thm. 22.34), shows that it is Markov with respect to a larger filtration that carries the full information of \(Y\) up to the current number of jumps and of \(N\) up to the current time. Here \(p\) may be any stochastic matrix on \(S\).

Theorem 89.2 (Explicit Filtration Construction (Driver, Thm 22.34)) Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}_0}, \mathbb{P})\) be a filtered probability space and let \(p\) be a stochastic matrix on the countable set \(S\), with \(P = T_p\). Assume:

  1. \((Y_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}_0}\) is an \((\mathcal{F}_n)\)-adapted, \(S\)-valued process that is Markov with respect to \((\mathcal{F}_n)\) with transition matrix \(p\): \[ \mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} = j \mid \mathcal{F}_n) = p(Y_n, j) \quad \text{a.s.}, \qquad n \in \mathbb{N}_0,\ j \in S; \]
  2. \((N_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda > 0\) as in hypothesis 2 of Theorem 89.1 (in particular every path is \(\mathbb{N}_0\)-valued, non-decreasing, with \(N_0 = 0\));
  3. the \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\sigma(N_t : t \ge 0)\) is independent of \[ \mathcal{F}_\infty \coloneqq \sigma\Bigl(\bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{N}_0} \mathcal{F}_n\Bigr). \] (Note that \(\mathcal{F}_\infty\) is the terminal \(\sigma\)-algebra of the filtration, not the tail \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\bigcap_n \sigma(Y_m : m \ge n)\); independence from the latter would not suffice.)

Let \(\mathcal{F}^N_t \coloneqq \sigma(N_r : 0 \le r \le t)\) and define, for \(t \ge 0\), \[ \mathcal{G}_t \coloneqq \Bigl\{ B \in \mathcal{F} :\ B \cap \{N_t = n\} \in \mathcal{F}_n \vee \mathcal{F}^N_t \ \text{ for all } n \in \mathbb{N}_0 \Bigr\}, \tag{89.7}\] where \(\mathcal{F}_n \vee \mathcal{F}^N_t = \sigma(\mathcal{F}_n \cup \mathcal{F}^N_t)\). Let \(X_t \coloneqq Y_{N_t}\) and \(Q_s \coloneqq e^{\lambda s (P - I)}\). Then:

  1. \((\mathcal{G}_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is a filtration on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\).
  2. \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is adapted to \((\mathcal{G}_t)_{t \ge 0}\); in particular \(\mathcal{F}^X_t \subseteq \mathcal{G}_t\).
  3. \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is a time-homogeneous Markov process relative to \((\mathcal{G}_t)_{t \ge 0}\) with transition operators \((Q_s)_{s \ge 0}\): for all \(s, t \ge 0\) and \(f \in B(S)\), \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_{t+s}) \mid \mathcal{G}_t] = (Q_s f)(X_t) \quad \text{a.s.} \]

Proof (Proof of Theorem 89.2). Throughout, \(P^m = T_{p^{(m)}}\) (see the discussion after Lemma 89.2), and hypothesis 1 implies, by induction on \(m\) and the tower property, \[ \mathbb{E}[f(Y_{n+m}) \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = (P^m f)(Y_n) \quad \text{a.s.}, \qquad n, m \in \mathbb{N}_0,\ f \in B(S). \tag{89.8}\] As in Step 1 of the proof of Theorem 89.1 (which used only \(\|P\|_{\mathrm{op}} = 1\)), \(Q_s = \sum_{m \ge 0} \pi_{\lambda s}(m) P^m\) in operator norm, hence pointwise.

  1. Filtration property. \(\mathcal{G}_t\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra: \(\Omega \in \mathcal{G}_t\) since \(\{N_t = n\} \in \mathcal{F}^N_t\); if \(B \in \mathcal{G}_t\) then \(B^c \cap \{N_t = n\} = \{N_t = n\} \setminus (B \cap \{N_t = n\}) \in \mathcal{F}_n \vee \mathcal{F}^N_t\); and countable unions commute with intersecting by \(\{N_t = n\}\).

    Monotonicity: let \(s \le t\) and \(B \in \mathcal{G}_s\). Since every path of \(N\) is \(\mathbb{N}_0\)-valued and non-decreasing, \(\{N_t = n\} \subseteq \{N_s \le n\}\), so \[ B \cap \{N_t = n\} = \bigcup_{m=0}^{n} \bigl(B \cap \{N_s = m\}\bigr) \cap \{N_t = n\}. \] Each \(B \cap \{N_s = m\}\) lies in \(\mathcal{F}_m \vee \mathcal{F}^N_s \subseteq \mathcal{F}_n \vee \mathcal{F}^N_t\) (as \(m \le n\) and \(s \le t\)), and \(\{N_t = n\} \in \mathcal{F}^N_t\). Hence \(B \cap \{N_t = n\} \in \mathcal{F}_n \vee \mathcal{F}^N_t\) for all \(n\), i.e. \(B \in \mathcal{G}_t\).

  2. Adaptedness. For \(E \subseteq S\) and \(n \in \mathbb{N}_0\), \[ \{X_t \in E\} \cap \{N_t = n\} = \{Y_n \in E\} \cap \{N_t = n\} \in \mathcal{F}_n \vee \mathcal{F}^N_t, \] because \(Y_n\) is \(\mathcal{F}_n\)-measurable and \(\{N_t = n\} \in \mathcal{F}^N_t\). Also \(\{X_t \in E\} = \bigcup_n \{Y_n \in E\} \cap \{N_t = n\} \in \mathcal{F}\). Thus \(\{X_t \in E\} \in \mathcal{G}_t\). Since \((\mathcal{G}_t)\) is a filtration, \(\mathcal{F}^X_t \subseteq \mathcal{G}_t\).

  3. Markov property. Fix \(s, t \ge 0\), \(f \in B(S)\), and for \(m \in \mathbb{N}_0\) let \(D_m \coloneqq \{N_{t+s} - N_t = m\}\), so that \(\mathbb{P}(D_m) = \pi_{\lambda s}(m)\).

    Step (a). For all \(n, m \in \mathbb{N}_0\) and \(C \in \mathcal{F}_n \vee \mathcal{F}^N_t\): \[ \mathbb{E}\bigl[f(Y_{n+m}) \mathbf{1}_C \mathbf{1}_{D_m}\bigr] = \mathbb{E}\bigl[(P^m f)(Y_n) \mathbf{1}_C\bigr]\, \mathbb{P}(D_m). \tag{89.9}\] First let \(C = F \cap G\) with \(F \in \mathcal{F}_n\), \(G \in \mathcal{F}^N_t\). The variables \(f(Y_{n+m}) \mathbf{1}_F\) and \((P^m f)(Y_n) \mathbf{1}_F\) are bounded and \(\mathcal{F}_\infty\)-measurable, while \(\mathbf{1}_G\) and \(\mathbf{1}_G \mathbf{1}_{D_m}\) are \(\sigma(N_r : r \ge 0)\)-measurable. By hypothesis 3, then the independence of \(D_m\) from \(\mathcal{F}^N_t\) (the increment \(N_{t+s} - N_t\) is independent of \((N_{r_1}, \dots, N_{r_k})\) for all \(r_1, \dots, r_k \le t\) by independent increments, and hence of \(\mathcal{F}^N_t\) by a \(\pi\)–\(\lambda\) argument), then Equation 89.8, then hypothesis 3 again: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[f(Y_{n+m}) \mathbf{1}_F \mathbf{1}_G \mathbf{1}_{D_m}] &= \mathbb{E}[f(Y_{n+m}) \mathbf{1}_F]\, \mathbb{P}(G \cap D_m) \\ & = \mathbb{E}[f(Y_{n+m}) \mathbf{1}_F]\, \mathbb{P}(G)\, \mathbb{P}(D_m) \\ &= \mathbb{E}[(P^m f)(Y_n) \mathbf{1}_F]\, \mathbb{P}(G)\, \mathbb{P}(D_m) \\ &= \mathbb{E}[(P^m f)(Y_n) \mathbf{1}_F \mathbf{1}_G]\, \mathbb{P}(D_m). \end{aligned} \] The sets \(F \cap G\) form a \(\pi\)-system containing \(\Omega\) and generating \(\mathcal{F}_n \vee \mathcal{F}^N_t\). Both sides of Equation 89.9 are finite signed measures in \(C\) (as \(f\) is bounded), so the class of \(C\) satisfying Equation 89.9 is a \(\lambda\)-system, and Dynkin’s \(\pi\)–\(\lambda\) theorem gives Equation 89.9 for all \(C \in \mathcal{F}_n \vee \mathcal{F}^N_t\).

    Step (b). Let \(B \in \mathcal{G}_t\). Since \(N_t\) and \(N_{t+s} - N_t\) are \(\mathbb{N}_0\)-valued everywhere, the events \(\{N_t = n\} \cap D_m\), \(n, m \in \mathbb{N}_0\), partition \(\Omega\), and on \(\{N_t = n\} \cap D_m\) we have \(X_{t+s} = Y_{n+m}\). Applying Equation 89.9 with \(C = B \cap \{N_t = n\} \in \mathcal{F}_n \vee \mathcal{F}^N_t\) and using dominated convergence (\(f\) bounded) to interchange sums and expectations, \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[f(X_{t+s}) \mathbf{1}_B] &= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{E}\bigl[f(Y_{n+m}) \mathbf{1}_{B \cap \{N_t = n\}} \mathbf{1}_{D_m}\bigr] \\ &= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \pi_{\lambda s}(m)\, \mathbb{E}\bigl[(P^m f)(Y_n) \mathbf{1}_{B \cap \{N_t = n\}}\bigr] \\ &= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{E}\bigl[(Q_s f)(Y_n) \mathbf{1}_{B \cap \{N_t = n\}}\bigr] \\ & = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{E}\bigl[(Q_s f)(X_t) \mathbf{1}_{B \cap \{N_t = n\}}\bigr] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\bigl[(Q_s f)(X_t) \mathbf{1}_B\bigr]. \end{aligned} \] The interchange of \(\sum_m\) and \(\mathbb{E}\) is justified by \(\sum_m \pi_{\lambda s}(m) \|P^m f\|_\infty \le \|f\|_\infty\). Since \((Q_s f)(X_t)\) is bounded and \(\mathcal{G}_t\)-measurable by part 2, this proves \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_{t+s}) \mid \mathcal{G}_t] = (Q_s f)(X_t)\) a.s.

Remark 89.2 (Consistency with Theorem 89.1). If \(p = \delta + a/\lambda\) as in Equation 89.3, then \(Q_s = e^{s A}\) with \(A = \lambda(P - I) = T_a\). Taking \(\mathcal{F}_n = \sigma(Y_0, \dots, Y_n)\), hypothesis 3 of Theorem 89.2 is hypothesis 3 of Theorem 89.1, and conditioning the conclusion of Theorem 89.2 on \(\mathcal{F}^X_t \subseteq \mathcal{G}_t\) (tower property) recovers the Markov property in Theorem 89.1.

89.7 The Compound Poisson Process

Definition 89.2 (Compound Poisson Process) Let \(d \ge 1\), let \(\nu\) be a probability measure on \(\mathbb{Z}^d\), and let \(\lambda > 0\). On a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) let \(Y_0\) be a \(\mathbb{Z}^d\)-valued random variable with law \(\mu\), let \((\xi_k)_{k \ge 1}\) be i.i.d. with law \(\nu\), and let \((N_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda\) as in hypothesis 2 of Theorem 89.1, such that \(Y_0\), \((\xi_k)_{k \ge 1}\) and \((N_t)_{t \ge 0}\) are independent. The process \[ X_t \coloneqq Y_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{N_t} \xi_k, \qquad t \ge 0, \] is a compound Poisson process with rate \(\lambda\), jump distribution \(\nu\), and initial law \(\mu\).

With \(Y_n \coloneqq Y_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{n} \xi_k\) (a random walk with step law \(\nu\)), we have \(X_t = Y_{N_t}\): the compound Poisson process is exactly the uniformization of a random walk.

Proposition 89.1 (Compound Poisson Processes are Uniformized Chains) In the setting of Definition 89.2, let \(S = \mathbb{Z}^d\) and define \[ a(i, j) \coloneqq \lambda \bigl(\nu(\{j - i\}) - \delta_{ij}\bigr), \qquad i, j \in \mathbb{Z}^d. \] Then \(a\) is a conservative Q-matrix with bounded rates, \(\sup_i |a(i,i)| = \lambda(1 - \nu(\{0\})) \le \lambda\), and \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is a time-homogeneous Markov chain with initial law \(\mu\) and transition function \(q_t(i,j) = e^{tA}(i,j)\), where \(A = T_a\) acts by \[ (Af)(i) = \lambda \sum_{j \in \mathbb{Z}^d} \nu(\{j\}) \bigl(f(i + j) - f(i)\bigr), \qquad f \in B(\mathbb{Z}^d),\ i \in \mathbb{Z}^d. \] Moreover, \(X\) is Markov with respect to the filtration \((\mathcal{G}_t)\) of Theorem 89.2 built from \(\mathcal{F}_n \coloneqq \sigma(Y_0, \xi_1, \dots, \xi_n)\).

Proof (Proof of Proposition 89.1). Q-matrix. For \(i \ne j\), \(a(i,j) = \lambda \nu(\{j - i\}) \ge 0\); \(a(i,i) = -\lambda(1 - \nu(\{0\})) \in [-\lambda, 0]\); and \(\sum_j a(i,j) = \lambda\bigl(\sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}^d} \nu(\{k\}) - 1\bigr) = 0\). Thus \(a\) is a conservative Q-matrix with \(\sup_i |a(i,i)| = \lambda(1 - \nu(\{0\})) \le \lambda\), and the formula for \(Af\) follows from \(Af(i) = \sum_j a(i,j) f(j)\) by the substitution \(j \mapsto i + j\) (absolutely convergent).

Random walk. The matrix of Equation 89.3 is \(p(i,j) = \delta_{ij} + a(i,j)/\lambda = \nu(\{j - i\})\). With \(\mathcal{F}_n = \sigma(Y_0, \xi_1, \dots, \xi_n)\), \(Y_n\) is \(\mathcal{F}_n\)-measurable and \(\xi_{n+1}\) is independent of \(\mathcal{F}_n\), so \[ \mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} = j \mid \mathcal{F}_n) = \mathbb{P}(\xi_{n+1} = j - i)\big|_{i = Y_n} = p(Y_n, j) \quad \text{a.s.} \] Hence \((Y_n)\) is Markov with respect to \((\mathcal{F}_n)\) with transition matrix \(p\) and initial law \(\mu\); in particular it satisfies the finite-dimensional formula preceding Equation 89.4.

Independence. \(\sigma(Y_n : n \ge 0) \subseteq \mathcal{F}_\infty = \sigma(Y_0, \xi_1, \xi_2, \dots)\), which is independent of \(\sigma(N_t : t \ge 0)\) by assumption.

All hypotheses of Theorem 89.1 (with this \(\lambda\)) and of Theorem 89.2 are therefore satisfied, and both conclusions apply to \(X_t = Y_{N_t}\).

Remark 89.3 (Fictitious Jumps). If \(\nu(\{0\}) > 0\), some Poisson arrivals produce a “jump” of size \(0\), so \(X\) does not move. The same phenomenon occurs in general uniformization whenever \(\lambda > |a(i,i)|\): then \(p(i,i) > 0\), and at an arrival of \(N\) the chain stays at \(i\) with probability \(p(i,i)\). The actual jump rate out of \(i\) is \(\lambda(1 - p(i,i)) = |a(i,i)|\), which does not depend on the choice of \(\lambda\).

89.8 Takeaways

  • For a Markov chain on a countable state space with operator-norm continuous semigroup, the generator \(A\) is represented by a conservative Q-matrix \(a\) with bounded rates, \(\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}} = 2 \sup_i |a(i,i)|\), and \(q_t(i,j) = \delta_{ij} + t\, a(i,j) + O(t^2)\) uniformly in \(i, j\).
  • Uniformization: for any \(\lambda > 0\) with \(\lambda \ge \sup_i |a(i,i)|\), the matrix \(p = \delta + a/\lambda\) is stochastic and \(e^{tA} = \sum_n \pi_{\lambda t}(n) P^n\). Running a \(p\)-chain \(Y\) at the arrival times of an independent rate-\(\lambda\) Poisson process gives \(X_t = Y_{N_t}\), a Markov chain with transition function \(e^{tA}\).
  • With the right hypotheses (Markov with respect to \((\mathcal{F}_n)\), and \(N\) independent of \(\mathcal{F}_\infty\)), \(X\) is Markov with respect to the larger filtration \(\mathcal{G}_t\) of Equation 89.7, not only its natural filtration.
  • A compound Poisson process on \(\mathbb{Z}^d\) is the uniformization of a random walk. Its generator is \(Af(i) = \lambda \sum_j \nu(\{j\})(f(i+j) - f(i))\).
