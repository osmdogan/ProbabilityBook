90  Lecture 42.1 Jump-Hold Description

90.1 The Discrete-Time Jump–Hold Construction

Throughout, \(S\) is a countable state space equipped with the discrete \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{P}(S)\) (and, whenever topological notions such as right-continuity are used, with the discrete topology). Let \(q : S \times S \to [0,1]\) be a stochastic transition kernel (transition matrix) on \(S\), meaning that \(q(i,j) \ge 0\) for all \(i,j \in S\) and \[ \sum_{j \in S} q(i,j) = 1 \qquad \text{for all } i \in S. \tag{90.1}\]

We use the conventions \(\inf \emptyset := +\infty\) and \(0^0 := 1\). For \(b \in (0,1]\), an \(\mathbb{N}\)-valued random variable \(G\) (where \(\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, \dots\}\)) has the geometric distribution \(\mathrm{Geom}(b)\) if \[ \mathbb{P}(G = m) = g_b(m) := (1-b)^{m-1}\, b, \qquad m \in \mathbb{N}. \tag{90.2}\] Note that \(\sum_{m \ge 1} g_b(m) = 1\), and that \(G = 1\) almost surely when \(b = 1\).

As in Lecture 41.2, a process \((Y_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}_0}\) (where \(\mathbb{N}_0 = \{0,1,2,\dots\}\)) of \(S\)-valued random variables on a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) is a (time-homogeneous) Markov chain with initial law \(\mu\) and transition kernel \(q\) if \[ \mathbb{P}(Y_0 = i_0, Y_1 = i_1, \dots, Y_n = i_n) = \mu(i_0) \prod_{\ell=1}^{n} q(i_{\ell-1}, i_\ell), \tag{90.3}\] for all \(n \in \mathbb{N}_0,\ i_0, \dots, i_n \in S\).

Definition 90.1 (Absorbing States and Self-Loops) Let \(q\) be a stochastic transition kernel on \(S\).

  1. A state \(i \in S\) is called absorbing if \(q(i,i) = 1\). Since \(q(i,j) \ge 0\) for all \(j \in S\) and \(\sum_{j \in S} q(i,j) = 1\), a state \(i\) is absorbing if and only if \(q(i,j) = 0\) for all \(j \neq i\).
  2. The kernel \(q\) has a self-loop at \(i \in S\) if \(q(i,i) > 0\). A chain with kernel \(q\) may then remain at \(i\) for several consecutive steps; these “fictitious transitions” \(i \to i\) are exactly what the jump chain below removes.

(In parts of the literature a chain is called lazy if \(q(i,i) \ge \tfrac12\) for all \(i \in S\), e.g. \(q = \tfrac12(I + q')\) for a stochastic kernel \(q'\). We do not use that notion here.)

Suppose now that \(q\) has no absorbing states, so that \(q(i,i) < 1\) (equivalently, \(1 - q(i,i) > 0\)) for every \(i \in S\). Under this assumption, we define the jump transition kernel \(\tilde{q} : S \times S \to [0,1]\) by \[ \tilde{q}(i,j) := \begin{cases} \dfrac{q(i,j)}{1 - q(i,i)}, & \text{if } i \neq j, \\[8pt] 0, & \text{if } i = j. \end{cases} \tag{90.4}\]

Let us verify that \(\tilde{q}\) is indeed a valid stochastic transition kernel on \(S\):

  • Non-negativity: For every \(i, j \in S\), since \(q(i,j) \ge 0\) and \(1 - q(i,i) > 0\), we have \(\tilde{q}(i,j) \ge 0\).
  • Row sums equal to \(1\): For every \(i \in S\), using Equation 90.1 (all series have non-negative terms, so the diagonal term may be split off), \[ \sum_{j \in S} \tilde{q}(i,j) = \sum_{j \neq i} \tilde{q}(i,j) = \sum_{j \neq i} \frac{q(i,j)}{1 - q(i,i)} = \frac{1 - q(i,i)}{1 - q(i,i)} = 1. \]

This kernel governs the embedded jump chain associated with a Markov chain with kernel \(q\), as formalized below.

Proposition 90.1 (Discrete-Time Jump–Hold Proposition) Let \((Y_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}_0}\) be a Markov chain on \(S\) with initial law \(\mu\) and transition kernel \(q\), defined on a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\), where \(q\) has no absorbing states (\(q(i,i) < 1\) for all \(i \in S\)). Define the jump times \((\tau_k)_{k \in \mathbb{N}_0}\) recursively by \(\tau_0 := 0\) and, for \(k \ge 0\), \[ \tau_{k+1} := \begin{cases} \inf\{n > \tau_k : Y_n \neq Y_{\tau_k}\}, & \text{on } \{\tau_k < \infty\}, \\ +\infty, & \text{on } \{\tau_k = \infty\}. \end{cases} \tag{90.5}\] On \(\{\tau_k < \infty\}\) set \(Z_k := Y_{\tau_k}\), and for \(k \ge 1\) let \(\rho_k := \tau_k - \tau_{k-1}\) denote the holding times. (Events concerning \(Z_0, \dots, Z_n\) or \(\rho_1, \dots, \rho_n\) are understood as subsets of \(\{\tau_n < \infty\}\).) Then:

  1. \(\tau_k < \infty\) \(\mathbb{P}\)-almost surely for every \(k \in \mathbb{N}_0\), and the embedded process \((Z_k)_{k \in \mathbb{N}_0}\) is a Markov chain on \(S\) with initial law \(\mu\) and transition kernel \(\tilde{q}\) given by Equation 90.4.
  2. Let \(n \ge 1\) and \(i_0, i_1, \dots, i_n \in S\) with \(\mathbb{P}(B) > 0\), where \(B := \{Z_0 = i_0, Z_1 = i_1, \dots, Z_n = i_n\}\) (this forces \(i_{k-1} \neq i_k\) for \(1 \le k \le n\)). Put \(b_k := 1 - q(i_{k-1}, i_{k-1}) \in (0,1]\). Then, under \(\mathbb{P}(\cdot \mid B)\), the holding times \(\rho_1, \dots, \rho_n\) are independent with \(\rho_k \sim \mathrm{Geom}(b_k)\), i.e. \[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb{P}(\rho_1 = m_1, \dots, \rho_n = m_n \mid B) \\ &= \prod_{k=1}^{n} q(i_{k-1}, i_{k-1})^{m_k-1}\big(1 - q(i_{k-1}, i_{k-1})\big), \qquad m_1, \dots, m_n \in \mathbb{N}. \end{aligned} \]

Remark 90.1 (Measurability of Jump and Holding Times). Each \(\tau_k\) is an \(\mathbb{N}_0 \cup \{\infty\}\)-valued random variable: by induction on \(k\), \(\{\tau_k = m\} \in \sigma(Y_0, \dots, Y_m)\) for every \(m \in \mathbb{N}_0\). To see this, recall that \(\tau_{k+1} = \inf\{n > \tau_k : Y_n \neq Y_{\tau_k}\}\). The event \(\{\tau_{k+1} = m\}\) says: the \((k+1)\)-th jump occurs exactly at time \(m\). For this to happen:

  1. The \(k\)-th jump must have occurred at some earlier time \(r < m\), so \(\tau_k = r\) and \(r < m\).
  2. At that time \(r\), the chain is in some state \(i = Y_r\).
  3. The chain stays in \(i\) throughout times \(r, r+1, \dots, m-1\) (no jump before \(m\)).
  4. At time \(m\), the chain leaves \(i\): \(Y_m \neq i\).

Combining: \(\tau_{k+1} = m\) iff there exist \(r < m\) and \(i \in S\) such that \(\tau_k = r\) and \(Y_r = Y_{r+1} = \dots = Y_{m-1} = i\) and \(Y_m \neq i\): \[ \{\tau_{k+1} = m\} = \bigcup_{r < m} \bigcup_{i \in S} \{\tau_k = r,\ Y_r = Y_{r+1} = \dots = Y_{m-1} = i,\ Y_m \neq i\}. \] Consequently \(Z_k = \sum_{m} \mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_k = m\}} Y_m\) is measurable on \(\{\tau_k < \infty\}\).

Proof (Proof of Proposition 90.1). Step 1: Path decomposition. Fix \(n \ge 1\), states \(i_0, \dots, i_n \in S\) with \(i_{k-1} \neq i_k\) for \(1 \le k \le n\), and \(m = (m_1, \dots, m_n) \in \mathbb{N}^n\). Put \(n_0 := 0\), \(n_k := m_1 + \dots + m_k\), \(c_k := q(i_{k-1}, i_{k-1}) \in [0,1)\) and \(b_k := 1 - c_k\). We claim that \[ \begin{aligned} &\{\tau_1 = n_1, \dots, \tau_n = n_n,\ Z_0 = i_0, \dots, Z_n = i_n\} \\ & = \bigcap_{k=1}^{n} \{Y_r = i_{k-1} \text{ for } n_{k-1} \le r < n_k,\ Y_{n_k} = i_k\} \\ & =: C_m. \end{aligned} \tag{90.6}\] This path decomposition identity says that specifying the jump times \(\tau_1 = n_1, \dots, \tau_n = n_n\) and the embedded chain states \(Z_0 = i_0, \dots, Z_n = i_n\) is exactly the same as prescribing the entire path of \(Y\) up to time \(n_n\). Each factor in the intersection describes one “segment” of the path. For \(k = 1, \dots, n\), the event \[ \{Y_r = i_{k-1} \text{ for } n_{k-1} \le r < n_k,\ Y_{n_k} = i_k\} \] says: the chain stays in \(i_{k-1}\) throughout the interval \([n_{k-1}, n_k)\), then jumps to \(i_k\) at time \(n_k\). Taking the intersection over all \(k\) pieces the full trajectory together: \[ \underbrace{i_0, i_0, \dots, i_0}_{n_1 \text{ steps}},\ \underbrace{i_1, i_1, \dots, i_1}_{m_2 \text{ steps}},\ \underbrace{i_2, \dots}_{m_3 \text{ steps}},\ \dots \] The chain sits in state \(i_{k-1}\) for exactly \(m_k = n_k - n_{k-1}\) steps, then moves to \(i_k\).

  • “\(\subseteq\)”: On the left-hand event, for each \(k\) we have \(\tau_{k-1} = n_{k-1} < \infty\) and \(Y_{\tau_{k-1}} = i_{k-1}\). By the definition of \(\tau_k\) as an infimum, \(Y_r = Y_{\tau_{k-1}} = i_{k-1}\) for \(\tau_{k-1} \le r < \tau_k\), and \(Y_{\tau_k} = Z_k = i_k\).
  • “\(\supseteq\)”: On \(C_m\) we show by induction on \(k\) that \(\tau_k = n_k\) and \(Z_k = i_k\). This holds for \(k = 0\). If \(\tau_{k-1} = n_{k-1}\) and \(Z_{k-1} = i_{k-1}\), then \(Y_r = i_{k-1}\) for \(n_{k-1} < r < n_k\) and \(Y_{n_k} = i_k \neq i_{k-1}\), so \(\tau_k = n_k\) and \(Z_k = i_k\).

Step 2: Path probabilities. Recall \(C_m = \bigcap_{k=1}^n \{Y_r = i_{k-1} \text{ for } n_{k-1} \le r < n_k,\ Y_{n_k} = i_k\}\). Written out explicitly, the path looks like: \[ \underbrace{i_0, \dots, i_0}_{m_1 \text{ times}},\ i_1,\ \underbrace{i_1, \dots, i_1}_{m_2 - 1 \text{ more}},\ i_2,\ \dots,\ i_{n-1},\ \underbrace{i_{n-1}, \dots}_{m_n - 1 \text{ more}},\ i_n \] For the \(k\)-th segment: the chain sits at \(i_{k-1}\) for \(m_k - 1\) steps (self-transitions), then makes one genuine jump to \(i_k\). The FDD formula for a Markov chain gives \(\mathbb{P}(Y_0 = y_0, \ldots, Y_N = y_N) = \mu(y_0)\prod_{j=0}^{N-1} q(y_j, y_{j+1})\). Reading off the transitions segment by segment:

  • Starting state \(i_0\): contributes \(\mu(i_0)\).
  • Within segment \(k\): the chain makes \(m_k - 1\) self-transitions \(i_{k-1} \to i_{k-1}\), each contributing a factor \(q(i_{k-1}, i_{k-1}) = c_k\). Total: \(c_k^{m_k - 1}\).
  • At the end of segment \(k\): one genuine transition \(i_{k-1} \to i_k\), contributing \(q(i_{k-1}, i_k)\).

Multiplying over all \(k\) gives the first line: \[ \mathbb{P}(C_m) = \mu(i_0)\prod_{k=1}^n c_k^{m_k-1}\, q(i_{k-1}, i_k). \]

Now substitute \(q(i_{k-1}, i_k) = b_k\,\tilde{q}(i_{k-1}, i_k)\), which holds because for \(i \neq j\): \[ \tilde{q}(i,j) = \frac{q(i,j)}{1 - q(i,i)} = \frac{q(i,j)}{b_k} \implies q(i_{k-1}, i_k) = b_k\,\tilde{q}(i_{k-1}, i_k). \] Substituting: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(C_m) &= \mu(i_0)\prod_{k=1}^n c_k^{m_k-1} b_k\,\tilde{q}(i_{k-1},i_k) \\ & = \mu(i_0)\prod_{k=1}^n \tilde{q}(i_{k-1},i_k)\prod_{k=1}^n \underbrace{c_k^{m_k-1} b_k}_{= g_{b_k}(m_k)}. \end{aligned} \tag{90.7}\] The last factor is the geometric PMF: \(g_{b_k}(m_k) = c_k^{m_k - 1} b_k\) is the probability of waiting exactly \(m_k\) steps before the first successful jump, where each step independently succeeds with probability \(b_k = 1 - q(i_{k-1}, i_{k-1})\). The probability of the full path splits cleanly into two independent parts:

  1. \(\mu(i_0)\prod_k \tilde{q}(i_{k-1}, i_k)\) — the probability that the jump chain visits states \(i_0, i_1, \dots, i_n\) in order (this is the FDD formula for a Markov chain with kernel \(\tilde{q}\)).
  2. \(\prod_k g_{b_k}(m_k)\) — the probability that the holding times are \(m_1, \dots, m_n\), as independent geometric random variables.

The events \(C_m\), \(m \in \mathbb{N}^n\), are pairwise disjoint with union \(\{\tau_n < \infty, Z_0 = i_0, \dots, Z_n = i_n\}\). Summing Equation 90.7 over \(m \in \mathbb{N}^n\) (a sum of non-negative terms over a product of counting measures, so it factorizes by Tonelli) and using \(\sum_{m \ge 1} g_{b}(m) = 1\), we obtain \[ \mathbb{P}(\tau_n < \infty,\ Z_0 = i_0, \dots, Z_n = i_n) = \mu(i_0) \prod_{k=1}^{n} \tilde{q}(i_{k-1}, i_k). \tag{90.8}\] This identity also holds if \(i_{k-1} = i_k\) for some \(k\): then both sides vanish, the left side because \(Z_k \neq Z_{k-1}\) on \(\{\tau_k < \infty\}\) by the definition of \(\tau_k\), and the right side because \(\tilde{q}(i,i) = 0\). It holds trivially for \(n = 0\).

Step 3: Summing Equation 90.8 over all \(i_0, \dots, i_n \in S\) (successively over \(i_n, i_{n-1}, \dots, i_1\), using that \(\tilde{q}\) is stochastic, and then over \(i_0\)) gives \(\mathbb{P}(\tau_n < \infty) = 1\) for every \(n\): \[ \mathbb{P}(\tau_n < \infty) = \sum_{i_0,\dots,i_n} \mu(i_0)\prod_{k=1}^n \tilde{q}(i_{k-1}, i_k). \] The text says to do this successively, starting from \(i_n\): \[ \sum_{i_n \in S} \tilde{q}(i_{n-1}, i_n) = 1 \quad \text{(since $\tilde{q}$ is stochastic)}. \] This collapses the \(i_n\) sum to \(1\), leaving a product up to \(k = n-1\). Repeating for \(i_{n-1}, i_{n-2}, \dots, i_1\) (each time using that \(\tilde{q}\) is stochastic), all factors of \(\tilde{q}\) collapse to \(1\), leaving: \(\mathbb{P}(\tau_n < \infty) = \sum_{i_0 \in S} \mu(i_0) = 1\). So every jump time is finite almost surely — the chain never gets “stuck” and always keeps jumping.

Hence Equation 90.8 reads \(\mathbb{P}(Z_0 = i_0, \dots, Z_n = i_n) = \mu(i_0) \prod_{k=1}^{n} \tilde{q}(i_{k-1}, i_k)\), which is Equation 90.3 for the kernel \(\tilde{q}\). Thus \((Z_k)\) is a Markov chain with initial law \(\mu\) and kernel \(\tilde{q}\).

Step 4: Since \(\mathbb{P}(\tau_n < \infty) = 1\), equation Equation 90.8 gives \(\mathbb{P}(B) = \mu(i_0)\prod_{k=1}^n \tilde{q}(i_{k-1}, i_k)\). For this to be positive, every factor \(\tilde{q}(i_{k-1}, i_k)\) must be positive. But \(\tilde{q}(i,i) = 0\) by construction (the jump chain never stays put), so consecutive states must differ. The holding time \(\rho_k = \tau_k - \tau_{k-1}\), so \(\rho_k = m_k\) iff \(\tau_k = n_k\) (since \(n_k = m_1 + \dots + m_k\) is the cumulative sum). Therefore: \[ \begin{aligned} &\{\rho_1 = m_1, \dots, \rho_n = m_n\} \cap B \\ &= \{\tau_1 = n_1, \dots, \tau_n = n_n,\ Z_0 = i_0, \dots, Z_n = i_n\} \\ &= C_m, \end{aligned} \] where the last equality is Equation 90.6. So the numerator of the conditional probability is exactly \(\mathbb{P}(C_m)\).Then \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(\rho_1 = m_1, \dots, \rho_n = m_n \mid B) &= \frac{\mathbb{P}(C_m)}{\mathbb{P}(B)}\\ & = \frac{\mu(i_0)\prod_{k=1}^n \tilde{q}(i_{k-1},i_k)\prod_{k=1}^n g_{b_k}(m_k)}{\mu(i_0)\prod_{k=1}^n \tilde{q}(i_{k-1},i_k)}\\ & = \prod_{k=1}^n g_{b_k}(m_k). \end{aligned} \] The \(\mu(i_0)\prod_k \tilde{q}(i_{k-1},i_k)\) factor cancels exactly between numerator (Equation 90.7) and denominator (Equation 90.8), leaving only the product of geometric PMFs.

A joint probability mass function on \(\mathbb{N}^n\) that is a product of probability mass functions is the law of independent random variables with these marginals (sum over all but one coordinate to identify the marginals). Hence, under \(\mathbb{P}(\cdot \mid B)\), \(\rho_1, \dots, \rho_n\) are independent with \(\rho_k \sim \mathrm{Geom}(b_k)\).

90.2 The Continuous-Time Jump–Hold Theorem

We now develop an analogous Jump–Hold characterization for right-continuous, time-homogeneous, continuous-time Markov chains on a countable state space \(S\) whose transition semigroup is operator-norm continuous.

Recall from Lectures 40.1–41.2 that the transition operators \(Q_t = T_{q_t}\) of a Markov chain act on \(B(S) = \ell_\infty(S)\), and that \((Q_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is operator-norm continuous (also called uniformly continuous) if \(\lim_{t \downarrow 0} \|Q_t - I\|_{\mathrm{op}} = 0\), equivalently \(\lim_{t \downarrow 0} \inf_{i \in S} q_t(i,i) = 1\) (Equation 88.4). This holds if and only if \((Q_t)_{t \ge 0}\) has a bounded generator \(A\), in which case \(Q_t = e^{tA}\) for all \(t \ge 0\) (Theorem 86.1). Moreover, \(A = T_a\) for a matrix \(a = (a(i,j))_{i,j \in S}\) that is a conservative Q-matrix with bounded rates (Corollary 88.2, Definition 89.1), i.e.

  • \(a(i,j) \ge 0\) for all \(i \neq j\);
  • \(a_i := -a(i,i) = \sum_{j \neq i} a(i,j) \in [0, \infty)\) for all \(i \in S\);
  • \(\sup_{i \in S} a_i < \infty\);

and conversely every such matrix generates an operator-norm continuous Markov semigroup (Theorem 88.1). By Lemma 89.1, \(\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}} = 2 \sup_{i \in S} a_i\).

The jump times of a path are defined through an infimum over uncountably many times. They are only meaningful under a path-regularity assumption, which we now analyze for a single deterministic path.

Lemma 90.1 (Jump Times of a Right-Continuous Path) Let \(x : [0, \infty) \to S\) be right-continuous for the discrete topology on \(S\), i.e. for every \(t \ge 0\) there exists \(\varepsilon > 0\) with \(x(s) = x(t)\) for all \(s \in [t, t + \varepsilon)\). Define \(j_0 := 0\) and, for \(k \ge 0\), \(j_{k+1} := \inf\{t > j_k : x(t) \neq x(j_k)\}\) if \(j_k < \infty\), and \(j_{k+1} := \infty\) if \(j_k = \infty\). Then for every \(k\) with \(j_k < \infty\):

  1. \(x(t) = x(j_k)\) for all \(t \in [j_k, j_{k+1})\);

  2. if \(j_{k+1} < \infty\), then \(j_{k+1} > j_k\) and \(x(j_{k+1}) \neq x(j_k)\);

  3. \(j_{k+1} = \inf\{q \in \mathbb{Q} : q > j_k,\ x(q) \neq x(j_k)\}\);

  4. there exists \(\varepsilon > 0\) such that \(x(q) = x(j_k)\) for all rational \(q \in (j_k, j_k + \varepsilon)\).

Proof (Proof of Lemma 90.1). Right-continuity here means: at every time \(t\), the path stays constant on some interval \([t, t+\varepsilon)\). The jump times are defined pathwise: \(j_0 = 0\) and \(j_{k+1} = \inf\{t > j_k : x(t) \neq x(j_k)\}\).

  1. is immediate from the definition of \(j_{k+1}\) as an infimum.

  2. If \(j_{k+1} < \infty\) then \(j_{k+1} > j_k\) (the path cannot jump instantaneously) and \(x(j_{k+1}) \neq x(j_k)\) (the path actually changes value at the jump). Both follow from right-continuity: at \(j_k\) there is a small interval where the path is constant, forcing \(j_{k+1}\) strictly away from \(j_k\); at \(j_{k+1}\) right-continuity forces a genuine change, otherwise the path would still be constant past \(j_{k+1}\) contradicting minimality.

  3. This is the most important part for measurability. It says you can compute the jump times from the values of the path on the rationals alone. Let \(U := \{t > j_k : x(t) \neq x(j_k)\}\). Since \(U \cap \mathbb{Q} \subseteq U\), we have \(\inf U \le \inf(U \cap \mathbb{Q})\) immediately. Take any \(t \in U\), so \(t > j_k\) and \(x(t) \neq x(j_k)\). By right-continuity of \(x\) at \(t\), there exists \(\eta > 0\) such that \[ x(s) = x(t) \quad \text{for all } s \in [t, t + \eta). \] Since \(x(t) \neq x(j_k)\), this gives \(x(s) \neq x(j_k)\) for all \(s \in [t, t+\eta)\). In particular every rational \(q \in [t, t + \eta)\) satisfies \(q > j_k\) (since \(t > j_k\)) and \(x(q) \neq x(j_k)\), so \(q \in U \cap \mathbb{Q}\). By density of \(\mathbb{Q}\), the interval \([t, t+\eta)\) contains rationals arbitrarily close to \(t\), so \(\inf(U \cap \mathbb{Q}) \le t\). Since \(t \in U\) was arbitrary, \(\inf(U \cap \mathbb{Q}) \le \inf U\).Combining: \(\inf(U \cap \mathbb{Q}) = \inf U = j_{k+1}\), with both sides equal to \(+\infty\) when \(U = \emptyset\).

  4. After each jump time \(j_k\) there is a rational interval \((j_k, j_k + \varepsilon)\) on which the path equals \(x(j_k)\). This is just right-continuity applied at the rational level, and it ensures the infimum in (c) is not attained by rationals arbitrarily close to \(j_k\) itself.

For an \(S\)-valued process \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) we define its jump times \((J_k)_{k \in \mathbb{N}_0}\) pathwise as in Lemma 90.1, i.e. \(J_0 := 0\) and, for \(k \ge 0\), \[ J_{k+1} := \begin{cases} \inf\{t > J_k : X_t \neq X_{J_k}\}, & \text{on } \{J_k < \infty\}, \\ +\infty, & \text{on } \{J_k = \infty\}, \end{cases} \tag{90.9}\] and, on \(\{J_k < \infty\}\), the embedded chain \(Z_k := X_{J_k}\) and (for \(k \ge 1\)) the holding times \(H_k := J_k - J_{k-1}\). Fix a cemetery point \(\partial \notin S\) and put \(S_\partial := S \cup \{\partial\}\), \(\hat{Z}_k := Z_k\) on \(\{J_k < \infty\}\) and \(\hat{Z}_k := \partial\) on \(\{J_k = \infty\}\).

The next lemma makes precise a key philosophical point: for right-continuous processes, jump times and jump states are entirely determined by the finite-dimensional distributions. Its consequence is that any result proved for a particular right-continuous realization automatically transfers to any other right-continuous process with the same FDDs.

Lemma 90.2 (Jump Functionals Are Determined by the Finite-Dimensional Distributions) Let \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) and \((X'_t)_{t \ge 0}\) on \((\Omega', \mathcal{F}', \mathbb{P}')\) be \(S\)-valued processes all of whose paths are right-continuous for the discrete topology, and assume they have the same finite-dimensional distributions. Then each \(J_k\) (resp. \(J'_k\)) is a \([0,\infty]\)-valued random variable, each \(\hat{Z}_k\) (resp. \(\hat{Z}'_k\)) is an \(S_\partial\)-valued random variable, and for every \(n \in \mathbb{N}_0\) \[ \mathrm{Law}_{\mathbb{P}}\big((J_k, \hat{Z}_k)_{k \le n}\big) = \mathrm{Law}_{\mathbb{P}'}\big((J'_k, \hat{Z}'_k)_{k \le n}\big) \qquad \text{on } ([0,\infty] \times S_\partial)^{n+1}. \]

Proof (Proof of Lemma 90.2). The goal is to construct a single measurable map \[ \Phi_n : S^{\mathbb{Q}_+} \to ([0,\infty] \times S_\partial)^{n+1}, \] such that \(\Phi_n\bigl((x(q))_{q \in \mathbb{Q}_+}\bigr) = (J_k, \hat{Z}_k)_{k \le n}\) for every right-continuous path \(x\). The result then follows because two processes with the same FDDs induce the same law on \(S^{\mathbb{Q}_+}\), so applying \(\Phi_n\) to both gives equal joint laws for the jump data.

Step 1: Define \(\Phi_n\). Let \(\Omega_0 = S^{\mathbb{Q}_+}\) with product \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{G}_0\) and coordinates \((y_q)_{q \in \mathbb{Q}_+}\). Set \(\hat{j}_0 = 0\) and \(\hat{z}_0 = y_0\). Given \((\hat{j}_k, \hat{z}_k)\) already \(\mathcal{G}_0\)-measurable, define: \[ \hat{j}_{k+1} := \begin{cases} \inf\{q \in \mathbb{Q}_+ : q > \hat{j}_k,\ y_q \neq \hat{z}_k\}, & \hat{z}_k \in S, \\ +\infty, & \hat{z}_k = \partial. \end{cases} \]

Measurability: for \(t \in (0,\infty]\), \[ \{\hat{j}_{k+1} < t\} = \bigcup_{\substack{q \in \mathbb{Q}_+ \\ q < t}} \bigl(\{\hat{j}_k < q\} \cap \{y_q \neq \hat{z}_k\}\bigr) \in \mathcal{G}_0. \] For the state at the new jump, observe that \(\hat{j}_{k+1}\) may be irrational, so \(y_{\hat{j}_{k+1}}\) is not defined. Right-continuity says the path holds its new value \(x(j_{k+1})\) on a whole right-interval \((j_{k+1}, j_{k+1}+\varepsilon)\); this interval contains rationals. So define: \[ \hat{z}_{k+1} := i \quad \text{if} \quad \exists\, q \in \mathbb{Q}_+,\ q > \hat{j}_{k+1} : y_r = i\ \text{ for all rational } r \in (\hat{j}_{k+1}, q], \] and \(\hat{z}_{k+1} := \partial\) otherwise. For measurability, write for each \(i \in S\): \[ \{\hat{z}_{k+1} = i\} = \bigcup_{q \in \mathbb{Q}_+} \left[\{\hat{j}_{k+1} < q\} \cap \bigcap_{\substack{r \in \mathbb{Q}_+ \\ r \le q}} \bigl(\{\hat{j}_{k+1} \ge r\} \cup \{y_r = i\}\bigr)\right] \in \mathcal{G}_0, \] a countable union of countable intersections of \(\mathcal{G}_0\)-sets. By induction, \(\Phi_n := (\hat{j}_k, \hat{z}_k)_{k \le n}\) is \(\mathcal{G}_0\)-measurable.

Step 2: Consistency. For a right-continuous path \(x\) and \(y = x|_{\mathbb{Q}_+}\), prove by induction that \(\hat{j}_k(y) = j_k\) and \(\hat{z}_k(y) = x(j_k)\) when \(j_k < \infty\).

  • Base: \(\hat{j}_0 = 0 = j_0\) and \(\hat{z}_0 = y_0 = x(0)\).
  • Inductive step: Assuming \(\hat{j}_k(y) = j_k\) and \(\hat{z}_k(y) = x(j_k)\), the formula for \(\hat{j}_{k+1}\) gives \(\inf\{q \in \mathbb{Q}_+ : q > j_k,\ x(q) \neq x(j_k)\} = j_{k+1}\) by lem-rc-jump-times (c). For the state: by lem-rc-jump-times (d), there exists \(\varepsilon > 0\) such that \(x(q) = x(j_{k+1})\) for all rational \(q \in (j_{k+1}, j_{k+1}+\varepsilon)\). Taking \(q\) to be any rational in this interval, all rationals \(r \in (j_{k+1}, q]\) satisfy \(y_r = x(j_{k+1})\), so \(\hat{z}_{k+1}(y) = x(j_{k+1})\).

Hence \((J_k, \hat{Z}_k)_{k \le n} = \Phi_n\bigl((X_q)_{q \in \mathbb{Q}_+}\bigr)\) pointwise on \(\Omega\), and likewise for \(X'\).

Step 3: Transfer. The law of \((X_q)_{q \in \mathbb{Q}_+}\) on \((\Omega_0, \mathcal{G}_0)\) is a probability measure that agrees with the law of \((X'_q)_{q \in \mathbb{Q}_+}\) on every cylinder set \(\{y_{q_1} = s_1, \dots, y_{q_r} = s_r\}\), since these are finite-dimensional distributions. These cylinder sets form a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\mathcal{G}_0\), so by the uniqueness theorem the two laws coincide on all of \(\mathcal{G}_0\). Applying the same measurable map \(\Phi_n\) yields \(\mathrm{Law}_\mathbb{P}(J_k, \hat{Z}_k)_{k \le n} = \mathrm{Law}_{\mathbb{P}'}(J'_k, \hat{Z}'_k)_{k \le n}\).

Theorem 90.1 (Continuous-Time Jump–Hold Theorem) Let \(a = (a(i,j))_{i,j \in S}\) be a conservative Q-matrix with bounded rates (Definition 89.1), let \(A := T_a\), and write \[ a_i := -a(i,i) = \sum_{j \neq i} a(i,j) \quad \text{for all } i \in S, \qquad \sup_{i \in S} a_i < \infty. \] Assume that there are no absorbing states, i.e., \(a_i > 0\) for all \(i \in S\). Let \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be a time-homogeneous Markov chain on \(S\) with initial law \(\mu\) and transition function \(q_t(i,j) = e^{tA}(i,j)\) (in the sense of Equation 89.1), defined on a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\), and assume that every path \(t \mapsto X_t(\omega)\) is right-continuous for the discrete topology on \(S\). Let \(J_k\), \(Z_k\), \(H_k\) be as in Equation 90.9. Then:

  1. Each \(J_k\) is a \([0,\infty]\)-valued random variable, \(J_k < \infty\) \(\mathbb{P}\)-almost surely for every \(k \in \mathbb{N}_0\), and \(J_k \uparrow \infty\) \(\mathbb{P}\)-almost surely. Moreover, \(X_t = Z_k\) for all \(t \in [J_k, J_{k+1})\) (for every \(\omega\) and every \(k\) with \(J_k(\omega) < \infty\)). The embedded process \((Z_k)_{k \in \mathbb{N}_0}\) is a Markov chain on \(S\) with initial law \(\mu\) and transition kernel \[ \tilde{p}(i,j) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{a(i,j)}{a_i}, & \text{if } i \neq j, \\[8pt] 0, & \text{if } i = j. \end{cases} \tag{90.10}\]
  2. Let \(n \ge 1\) and \(i_0, i_1, \dots, i_n \in S\) with \(\mathbb{P}(B) > 0\), where \(B := \{Z_0 = i_0, Z_1 = i_1, \dots, Z_n = i_n\}\) (this forces \(i_{k-1} \neq i_k\)). Then, under \(\mathbb{P}(\cdot \mid B)\), the holding times \(H_1, \dots, H_n\) are independent with \[ H_k \sim \mathrm{Exp}(a_{i_{k-1}}) \qquad \text{for each } k = 1, \dots, n. \]

Before proving Theorem 90.1, we record a lemma on geometric sums of independent exponential random variables used in the second part of the proof.

Lemma 90.3 (Geometric Compounding of Exponential Random Variables) On a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\), let \((E_\ell)_{\ell \in \mathbb{N}}\) be i.i.d. with \(E_\ell \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)\) for some rate \(\lambda > 0\), and let \(\rho_1, \dots, \rho_n\) be independent \(\mathbb{N}\)-valued random variables with \(\rho_k \sim \mathrm{Geom}(b_k)\), \(b_k \in (0,1]\), such that \((\rho_1, \dots, \rho_n)\) is independent of \((E_\ell)_{\ell \in \mathbb{N}}\). Define \[ W_0 := 0, \qquad W_m := E_1 + \dots + E_m \quad (m \ge 1), \] and set \(\tau_0 := 0\), \(\tau_k := \rho_1 + \dots + \rho_k\), and \[ H_k := W_{\tau_k} - W_{\tau_{k-1}} = \sum_{\ell = \tau_{k-1} + 1}^{\tau_k} E_\ell \qquad (k = 1, \dots, n). \] Then \(H_1, \dots, H_n\) are independent random variables with \[ H_k \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda b_k) \qquad \text{for each } k = 1, \dots, n. \]

Proof (Proof of Lemma 90.3). For \(m \in \mathbb{N}\), the sum of \(m\) independent \(\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)\) random variables has the Gamma density \[ \gamma_m(x) := \frac{\lambda^m x^{m-1}}{(m-1)!} e^{-\lambda x}, \qquad x > 0. \] This is the marginal density of the \(m\)-th arrival time in ?lem-joint-density: integrate \(\lambda^m e^{-\lambda x_m}\) over \((x_1, \dots, x_{m-1}) \in D_{m-1}(x_m)\), using ?lem-leb-simplex.

For \(m = (m_1, \dots, m_n) \in \mathbb{N}^n\) with partial sums \(n_0 := 0\), \(n_k := m_1 + \dots + m_k\), put \(U^{(m)}_k := \sum_{\ell = n_{k-1}+1}^{n_k} E_\ell\). On \(\{\rho = m\}\) we have \(H_k = U^{(m)}_k\); in particular \(H_k = \sum_{m} \mathbf{1}_{\{\rho = m\}} U^{(m)}_k\) is measurable. The variables \(U^{(m)}_1, \dots, U^{(m)}_n\) are functions of disjoint blocks of the independent family \((E_\ell)\), hence independent (?lem-grouping-sigma-fields), and \(U^{(m)}_k\) has density \(\gamma_{m_k}\).

Fix Borel sets \(C_1, \dots, C_n \subseteq \mathbb{R}\). Using the independence of \((\rho_1, \dots, \rho_n)\) and \((E_\ell)\), then Tonelli’s theorem for counting measure and for Lebesgue measure (all integrands are non-negative), \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(H_1 \in C_1, \dots, H_n \in C_n) &= \sum_{m \in \mathbb{N}^n} \mathbb{P}(\rho = m)\, \mathbb{P}\big(U^{(m)}_1 \in C_1, \dots, U^{(m)}_n \in C_n\big) \\ &= \sum_{m \in \mathbb{N}^n} \prod_{k=1}^{n} g_{b_k}(m_k) \int_{C_k \cap (0,\infty)} \gamma_{m_k}(x)\, dx\\ &= \prod_{k=1}^{n} \int_{C_k \cap (0,\infty)} \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} g_{b_k}(m)\, \gamma_m(x)\, dx. \end{aligned} \] For \(b \in (0,1]\) and \(x > 0\), \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} (1-b)^{m-1} b\, \frac{\lambda^m x^{m-1}}{(m-1)!} e^{-\lambda x} &= b \lambda e^{-\lambda x} \sum_{r=0}^{\infty} \frac{\big((1-b)\lambda x\big)^r}{r!} \\ & = b\lambda e^{-\lambda x} e^{(1-b)\lambda x} = \lambda b\, e^{-\lambda b x}. \end{aligned} \] Therefore \(\mathbb{P}(H_1 \in C_1, \dots, H_n \in C_n) = \prod_{k=1}^{n} \int_{C_k \cap (0,\infty)} \lambda b_k e^{-\lambda b_k x}\, dx\). Taking all but one \(C_k\) equal to \(\mathbb{R}\) shows \(H_k \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda b_k)\), and the product formula then shows that \(H_1, \dots, H_n\) are independent.

Proof (Proof of Theorem 90.1). The strategy is as follows. We construct, via uniformization, a second right-continuous chain \(X'\) with the same finite-dimensional distributions whose jumps can be identified explicitly. We prove both parts for \(X'\), and then transfer them to \(X\) by Lemma 90.2.

Step 1: The uniformized kernel. Choose a constant \(\lambda\) with \[ \lambda > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \lambda \ge \sup_{i \in S} a_i = \frac{1}{2}\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}} \] (Lemma 89.1). For instance, \(\lambda := \sup_{i} a_i\) is admissible, since it is finite and positive because \(a_i > 0\). Define the matrix \(p\) by \[ p(i,j) = \delta_{ij} + \frac{1}{\lambda} a(i,j) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{a(i,j)}{\lambda}, & \text{if } i \neq j, \\[8pt] 1 - \dfrac{a_i}{\lambda}, & \text{if } i = j, \end{cases} \tag{90.11}\] where \(\delta_{ij}\) denotes the Kronecker delta. By Lemma 89.2, \(p\) is a stochastic kernel on \(S\). Since \(a_i > 0\), \(p(i,i) = 1 - a_i/\lambda < 1\) for every \(i \in S\), so \(p\) has no absorbing states (although \(p\) may have self-loops). Its jump kernel Equation 90.4 is, for \(i \neq j\), \[ \frac{p(i,j)}{1 - p(i,i)} = \frac{a(i,j)/\lambda}{a_i/\lambda} = \frac{a(i,j)}{a_i} = \tilde{p}(i,j), \] and it vanishes on the diagonal, so it coincides with Equation 90.10.

Step 2: A right-continuous realization. On some probability space \((\Omega', \mathcal{F}', \mathbb{P}')\) (e.g. a product space, using the Kolmogorov extension theorem for \(Y\)), let \((Y_m)_{m \in \mathbb{N}_0}\) be a Markov chain with initial law \(\mu\) and kernel \(p\), and let \((E_\ell)_{\ell \in \mathbb{N}}\) be i.i.d. \(\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)\) random variables such that \(\sigma(Y_m : m \in \mathbb{N}_0)\) and \(\sigma(E_\ell : \ell \in \mathbb{N})\) are independent. Put \(T_0 := 0\), \(T_m := E_1 + \dots + E_m\), and \[ N_t := \sup\{m \in \mathbb{N}_0 : T_m \le t\}, \qquad t \ge 0, \] as in ?eq-poisson-explicit-def. Almost surely, \(E_\ell > 0\) for all \(\ell\) and \(T_m \to \infty\) (by the strong law of large numbers, \(T_m / m \to 1/\lambda\)). Restricting \(\Omega'\) to this event of full probability, which changes no joint law, we may assume that these properties hold for every \(\omega'\). Then, for every \(\omega'\), \(0 = T_0 < T_1 < T_2 < \dots\), \(T_m \to \infty\), and \(t \mapsto N_t\) is \(\mathbb{N}_0\)-valued, non-decreasing and right-continuous with \(N_0 = 0\), \(N_t = m\) for \(t \in [T_m, T_{m+1})\), and in particular \(N_{T_m} = m\). By Lecture 35.2 (?thm-poisson-measure), \(N\) has independent increments with \(N_t - N_s \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda(t-s))\). Moreover, \(\sigma(N_t : t \ge 0) \subseteq \sigma(E_\ell : \ell \in \mathbb{N})\) is independent of \(\sigma(Y_m : m \in \mathbb{N}_0)\). Hence all hypotheses of Theorem 89.1 hold, and \[ X'_t := Y_{N_t}, \qquad t \ge 0, \tag{90.12}\] is a Markov chain with initial law \(\mu\) and transition function \(e^{tA}\). Thus \(X'\) has the same finite-dimensional distributions as \(X\). Every path of \(X'\) is right-continuous: given \(t\), \(N_s = N_t\) for \(s \in [t, T_{N_t + 1})\), so \(X'_s = X'_t\) there.

Step 3: Pathwise identification of the jumps of \(X'\). Let \((\tau_k)\) and \((\rho_k)\) be the jump times and holding times of \(Y\) as in Proposition 90.1 (with kernel \(p\)). By Proposition 90.1 (Part 1), the event \(\Omega'_1 := \{\tau_k < \infty \text{ for all } k\}\) has \(\mathbb{P}'(\Omega'_1) = 1\). Let \(J'_k, Z'_k, H'_k\) denote the jump times, embedded chain and holding times of \(X'\). We claim that on \(\Omega'_1\), \[ J'_k = T_{\tau_k} \quad \text{and} \quad Z'_k = Y_{\tau_k} \qquad \text{for all } k \in \mathbb{N}_0. \tag{90.13}\] For \(k = 0\) this holds since \(J'_0 = 0 = T_0\) and \(Z'_0 = X'_0 = Y_0\). Assume it holds for \(k\). For \(t > T_{\tau_k}\) we have \(N_t \ge N_{T_{\tau_k}} = \tau_k\). If moreover \(t < T_{\tau_{k+1}}\), then \(\tau_k \le N_t < \tau_{k+1}\), so \(X'_t = Y_{N_t} = Y_{\tau_k} = Z'_k\) by the definition of \(\tau_{k+1}\). At \(t = T_{\tau_{k+1}}\), which is \(> T_{\tau_k}\) because \(\tau_{k+1} > \tau_k\) and \((T_m)\) is strictly increasing, we have \(N_t = \tau_{k+1}\) and \(X'_t = Y_{\tau_{k+1}} \neq Y_{\tau_k}\). Hence the infimum defining \(J'_{k+1}\) is attained at \(T_{\tau_{k+1}}\), i.e. \(J'_{k+1} = T_{\tau_{k+1}}\) and \(Z'_{k+1} = Y_{\tau_{k+1}}\).

Consequently, on \(\Omega'_1\), \[ H'_k = T_{\tau_k} - T_{\tau_{k-1}} = \sum_{\ell = \tau_{k-1} + 1}^{\tau_k} E_\ell \quad (k \ge 1), \qquad J'_k = T_{\tau_k} \ge T_k \to \infty, \] since \(\tau_k \ge k\).

Step 4: Both parts for \(X'\). Part 1. By Step 3, \(J'_k < \infty\) for all \(k\) and \(J'_k \to \infty\) on \(\Omega'_1\), and \((Z'_k) = (Y_{\tau_k})\) there. By Proposition 90.1 (Part 1) and Step 1, \((Z'_k)\) is a Markov chain with initial law \(\mu\) and kernel \(\tilde{p}\).

Part 2. Fix \(n \ge 1\) and \(i_0, \dots, i_n\), and put \(B' := \{Z'_0 = i_0, \dots, Z'_n = i_n\}\) and \(B_Y := \{Y_{\tau_0} = i_0, \dots, Y_{\tau_n} = i_n\} \in \sigma(Y_m : m \in \mathbb{N}_0)\). By Step 3, \(B' \cap \Omega'_1 = B_Y \cap \Omega'_1\), so \(\mathbb{P}'(B') = \mathbb{P}'(B_Y)\); assume this is positive. By Proposition 90.1 (Part 2), under \(\mathbb{P}'(\cdot \mid B_Y)\) the variables \(\rho_1, \dots, \rho_n\) are independent with \[ \rho_k \sim \mathrm{Geom}(b_k), \qquad b_k := 1 - p(i_{k-1}, i_{k-1}) = \frac{a_{i_{k-1}}}{\lambda} \in (0,1]. \] Under \(\mathbb{P}'(\cdot \mid B_Y)\), the sequence \((E_\ell)\) is still i.i.d. \(\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)\) and independent of \(\sigma(Y_m : m \in \mathbb{N}_0)\). Indeed, for \(C \in \sigma(Y_m : m \in \mathbb{N}_0)\) and \(D \in \sigma(E_\ell : \ell \in \mathbb{N})\), since \(C \cap B_Y \in \sigma(Y_m : m \in \mathbb{N}_0)\), \[ \mathbb{P}'(C \cap D \mid B_Y) = \frac{\mathbb{P}'(C \cap B_Y)\, \mathbb{P}'(D)}{\mathbb{P}'(B_Y)} = \mathbb{P}'(C \mid B_Y)\, \mathbb{P}'(D), \] and taking \(C = \Omega'\) gives \(\mathbb{P}'(D \mid B_Y) = \mathbb{P}'(D)\). As \(\rho_1, \dots, \rho_n\) are \(\sigma(Y_m : m \in \mathbb{N}_0)\)-measurable, Lemma 90.3 applies under \(\mathbb{P}'(\cdot \mid B_Y)\). Since \(H'_k\) equals the block sums of Step 3 almost surely, under \(\mathbb{P}'(\cdot \mid B_Y) = \mathbb{P}'(\cdot \mid B')\) the variables \(H'_1, \dots, H'_n\) are independent with \[ H'_k \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda b_k) = \mathrm{Exp}(a_{i_{k-1}}) \qquad (k = 1, \dots, n). \]

Step 5: Transfer to \(X\). By Lemma 90.2, each \(J_k\) is a random variable and, for every \(n\), \((J_k, \hat{Z}_k)_{k \le n}\) under \(\mathbb{P}\) has the same law as \((J'_k, \hat{Z}'_k)_{k \le n}\) under \(\mathbb{P}'\). All assertions of the theorem are statements about these joint laws:

  • \(\mathbb{P}(J_n < \infty) = \mathbb{P}'(J'_n < \infty) = 1\) for every \(n\).
  • \(\mathbb{P}(Z_0 = i_0, \dots, Z_n = i_n) = \mathbb{P}'(Z'_0 = i_0, \dots, Z'_n = i_n) = \mu(i_0) \prod_{k=1}^{n} \tilde{p}(i_{k-1}, i_k)\), so \((Z_k)\) is a Markov chain with initial law \(\mu\) and kernel \(\tilde{p}\).
  • Since \((J_k)\) is non-decreasing, for every \(M \in \mathbb{N}\), \(\mathbb{P}(\sup_k J_k \le M) = \lim_{n} \mathbb{P}(J_n \le M) = \lim_n \mathbb{P}'(J'_n \le M) = \mathbb{P}'(\sup_k J'_k \le M) = 0\); hence \(J_k \uparrow \infty\) almost surely. By Lemma 90.1 (b), the finite \(J_k\) are strictly increasing.
  • On \(\{J_n < \infty\}\), \((H_1, \dots, H_n)\) is the same measurable function of \((J_0, \dots, J_n)\) as \((H'_1, \dots, H'_n)\) is of \((J'_0, \dots, J'_n)\). Hence \(\mathbb{P}(B \cap \{H_1 \in C_1, \dots, H_n \in C_n\}) = \mathbb{P}'(B' \cap \{H'_1 \in C_1, \dots, H'_n \in C_n\})\) for Borel sets \(C_k\), and \(\mathbb{P}(B) = \mathbb{P}'(B')\). Thus the conditional law of \((H_1, \dots, H_n)\) given \(B\) equals that of \((H'_1, \dots, H'_n)\) given \(B'\), which was identified in Step 4.

Finally, \(X_t = Z_k\) for \(t \in [J_k, J_{k+1})\) holds for every \(\omega\) by Lemma 90.1 (a). This completes the proof.

90.3 Scope and Limitations: Bounded vs. Unbounded Generators

Remark 90.2 (Applicability of the Jump–Hold Description). The Jump–Hold description established in Theorem 90.1 applies to every right-continuous time-homogeneous, continuous-time Markov chain \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) that has no absorbing states and whose transition semigroup \((Q_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is operator-norm continuous, meaning that \[ \|Q_t - I\|_{\mathrm{op}} \to 0 \qquad \text{as } t \downarrow 0, \] or equivalently (Equation 88.4), in terms of the diagonal transition probabilities, \[ \inf_{i \in S} q_t(i,i) \to 1 \qquad \text{as } t \downarrow 0. \tag{90.14}\] As noted earlier, operator-norm continuity of \((Q_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is equivalent to \((Q_t)_{t \ge 0}\) possessing a bounded generator \(A\), in which case \(Q_t = e^{tA}\) for all \(t \ge 0\).

  1. Path regularity cannot be dropped. The finite-dimensional distributions alone do not determine the jump times. Let \(X\) be as in Theorem 90.1, let \(\phi : S \to S\) be any map without fixed points (which exists since \(|S| \ge 2\) when there are no absorbing states), and let \(U \sim \mathrm{Exp}(1)\) be independent of \(X\). Define \(X'_t := X_t\) for \(t \neq U\) and \(X'_U := \phi(X_U)\). Then \(\mathbb{P}(X'_t = X_t) = 1\) for every \(t\), so \(X'\) has the same finite-dimensional distributions as \(X\). However, its first jump time is \(J'_1 = \min(J_1, U)\) almost surely, and given \(X_0 = i\) this is \(\mathrm{Exp}(a_i + 1)\)-distributed rather than \(\mathrm{Exp}(a_i)\)-distributed. Moreover, on \(\{U < J_1\}\) the path of \(X'\) leaves \(X_0\) at time \(U\) and returns immediately, so \(J'_2 = J'_1 = U\) (a holding time equal to \(0\)). Right-continuity is exactly what rules out such modifications (Lemma 90.2).

  2. Unbounded rates. In many important applications (such as birth–death processes with unbounded rates or general jump processes on infinite state spaces), \(\sup_{i \in S} a_i = \infty\). Then \((Q_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is not operator-norm continuous as \(t \downarrow 0\) (i.e., \(\|Q_t - I\|_{\mathrm{op}} \not\to 0\)), and in fact it is not even strongly continuous on \(B(S) = \ell_\infty(S)\). By a theorem of Lotz, every strongly continuous semigroup on \(\ell_\infty\) is automatically norm continuous, because \(\ell_\infty\) is a Grothendieck space with the Dunford–Pettis property. A Hille–Yosida-type description with an unbounded generator is instead available for the action on measures: on \(\ell_1(S)\), the semigroup \(\nu \mapsto \nu Q_t\) is strongly continuous as soon as \(q_t(i,i) \to 1\) for each \(i\). Indeed, \(\|\delta_i Q_t - \delta_i\|_{\ell_1} = 2(1 - q_t(i,i))\), and dominated convergence handles general \(\nu \in \ell_1(S)\).

  3. What fails, and what survives. Uniformization requires a finite \(\lambda \ge \sup_i a_i\), so the proof above breaks down when rates are unbounded. The jump–hold structure itself survives for right-continuous chains with a conservative Q-matrix whose rates \(a_i\) are all finite: the jump chain still has kernel \(a(i,j)/a_i\) and the holding times are still conditionally independent \(\mathrm{Exp}(a_i)\). This can be proved with the strong Markov property instead of uniformization. However, it now describes the process only up to the explosion time \(J_\infty := \lim_k J_k\), which may be finite with positive probability (infinitely many jumps in finite time). If some \(a_i = \infty\) (an instantaneous state), there is no first jump time at all. We will explore these unbounded-generator regimes in later lectures.

90.4 Takeaways

  • Discrete jump chain. For a Markov chain with kernel \(q\) and no absorbing states, deleting the fictitious transitions \(i \to i\) yields the jump chain \(Z_k = Y_{\tau_k}\). It is Markov with kernel \(\tilde{q}(i,j) = q(i,j)/(1 - q(i,i))\) for \(i \neq j\) and \(\tilde{q}(i,i) = 0\). Conditionally on the jump-chain path \(i_0, \dots, i_n\), the holding times are independent \(\mathrm{Geom}(1 - q(i_{k-1}, i_{k-1}))\) (Proposition 90.1). Both facts come from a single computation: the probability of a path factorizes as \(\mu(i_0) \prod_k \tilde{q}(i_{k-1}, i_k) \cdot \prod_k g_{b_k}(m_k)\) (Equation 90.7).
  • Geometric compounding. A \(\mathrm{Geom}(b)\) number of i.i.d. \(\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)\) variables sums to an \(\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda b)\) variable, and disjoint blocks of this kind are independent (Lemma 90.3).
  • Continuous-time Jump–Hold Theorem. Consider a right-continuous chain with bounded rates and no absorbing states. The jump chain is Markov with kernel \(\tilde{p}(i,j) = a(i,j)/a_i\). Given the jump-chain path, the holding times are independent \(\mathrm{Exp}(a_{i_{k-1}})\). The jump times increase to \(\infty\) almost surely (no explosion), and \(X_t = Z_k\) on \([J_k, J_{k+1})\) (Theorem 90.1). Thus the chain stays in state \(i\) for an \(\mathrm{Exp}(a_i)\) time and then jumps to \(j \neq i\) with probability \(a(i,j)/a_i\).
  • Proof strategy. Uniformization with \(\lambda \ge \sup_i a_i\) gives the realization \(X'_t = Y_{N_t}\), whose jumps occur exactly at \(T_{\tau_k}\), the Poisson epochs where the \(p\)-chain \(Y\) really changes state. The discrete Jump–Hold Proposition and geometric compounding (with \(b_k = a_{i_{k-1}}/\lambda\)) then give the result for \(X'\).
  • From distributions to paths. Equality of finite-dimensional distributions does not imply pathwise equality. For right-continuous processes, however, jump times and jump states are measurable functions of the values at rational times (Lemma 90.1, Lemma 90.2). Their joint law is therefore determined by the finite-dimensional distributions, which transfers the result from \(X'\) to any right-continuous version \(X\). Without right-continuity, the conclusion can fail for a modification (Remark 90.2).
  • Limits of the method. Unbounded rates destroy operator-norm continuity, and on \(\ell_\infty(S)\) even strong continuity (Lotz’s theorem), so uniformization is unavailable. The jump–hold structure persists up to a possibly finite explosion time, which is the subject of later lectures.
