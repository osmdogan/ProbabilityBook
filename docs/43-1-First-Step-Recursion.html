92  Lecture 43.1 First Step Recursion

92.1 Hitting Times and First-Step Analysis

Throughout this lecture we work in the canonical setting of Lecture 42.2. Thus \(\mathbb{N}_0 = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}\), \(\overline{\mathbb{N}}_0 := \mathbb{N}_0 \cup \{\infty\}\), \((S, \mathcal{B})\) is a (non-empty) measurable state space, and \(q: S \times \mathcal{B} \to [0, 1]\) is a Markov kernel. Moreover, \(\Omega = S^{\mathbb{N}_0}\), \(\mathcal{F} = \mathcal{B}^{\otimes \mathbb{N}_0}\), \(X_n(\omega) = \omega_n\) is the \(n\)-th coordinate map, and \(\mathcal{F}_n = \sigma(X_0, \dots, X_n)\). For each \(x \in S\), \(\mathbb{P}^x\) is the unique probability measure on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) under which \(X = (X_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}_0}\) is a time-homogeneous Markov chain with respect to \((\mathcal{F}_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}_0}\) with one-step transition kernel \(q\) and \(\mathbb{P}^x(X_0 = x) = 1\). We write \(\mathbb{E}^x\) for the corresponding expectation.

As in Lecture 42.2, \(\mathcal{B}_b(S)\) denotes the space of bounded \(\mathcal{B}\)-measurable functions \(f: S \to \mathbb{R}\), and \(\mathcal{B}_+(S)\) denotes the space of \(\mathcal{B}/\mathcal{B}([0, \infty])\)-measurable functions \(f: S \to [0, \infty]\); the analogous notation is used on the path space \(S^{\mathbb{N}_0}\). The transition operator \[ (Qf)(x) = \int_S q(x, dy)\, f(y), \qquad x \in S, \] maps \(\mathcal{B}_b(S)\) into \(\mathcal{B}_b(S)\) and \(\mathcal{B}_+(S)\) into \(\mathcal{B}_+(S)\). In computations with \([0, \infty]\)-valued quantities we use the convention \(0 \cdot \infty = 0\).

We use the hitting-time notation of Definition 91.1. For \(B \in \mathcal{B}\) and a path \(\underline{x} = (x_0, x_1, \dots) \in S^{\mathbb{N}_0}\), \[ T_B(\underline{x}) = \inf\{ n \ge 0 : x_n \in B \} \in \overline{\mathbb{N}}_0 \qquad (\inf \emptyset = \infty) \] is the first hitting time of \(B\), and \(L_B(\underline{x}) = x_{T_B(\underline{x})}\) is the hitting location (with \(L_B(\underline{x}) = \Delta\) if \(T_B(\underline{x}) = \infty\)). We use the same symbol \(T_B\) for the random variable \(T_B(X)\), and we write \(X_{T_B} := L_B(X)\) on \(\{T_B < \infty\}\). By Remark 91.1, \(T_B\) is an \((\mathcal{F}_n)\)-stopping time, and \(T_B\) and \(L_B\) are measurable functions on path space. Since the infimum includes \(n = 0\), we have \(T_B = 0\) \(\mathbb{P}^x\)-a.s. whenever \(x \in B\).

We shall use the following results from Lecture 42.2.

  • First-step analysis (Theorem 91.1). For \(F \in \mathcal{B}_b(S^{\mathbb{N}_0})\) or \(F \in \mathcal{B}_+(S^{\mathbb{N}_0})\) and \(x \in S\), the map \(y \mapsto \mathbb{E}^y[F(x, X)]\) is \(\mathcal{B}\)-measurable and \[ \mathbb{E}^x[F(X)] = \int_S q(x, dy)\, \mathbb{E}^y[F(x, X)], \qquad (x, X) := (x, X_0, X_1, X_2, \dots). \] Its iterated form is the \(n\)-step formula Equation 91.8.
  • Shift identities (Proposition 91.1). If \(x \in B^c\), then for every path \(\underline{y} \in S^{\mathbb{N}_0}\), \[ T_B(x, \underline{y}) = 1 + T_B(\underline{y}) \qquad \text{and} \qquad L_B(x, \underline{y}) = L_B(\underline{y}). \] (Apply Proposition 91.1 to the path \((x, \underline{y})\), whose initial coordinate lies in \(B^c\) and whose shift is \(\underline{y}\).)

We also record a measurability statement that is used repeatedly.

Lemma 92.1 (Measurability in the Starting Point) Let \(H \in \mathcal{B}_b(S^{\mathbb{N}_0})\) or \(H \in \mathcal{B}_+(S^{\mathbb{N}_0})\). Then the map \(y \mapsto \mathbb{E}^y[H(X)]\) is \(\mathcal{B}\)-measurable; it is bounded by \(\sup |H|\) in the first case and \([0, \infty]\)-valued in the second.

Proof (Proof of Lemma 92.1). The shift \(\theta(x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots) := (x_1, x_2, \dots)\) is \(\mathcal{B}^{\otimes \mathbb{N}_0}/\mathcal{B}^{\otimes \mathbb{N}_0}\)-measurable, because each of its coordinates is a coordinate projection. Hence \(F := H \circ \theta\) belongs to the same class as \(H\), and \(F(x, X) = H(X)\) for every \(x \in S\). Fixing any \(x \in S\), the measurability clause of Theorem 91.1 shows that \(y \mapsto \mathbb{E}^y[F(x, X)] = \mathbb{E}^y[H(X)]\) is \(\mathcal{B}\)-measurable. The bounds are immediate.

We are interested in characterizing the distribution of \(T_B\), for instance by computing the hitting probabilities \(\mathbb{P}^x(T_B < \infty)\) or the expected hitting times \(\mathbb{E}^x[T_B]\) as functions of the starting state \(x \in S\). To analyze these quantities systematically, it is fruitful to embed them into two broader families of functionals, which have natural interpretations in terms of stochastic games:

  1. Terminal-payoff game: Given a payoff function \(h \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\) or \(h \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\), consider \[ u(x) := \mathbb{E}^x\big[h(X_{T_B})\mathbf{1}_{\{T_B < \infty\}}\big], \qquad x \in S, \] where the integrand is understood to be \(h(X_{T_B})\) on \(\{T_B < \infty\}\) and \(0\) on \(\{T_B = \infty\}\).

    Interpretation: Start the chain at state \(x\) and let it evolve until the first time \(T_B\) it enters the target set \(B\). At the entrance state \(y = X_{T_B} \in B\), you collect a terminal reward of \(\$h(y)\) (and \(\$0\) if the chain never hits \(B\)). Only the values of \(h\) on \(B\) matter. In the special case \(h \equiv 1\), this reduces to the hitting probability: \[ u(x) = \mathbb{E}^x[\mathbf{1}_{\{T_B < \infty\}}] = \mathbb{P}^x(T_B < \infty). \]

  2. Running-cost (or running-reward) game: Given a running-reward function \(g \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\), consider \[ v(x) := \mathbb{E}^x\left[\sum_{0 \le n < T_B} g(X_n)\right], \qquad x \in S. \] Interpretation: Start at \(x\) and follow the random trajectory \(X_0, X_1, X_2, \dots\) until the first time \(T_B\) that the chain enters \(B\) (if \(T_B = 0\), the empty sum is \(0\)). At each step \(n < T_B\) before hitting \(B\), you collect \(\$g(X_n)\). What is your expected total reward? Since \(X_n \in B^c\) for every \(n < T_B\), only the values of \(g\) on \(B^c\) matter. In particular, for \(g = \mathbf{1}_{B^c}\) (or \(g \equiv 1\)), each step prior to hitting \(B\) contributes \(1\), so this reduces to the expected hitting time: \[ v(x) = \mathbb{E}^x\left[\sum_{0 \le n < T_B} 1\right] = \mathbb{E}^x[T_B]. \]

92.2 Sub-Probability Kernels

To express the recursive equations satisfied by \(u\) and \(v\) cleanly, we split the transition kernel \(q\) according to whether the next state lies in a given set \(B \in \mathcal{B}\) or not.

For \(C \in \mathcal{B}\), recall that the trace \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(C\) is \[ \mathcal{B}|_C := \{ E \in \mathcal{B} : E \subseteq C \} = \{ F \cap C : F \in \mathcal{B} \}, \] which is a \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(C\) (the two descriptions agree because \(C \in \mathcal{B}\)).

Definition 92.1 (Restricted Sub-Probability Kernel and Operator) Let \(q: S \times \mathcal{B} \to [0, 1]\) be a Markov kernel and let \(B \in \mathcal{B}\).

  1. The restriction of \(q\) to \(B\) is the map \(q_B: S \times \mathcal{B} \to [0, 1]\) defined by \[ q_B(x, A) := q(x, A \cap B), \qquad x \in S, \; A \in \mathcal{B}. \] For each \(x \in S\), \(q_B(x, \cdot)\) is a measure on \((S, \mathcal{B})\) concentrated on \(B\), and for each \(A \in \mathcal{B}\) the map \(x \mapsto q_B(x, A) = q(x, A \cap B)\) is \(\mathcal{B}\)-measurable. Thus \(q_B\) is a kernel on \((S, \mathcal{B})\). Note that the starting point \(x\) ranges over all of \(S\); in particular, for \(x \in B^c\), \(q_B(x, \cdot)\) describes one-step transitions from outside \(B\) into \(B\).
  2. The associated operator \(Q_B\) is defined, for \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\) or \(f \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\), by \[ \begin{aligned} (Q_B f)(x) &:= \int_S q_B(x, dy)\, f(y) \\ &= \int_B q(x, dy)\, f(y) \\ &= Q(f \mathbf{1}_B)(x), \qquad x \in S. \end{aligned} \] It maps \(\mathcal{B}_b(S)\) into \(\mathcal{B}_b(S)\) and \(\mathcal{B}_+(S)\) into \(\mathcal{B}_+(S)\), and \(Q_B f\) depends only on the restriction \(f|_B\).

Remark 92.1 (Properties of \(q_B\) and \(Q_B\)). For every \(x \in S\), the total mass of \(q_B(x, \cdot)\) is \[ q_B(x, S) = q(x, B) \le 1, \] which is not necessarily equal to \(1\) (in contrast to \(q(x, S) = 1\)). Consequently, \(q_B\) is a sub-probability kernel, and \(Q_B\) is a sub-Markovian operator:

  • \(Q_B\) is linear on \(\mathcal{B}_b(S)\), additive and positively homogeneous on \(\mathcal{B}_+(S)\), positive (\(f \ge 0 \Rightarrow Q_B f \ge 0\)), and therefore monotone; moreover, if \(0 \le f_n \uparrow f\), then \(Q_B f_n \uparrow Q_B f\) by the monotone convergence theorem.
  • \((Q_B \mathbf{1})(x) = Q(\mathbf{1}_B)(x) = q(x, B) \le 1\), rather than \(1\).
  • Because \(\{B, B^c\}\) is a measurable partition of \(S\), the kernel \(q\) and operator \(Q\) decompose additively as \[ q = q_B + q_{B^c} \qquad \text{and} \qquad Q = Q_B + Q_{B^c}, \] meaning that for any \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\) or \(f \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\) and any \(x \in S\), \[ \begin{aligned} (Qf)(x) &= (Q_B f)(x) + (Q_{B^c} f)(x) \\ &= \int_B q(x, dy)\, f(y) + \int_{B^c} q(x, dy)\, f(y), \end{aligned} \] with the sum taken in \([0, \infty]\) when \(f \ge 0\).
  • Iterates of \(Q_{B^c}\) can be expressed through the chain. For \(f \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\) or \(f \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\), \(k \in \mathbb{N}_0\), and \(x \in S\), \[ (Q_{B^c}^k f)(x) = \mathbb{E}^x\big[\mathbf{1}_{B^c}(X_1) \cdots \mathbf{1}_{B^c}(X_k)\, f(X_k)\big]. \tag{92.1}\] Indeed, unwinding \(Q_{B^c} f = Q(\mathbf{1}_{B^c} f)\) shows that the left side is the iterated integral \(\int_S q(x, dx_1) \cdots \int_S q(x_{k-1}, dx_k)\, \mathbf{1}_{B^c}(x_1) \cdots \mathbf{1}_{B^c}(x_k) f(x_k)\), which equals the right side by Equation 91.8 applied to a functional of \((X_0, \dots, X_k)\) (using \(X_0 = x_k\) \(\mathbb{P}^{x_k}\)-a.s.).

When working only with functions defined on \(B^c\), we also regard \(Q_{B^c}\) as an operator on functions \(f: B^c \to \mathbb{R}\) that are bounded and \(\mathcal{B}|_{B^c}\)-measurable, via \((Q_{B^c} f)(x) = \int_{B^c} q(x, dy)\, f(y)\) for \(x \in B^c\). This is the restriction of the operator of Definition 92.1 to functions supported on \(B^c\), evaluated at points of \(B^c\).

92.3 First-Step Equations for Hitting Distributions and Costs

We now derive the fundamental recursive equations satisfied by the expected terminal payoff \(u\) and the expected cumulative running cost \(v\).

Proposition 92.1 (Equation for Terminal Payoff at the Hitting Time) Let \(B \in \mathcal{B}\), and let \(h \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\) or \(h \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\). Define \[ u(x) := \mathbb{E}^x\big[h(X_{T_B}) \mathbf{1}_{\{T_B < \infty\}}\big], \qquad x \in S. \] Then:

  1. \(u \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\) with \(\sup|u| \le \sup|h|\) (when \(h \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\)), and \(u \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\) (when \(h \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\)).
  2. On \(B\), \(u\) agrees with \(h\): \[ u(x) = h(x) \qquad \text{for all } x \in B. \]
  3. On \(B^c\), \(u\) satisfies \(u = Qu\), or equivalently, \[ u = Q_B h + Q_{B^c} u \qquad \text{on } B^c, \] that is, for all \(x \in B^c\), \[ u(x) = \int_B q(x, dy)\, h(y) + \int_{B^c} q(x, dy)\, u(y). \]

Proof (Proof of Proposition 92.1). Define the path functional \(F: S^{\mathbb{N}_0} \to \mathbb{R}\) (resp. \([0, \infty]\)) by \[ F(\underline{x}) := \sum_{k \in \mathbb{N}_0} \mathbf{1}_{\{T_B(\underline{x}) = k\}}\, h(x_k), \qquad \underline{x} = (x_0, x_1, \dots) \in S^{\mathbb{N}_0}. \] For each path at most one term of the sum is non-zero, so \(F(\underline{x}) = h(L_B(\underline{x}))\) if \(T_B(\underline{x}) < \infty\) and \(F(\underline{x}) = 0\) otherwise. In particular \(F(X) = h(X_{T_B}) \mathbf{1}_{\{T_B < \infty\}}\) and \(u(x) = \mathbb{E}^x[F(X)]\). Each set \(\{T_B = k\}\) belongs to \(\mathcal{B}^{\otimes \mathbb{N}_0}\) by Remark 91.1, and \(\underline{x} \mapsto h(x_k)\) is measurable, so \(F\) is measurable. Moreover, \(\sup|F| \le \sup|h|\) if \(h\) is bounded, and \(F \ge 0\) if \(h \ge 0\).

(1) By Lemma 92.1 applied to \(H = F\), \(u\) is \(\mathcal{B}\)-measurable, with \(\sup|u| \le \sup|h|\) if \(h \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\) and values in \([0, \infty]\) if \(h \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\).

(2) Let \(x \in B\). Under \(\mathbb{P}^x\) we have \(X_0 = x \in B\) almost surely, so \(T_B = 0\) and \(F(X) = h(X_0) = h(x)\) almost surely. Hence \(u(x) = h(x)\).

(3) Fix \(x \in B^c\) and a path \(\underline{y} = (y_0, y_1, \dots)\). By the shift identities, \(T_B(x, \underline{y}) = 1 + T_B(\underline{y})\). In particular \(T_B(x, \underline{y}) \ne 0\), and for \(k \in \mathbb{N}_0\) we have \(T_B(x, \underline{y}) = k + 1\) if and only if \(T_B(\underline{y}) = k\), while the \((k+1)\)-st coordinate of \((x, \underline{y})\) is \(y_k\). Therefore \[ F(x, \underline{y}) = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{N}_0} \mathbf{1}_{\{T_B(\underline{y}) = k\}}\, h(y_k) = F(\underline{y}) \qquad \text{for all } \underline{y} \in S^{\mathbb{N}_0}. \] Applying Theorem 91.1 at \(x \in B^c\) yields \[ \begin{aligned} u(x) &= \mathbb{E}^x[F(X)] = \int_S q(x, dy) \, \mathbb{E}^y[F(x, X)] \\ &= \int_S q(x, dy) \, \mathbb{E}^y[F(X)] = \int_S q(x, dy)\, u(y) = (Qu)(x). \end{aligned} \] Finally, decomposing \(Q = Q_B + Q_{B^c}\) (Remark 92.1) and using \(u = h\) on \(B\) from (2), \[ \begin{aligned} u(x) &= (Qu)(x) = \int_B q(x, dy)\, h(y) + \int_{B^c} q(x, dy)\, u(y) \\ &= (Q_B h)(x) + (Q_{B^c} u)(x). \end{aligned} \]

Corollary 92.1 (Hitting Probabilities) Taking \(h \equiv 1\) in Proposition 92.1, the hitting probability \(u(x) := \mathbb{P}^x(T_B < \infty)\) is a \([0, 1]\)-valued \(\mathcal{B}\)-measurable function satisfying \[ \begin{cases} u(x) = 1 & \text{for } x \in B, \\ u(x) = (Q_{B^c} u)(x) + (Q_B \mathbf{1})(x) = (Q_{B^c} u)(x) + q(x, B) & \text{for } x \in B^c. \end{cases} \]

We next establish the analogous first-step equation for cumulative running rewards prior to hitting \(B\).

Proposition 92.2 (Equation for Cumulative Running Cost) Let \(B \in \mathcal{B}\) and \(g \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\). Define \[ v(x) := \mathbb{E}^x\left[\sum_{0 \le n < T_B} g(X_n)\right], \qquad x \in S. \] Then:

  1. \(v \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\).
  2. On \(B\), \(v\) vanishes identically: \[ v(x) = 0 \qquad \text{for all } x \in B. \]
  3. On \(B^c\), \(v\) satisfies (as an identity in \([0, \infty]\)) \[ v(x) = g(x) + (Qv)(x) = g(x) + (Q_{B^c} v)(x) \qquad \text{for all } x \in B^c. \]

Proof (Proof of Proposition 92.2). Define the path functional \(G: S^{\mathbb{N}_0} \to [0, \infty]\) by \[ G(\underline{x}) := \sum_{0 \le n < T_B(\underline{x})} g(x_n) = \sum_{n \in \mathbb{N}_0} \mathbf{1}_{\{T_B(\underline{x}) > n\}}\, g(x_n). \] Each summand is a product of non-negative measurable functions on path space (by Remark 91.1, \(\{T_B > n\} \in \mathcal{B}^{\otimes \mathbb{N}_0}\)), so \(G \in \mathcal{B}_+(S^{\mathbb{N}_0})\), and \(v(x) = \mathbb{E}^x[G(X)]\).

(1) By Lemma 92.1 applied to \(H = G\), \(v \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\).

(2) If \(x \in B\), then \(X_0 = x \in B\) \(\mathbb{P}^x\)-a.s., so \(T_B = 0\) and the sum defining \(G(X)\) is empty, i.e. \(G(X) = 0\) \(\mathbb{P}^x\)-a.s. Hence \(v(x) = 0\).

(3) Fix \(x \in B^c\) and a path \(\underline{y} = (y_0, y_1, \dots)\). By the shift identities, \(T_B(x, \underline{y}) = 1 + T_B(\underline{y}) \ge 1\). The \(0\)-th coordinate of \((x, \underline{y})\) is \(x\) and its \(n\)-th coordinate is \(y_{n-1}\) for \(n \ge 1\), so, separating the term \(n = 0\) and substituting \(m = n - 1\), \[ \begin{aligned} G(x, \underline{y}) &= g(x) + \sum_{n \ge 1} \mathbf{1}_{\{1 + T_B(\underline{y}) > n\}}\, g(y_{n-1}) \\ &= g(x) + \sum_{m \in \mathbb{N}_0} \mathbf{1}_{\{T_B(\underline{y}) > m\}}\, g(y_m) \\ &= g(x) + G(\underline{y}). \end{aligned} \] Applying Theorem 91.1 at \(x\), together with additivity of integrals of \([0, \infty]\)-valued functions and \(q(x, S) = 1\), we obtain \[ \begin{aligned} v(x) &= \mathbb{E}^x[G(X)] = \int_S q(x, dy) \, \mathbb{E}^y[G(x, X)] \\ &= \int_S q(x, dy) \, \big( g(x) + \mathbb{E}^y[G(X)] \big) \\ &= g(x)\, q(x, S) + \int_S q(x, dy)\, v(y) = g(x) + (Qv)(x). \end{aligned} \] Since \(v = 0\) on \(B\) by (2), we have \(v = v \mathbf{1}_{B^c}\), hence \((Qv)(x) = Q(v\mathbf{1}_{B^c})(x) = (Q_{B^c} v)(x)\).

Corollary 92.2 (Expected Hitting Time) Taking \(g = \mathbf{1}_{B^c}\) in Proposition 92.2, for which \(\sum_{0 \le n < T_B} g(X_n) = T_B\), the expected hitting time \(v(x) := \mathbb{E}^x[T_B] \in [0, \infty]\) satisfies \[ \begin{cases} v(x) = 0 & \text{for } x \in B, \\ v(x) = (Q_{B^c} v)(x) + 1 = \displaystyle\int_{B^c} q(x, dy) \, \mathbb{E}^y[T_B] + 1 & \text{for } x \in B^c, \end{cases} \] as identities in \([0, \infty]\).

92.4 Solving the Recursive Equations and Minimality

Proposition 92.1 and Proposition 92.2 reduce the computation of \(u\) and \(v\) to solving linear operator equations on \(B^c\) subject to prescribed boundary values on \(B\): \[ \begin{aligned} u &= Q_{B^c} u + Q_B h \quad \text{on } B^c, \qquad u = h \quad \text{on } B, \\ v &= Q_{B^c} v + g \quad \text{on } B^c, \qquad v = 0 \quad \text{on } B. \end{aligned} \] Heuristically, rearranging these equations on \(B^c\) yields \[ (I - Q_{B^c}) u = Q_B h \implies u \text{ ``}=\text{'' } (I - Q_{B^c})^{-1} Q_B h \] and \[ (I - Q_{B^c}) v = g \implies v \text{ ``}=\text{'' } (I - Q_{B^c})^{-1} g, \] where \(I\) denotes the identity operator on functions on \(B^c\), \(Q_{B^c}\) is regarded as an operator on functions on \(B^c\), and \((I - Q_{B^c})^{-1}\) is formally given by the Neumann series \(\sum_{n=0}^\infty Q_{B^c}^n\). These manipulations are only formal: \(u\) and \(v\) may take the value \(+\infty\), in which case \((I - Q_{B^c})u\) involves \(\infty - \infty\), and \(I - Q_{B^c}\) need not be invertible. For non-negative data, however, the Neumann series does make sense and gives exactly \(u\) and \(v\) on \(B^c\) (Theorem 92.1, Remark 92.3).

Remark 92.2 (Invertibility and Non-Uniqueness). Suppose \(h \in \mathcal{B}_b(S)\) (resp. \(g \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\) is bounded), and regard \(Q_{B^c}\) as a bounded operator on the Banach space of bounded \(\mathcal{B}|_{B^c}\)-measurable functions on \(B^c\) with the supremum norm; note \(\|Q_{B^c}^n\| = \sup_{x \in B^c} (Q_{B^c}^n \mathbf{1})(x) \le 1\). If \(I - Q_{B^c}\) is invertible on this space, then the corresponding system has exactly one bounded solution: its values on \(B\) are prescribed, and on \(B^c\) any bounded solution \(w\) satisfies \((I - Q_{B^c})(w|_{B^c}) = (Q_B h)|_{B^c}\) (resp. \(= g|_{B^c}\)).

This holds, for example, when \(B^c\) is finite and \(\mathbb{P}^x(T_B < \infty) = 1\) for all \(x \in B^c\). Indeed, for \(x \in B^c\), Equation 92.1 gives \((Q_{B^c}^n \mathbf{1})(x) = \mathbb{P}^x(X_1, \dots, X_n \in B^c) = \mathbb{P}^x(T_B > n) \to 0\) as \(n \to \infty\). Since \(B^c\) is finite, \(\|Q_{B^c}^N\| = \max_{x \in B^c} \mathbb{P}^x(T_B > N) < 1\) for some \(N\), so \(\sum_{n \ge 0} \|Q_{B^c}^n\| < \infty\) and the Neumann series \(\sum_{n \ge 0} Q_{B^c}^n\) converges in operator norm to \((I - Q_{B^c})^{-1}\).

Even then, uniqueness may fail among non-negative solutions that are allowed to take the value \(+\infty\). For example, let \(S = \{0, 1\}\), \(B = \{0\}\), \(q(0, \{0\}) = 1\), and \(q(1, \{0\}) = q(1, \{1\}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\). With \(g = \mathbf{1}_{B^c}\), the system of Corollary 92.2 reads \(w(0) = 0\), \(w(1) = \tfrac{1}{2} w(1) + 1\), which has the two solutions \(w(1) = 2 = \mathbb{E}^1[T_B]\) and \(w(1) = \infty\) in \([0, \infty]\).

In general, \(I - Q_{B^c}\) need not be invertible, and the recursive equations may admit several bounded solutions. For instance, in Corollary 92.1 (\(h \equiv 1\)), the constant function \(\tilde{u} \equiv 1\) on \(S\) always satisfies \[ \begin{aligned} (Q_{B^c} \mathbf{1})(x) + (Q_B \mathbf{1})(x)&= q(x, B^c) + q(x, B) \\ &= q(x, S) = 1 = \tilde{u}(x) \; \text{for all } x \in B^c. \end{aligned} \] But the true hitting probability \(u(x) = \mathbb{P}^x(T_B < \infty)\) may be strictly less than \(1\). For example, for the simple symmetric random walk on \(\mathbb{Z}^3\) with \(B = \{0\}\), transience gives \(\mathbb{P}^x(T_B < \infty) < 1\) for every \(x \ne 0\). In that case \(\tilde{u} - u\) is a non-zero bounded function on \(B^c\) with \((I - Q_{B^c})(\tilde{u} - u) = 0\), so \(I - Q_{B^c}\) is not injective. See Driver, Probability Tools with Examples, Corollary 22.58.

When uniqueness fails, we need a selection principle to single out the true probabilistic quantities \(u\) and \(v\) among all solutions to the first-step equations. A function \(w \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\) is called the least (or minimal) non-negative solution of a system if it solves the system and \(w \le \tilde{w}\) pointwise for every non-negative measurable solution \(\tilde{w}\); such a function is necessarily unique.

Theorem 92.1 (Characterization via Minimal Non-Negative Solutions) Let \(B \in \mathcal{B}\), \(h \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\), and \(g \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\).

  1. The function \(u(x) = \mathbb{E}^x[h(X_{T_B}) \mathbf{1}_{\{T_B < \infty\}}]\) is the least non-negative measurable solution of \[ \begin{cases} w(x) = h(x) & \text{for } x \in B, \\ w(x) = (Q_{B^c} w)(x) + (Q_B h)(x) & \text{for } x \in B^c. \end{cases} \] More precisely, \(u\) solves this system, and if \(\tilde{u} \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\) is any supersolution, i.e. \[ \tilde{u} \ge h \ \text{ on } B \qquad \text{and} \qquad \tilde{u} \ge Q_{B^c}\tilde{u} + Q_B h \ \text{ on } B^c, \] then \(u(x) \le \tilde{u}(x)\) for all \(x \in S\).

  2. The function \(v(x) = \mathbb{E}^x\big[\sum_{0 \le n < T_B} g(X_n)\big]\) is the least non-negative measurable solution of \[ \begin{cases} w(x) = 0 & \text{for } x \in B, \\ w(x) = (Q_{B^c} w)(x) + g(x) & \text{for } x \in B^c. \end{cases} \] More precisely, \(v\) solves this system, and if \(\tilde{v} \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\) satisfies \[ \tilde{v} \ge Q_{B^c}\tilde{v} + g \ \text{ on } B^c, \] then \(v(x) \le \tilde{v}(x)\) for all \(x \in S\).

(Compare Driver, Probability Tools with Examples, Theorem 22.81.)

Proof (Proof of Theorem 92.1). That \(u\) and \(v\) solve their systems is the content of Proposition 92.1 and Proposition 92.2. All functions below are \([0, \infty]\)-valued, and we use repeatedly that \(Q_{B^c}\) is additive and monotone on \(\mathcal{B}_+(S)\) and that \(Q_{B^c} f = Q_{B^c}(f \mathbf{1}_{B^c})\) depends only on \(f|_{B^c}\) (Remark 92.1).

Step 1: probabilistic form of the iterates. Let \(x \in B^c\) and \(k \in \mathbb{N}_0\). Under \(\mathbb{P}^x\) we have \(X_0 = x \in B^c\) a.s., hence \(\mathbb{P}^x\)-a.s. \[ \begin{aligned} &\{X_1, \dots, X_k \in B^c\} = \{T_B > k\}, \\ &\{X_1, \dots, X_k \in B^c,\ X_{k+1} \in B\} = \{T_B = k + 1\}. \end{aligned} \] By Equation 92.1 with \(f = g\), \[ (Q_{B^c}^k g)(x) = \mathbb{E}^x\big[g(X_k)\, \mathbf{1}_{\{T_B > k\}}\big]. \] Applying Equation 92.1 with \(f = Q_B h = Q(h \mathbf{1}_B)\), and then Equation 91.8 to express \((Q(h\mathbf{1}_B))(X_k)\) as an expectation over one further step, gives \[ \begin{aligned} (Q_{B^c}^k Q_B h)(x) &= \mathbb{E}^x\big[\mathbf{1}_{B^c}(X_1) \cdots \mathbf{1}_{B^c}(X_k)\, \mathbf{1}_B(X_{k+1})\, h(X_{k+1})\big] \\ &= \mathbb{E}^x\big[h(X_{T_B})\, \mathbf{1}_{\{T_B = k+1\}}\big]. \end{aligned} \]

Step 2: iterating the supersolution inequality (part 1). Let \(\tilde{u}\) be a supersolution. We claim that for every \(n \in \mathbb{N}_0\), \[ \tilde{u} \ \ge\ \sum_{k=0}^{n} Q_{B^c}^k Q_B h + Q_{B^c}^{n+1}\tilde{u} \qquad \text{on } B^c. \tag{92.2}\] For \(n = 0\) this is the hypothesis. If it holds for \(n\), then, since \(Q_{B^c}^{n+1}\) only sees values on \(B^c\), where \(\tilde{u} \ge Q_{B^c}\tilde{u} + Q_B h\), monotonicity and additivity give \[ \begin{aligned} Q_{B^c}^{n+1}\tilde{u} &= Q_{B^c}^{n+1}(\tilde{u}\mathbf{1}_{B^c}) \ge Q_{B^c}^{n+1}\big((Q_{B^c}\tilde{u} + Q_B h)\mathbf{1}_{B^c}\big) \\ &= Q_{B^c}^{n+2}\tilde{u} + Q_{B^c}^{n+1} Q_B h, \end{aligned} \] which yields Equation 92.2 for \(n + 1\). Dropping the non-negative term \(Q_{B^c}^{n+1}\tilde{u}\) and using Step 1, for \(x \in B^c\), \[ \tilde{u}(x) \ge \sum_{k=0}^{n} \mathbb{E}^x\big[h(X_{T_B})\, \mathbf{1}_{\{T_B = k+1\}}\big] = \mathbb{E}^x\big[h(X_{T_B})\, \mathbf{1}_{\{1 \le T_B \le n+1\}}\big]. \] Letting \(n \to \infty\) and using the monotone convergence theorem together with \(T_B \ge 1\) \(\mathbb{P}^x\)-a.s. (since \(x \in B^c\)), we get \(\tilde{u}(x) \ge \mathbb{E}^x[h(X_{T_B}) \mathbf{1}_{\{T_B < \infty\}}] = u(x)\). On \(B\) we have \(\tilde{u} \ge h = u\) by Proposition 92.1. Hence \(u \le \tilde{u}\) on \(S\).

Step 3: part 2. Let \(\tilde{v} \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\) satisfy \(\tilde{v} \ge Q_{B^c}\tilde{v} + g\) on \(B^c\). The same induction, with \(Q_B h\) replaced by \(g\), gives for all \(n \in \mathbb{N}_0\) \[ \tilde{v} \ge \sum_{k=0}^{n} Q_{B^c}^k g + Q_{B^c}^{n+1}\tilde{v} \ge \sum_{k=0}^{n} Q_{B^c}^k g \qquad \text{on } B^c. \] By Step 1, for \(x \in B^c\), the right side equals \(\mathbb{E}^x\big[\sum_{k=0}^{n} g(X_k)\, \mathbf{1}_{\{T_B > k\}}\big]\), which increases to \(\mathbb{E}^x\big[\sum_{k \in \mathbb{N}_0} g(X_k)\, \mathbf{1}_{\{T_B > k\}}\big] = v(x)\) by the monotone convergence theorem. On \(B\) we have \(\tilde{v} \ge 0 = v\). Hence \(v \le \tilde{v}\) on \(S\).

Finally, any non-negative measurable solution of either system is in particular a supersolution, so \(u\) and \(v\) are the least non-negative measurable solutions.

Remark 92.3 (The Neumann Series Made Rigorous). Summing the identities of Step 1 of the proof over \(k \in \mathbb{N}_0\) (Tonelli’s theorem for series of non-negative terms) shows that, for \(h, g \in \mathcal{B}_+(S)\) and every \(x \in B^c\), \[ u(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (Q_{B^c}^k Q_B h)(x), \qquad v(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (Q_{B^c}^k g)(x), \] as identities in \([0, \infty]\). Thus the formal expressions \((I - Q_{B^c})^{-1} Q_B h\) and \((I - Q_{B^c})^{-1} g\) are meaningful for non-negative data when \((I - Q_{B^c})^{-1}\) is interpreted as the series \(\sum_{k \ge 0} Q_{B^c}^k\), and they select precisely the least non-negative solutions. In particular, \(\mathbb{P}^x(T_B < \infty)\) and \(\mathbb{E}^x[T_B]\) are the least non-negative solutions of the systems in Corollary 92.1 and Corollary 92.2, respectively.

92.5 Takeaways

  • Two master functionals. Hitting questions for a set \(B \in \mathcal{B}\) are embedded into the terminal payoff \(u(x) = \mathbb{E}^x[h(X_{T_B})\mathbf{1}_{\{T_B < \infty\}}]\) and the running cost \(v(x) = \mathbb{E}^x[\sum_{0 \le n < T_B} g(X_n)]\). The choices \(h \equiv 1\) and \(g = \mathbf{1}_{B^c}\) give the hitting probability \(\mathbb{P}^x(T_B < \infty)\) and the expected hitting time \(\mathbb{E}^x[T_B]\).
  • Splitting the kernel. The sub-probability kernels \(q_B(x, \cdot) = q(x, \cdot \cap B)\) and \(q_{B^c}(x, \cdot) = q(x, \cdot \cap B^c)\), defined for every starting point \(x \in S\), decompose \(q = q_B + q_{B^c}\) and \(Q = Q_B + Q_{B^c}\) (Definition 92.1, Remark 92.1). For \(x \in B^c\), \(Q_B\) records a jump from outside \(B\) into \(B\), and the iterates \(Q_{B^c}^k\) describe the chain while it avoids \(B\) (Equation 92.1).
  • First-step equations. Combining first-step analysis (Theorem 91.1) with the shift identities \(T_B(x, \underline{y}) = 1 + T_B(\underline{y})\) and \(L_B(x, \underline{y}) = L_B(\underline{y})\) for \(x \in B^c\) gives the boundary value problems \(u = h\) on \(B\), \(u = Q_{B^c} u + Q_B h\) on \(B^c\) (Proposition 92.1), and \(v = 0\) on \(B\), \(v = Q_{B^c} v + g\) on \(B^c\) (Proposition 92.2). Measurability of \(u\) and \(v\) comes from Lemma 92.1.
  • Non-uniqueness. These systems need not determine \(u\) and \(v\). Among bounded solutions, uniqueness holds when \(I - Q_{B^c}\) is invertible, e.g. when \(B^c\) is finite and \(B\) is reached almost surely from every \(x \in B^c\). In general it fails: \(\tilde{u} \equiv 1\) always solves the hitting-probability system, even for a transient chain, and \(w \equiv \infty\) on \(B^c\) can solve the expected-hitting-time system alongside the true finite solution (Remark 92.2).
  • Minimality selects the truth. For non-negative data, \(u\) and \(v\) are the least non-negative measurable solutions, and they lie below every supersolution (Theorem 92.1). The proof iterates the supersolution inequality and identifies \(Q_{B^c}^k Q_B h\) and \(Q_{B^c}^k g\) with contributions from paths that avoid \(B\) for \(k\) steps.
  • Neumann series. For non-negative data, the formal inverse \((I - Q_{B^c})^{-1} = \sum_{k \ge 0} Q_{B^c}^k\) is meaningful in \([0, \infty]\) and gives exactly \(u = \sum_k Q_{B^c}^k Q_B h\) and \(v = \sum_k Q_{B^c}^k g\) on \(B^c\) (Remark 92.3).
