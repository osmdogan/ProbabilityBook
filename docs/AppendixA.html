Appendix A — Limsups, Liminfs and Extended Limits

A.1 Basic Definitions and Operational Conventions

Definition A.1 (Extended Real Numbers) The extended real number line is defined as the set \[ \overline{\mathbb{R}} := \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty, +\infty\}, \] formed by adjoining two distinct elements, \(-\infty\) and \(+\infty\), to the real line \(\mathbb{R}\).

We adopt the following arithmetic conventions:

  1. Addition and Subtraction: \[ \begin{aligned} &+\infty + a = +\infty \quad \text{and} \quad -\infty + a = -\infty \quad \text{for any } a \in \mathbb{R}, \\ &+\infty + (+\infty) = +\infty,\,-\infty + (-\infty) = -\infty. \end{aligned} \] The expressions \((+\infty) - (+\infty)\) and \((+\infty) + (-\infty)\) are undefined.

  2. Multiplication: \[ 0 \cdot (\pm\infty) = 0. \] For any \(a \in \mathbb{R}\) with \(a > 0\): \[ (\pm\infty) \cdot a = \pm\infty \quad \text{and} \quad (\pm\infty) \cdot (-a) = \mp\infty. \] Furthermore: \[ (\pm\infty) \cdot (\pm\infty) = +\infty \quad \text{and} \quad (\pm\infty) \cdot (\mp\infty) = -\infty. \]

  3. Limits involving infinity: A sequence \(\{a_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset \mathbb{R}\) is said to converge to \(+\infty\) (denoted \(\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = +\infty\)) if for every \(M \in \mathbb{R}\), there exists \(N \in \mathbb{N}\) such that \(a_n \ge M\) for all \(n \ge N\). Similarly, \(\{a_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) converges to \(-\infty\) (denoted \(\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = -\infty\)) if for every \(M \in \mathbb{R}\), there exists \(N \in \mathbb{N}\) such that \(a_n \le M\) for all \(n \ge N\).

Definition A.2 (Almost All (a.a.)) We write “\(a_n \le b_n\) for almost all \(n\)” (abbreviated \(a_n \le b_n\) a.a. \(n\)) if there exists an integer \(N < \infty\) such that \(a_n \le b_n\) for all \(n \ge N\).

A.2 Limit Laws in \(\overline{\mathbb{R}}\)

Lemma A.1 (Extended Limit Laws) Suppose \(\{a_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) and \(\{b_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) are convergent sequences in \(\overline{\mathbb{R}}\). Then:

  1. If \(a_n \le b_n\) for a.a. \(n\), then \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n \le \lim_{n \to \infty} b_n. \]
  2. If \(c \in \mathbb{R}\), then \(\{c a_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) converges in \(\overline{\mathbb{R}}\) and \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} (c a_n) = c \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n. \]
  3. The sum sequence \(\{a_n + b_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) converges in \(\overline{\mathbb{R}}\) and \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b_n) = \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n + \lim_{n \to \infty} b_n, \tag{A.1}\] provided the right-hand side is not of the undefined form \(\infty - \infty\).
  4. The product sequence \(\{a_n b_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) converges in \(\overline{\mathbb{R}}\) and \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} (a_n b_n) = \left(\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n\right) \cdot \left(\lim_{n \to \infty} b_n\right), \tag{A.2}\] provided the right-hand side is not of the form \(\pm\infty \cdot 0\) or \(0 \cdot (\pm\infty)\).

Proof (Proof of Lemma A.1). Proof of Items 1 and 2: Left as an exercise to the reader.

Proof of Item 3 (Equation A.1): Let \(a := \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n\) and \(b := \lim_{n \to \infty} b_n\).

  • Case 1 (\(a, b \in \mathbb{R}\)): Given any \(\epsilon > 0\), there exist \(N_1, N_2 \in \mathbb{N}\) such that \(|a_n - a| \le \epsilon\) for all \(n \ge N_1\) and \(|b_n - b| \le \epsilon\) for all \(n \ge N_2\). Setting \(N = \max(N_1, N_2)\), the triangle inequality gives \[ |(a_n + b_n) - (a + b)| = |(a_n - a) + (b_n - b)| \le |a_n - a| + |b_n - b| \le 2\epsilon \] for all \(n \ge N\). Since \(\epsilon > 0\) is arbitrary, \(\lim_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b_n) = a + b\).

  • Case 2 (\(b = +\infty\) and \(a > -\infty\)): For any \(M > 0\), choose \(N_1 \in \mathbb{N}\) such that \(b_n \ge M - a + 1\) for \(n \ge N_1\). Since \(a > -\infty\), choose \(N_2 \in \mathbb{N}\) such that \(a_n \ge a - 1\) for all \(n \ge N_2\) (if \(a = +\infty\), we choose \(a_n \ge 0\)). Thus, for \(n \ge N = \max(N_1, N_2)\), \[ a_n + b_n \ge (a - 1) + (M - a + 1) = M. \] Since \(M > 0\) is arbitrary, \(\lim_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b_n) = +\infty = a + b\). The cases where \(b = -\infty\) or \(a = \pm\infty\) follow symmetrically.

Proof of Item 4 (Equation A.2): We consider the extended case where \(a = \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n > 0\) and \(b = \lim_{n \to \infty} b_n = +\infty\).

Let \(\alpha := \min(a/2, 1) > 0\). Given any \(M < \infty\), there exists \(N \in \mathbb{N}\) such that \(a_n \ge \alpha\) and \(b_n \ge M / \alpha\) for all \(n \ge N\). Thus, for all \(n \ge N\), \[ a_n b_n \ge \alpha \cdot \frac{M}{\alpha} = M. \] Since \(M\) is arbitrary, \(\lim_{n \to \infty} (a_n b_n) = +\infty = a \cdot b\). The case \(a < 0\) is handled analogously.

Remark A.1. To see the necessity of excluding indeterminate forms:

  1. Sum Indeterminacy (\(\infty - \infty\)): Let \(a_n = n\) and \(b_n = -\alpha n\) for \(\alpha > 0\). Here \(\lim a_n = \infty\) and \(\lim b_n = -\infty\), but \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b_n) = \lim_{n \to \infty} (1 - \alpha) n = \begin{cases} +\infty & \text{if } \alpha < 1, \\ 0 & \text{if } \alpha = 1, \\ -\infty & \text{if } \alpha > 1. \end{cases} \]
  2. Product Indeterminacy (\(\infty \cdot 0\)): Let \(a_n = n\) and \(b_n = n^{-\alpha}\) with \(\alpha > 0\). Then \(\lim a_n = \infty\) and \(\lim b_n = 0\), while \(\lim (a_n b_n) = \lim n^{1-\alpha}\) can be \(0, 1,\) or \(+\infty\) depending on \(\alpha\).

A.3 Liminfs, Limsups, and Supremum Conventions

Definition A.3 For any subset \(\Lambda \subset \overline{\mathbb{R}}\), \(\sup \Lambda\) denotes the least upper bound and \(\inf \Lambda\) denotes the greatest lower bound. We enforce the following extended conventions:

  • \(\sup \Lambda = +\infty\) if \(+\infty \in \Lambda\) or if \(\Lambda\) is not bounded above in \(\mathbb{R}\).
  • \(\inf \Lambda = -\infty\) if \(-\infty \in \Lambda\) or if \(\Lambda\) is not bounded below in \(\mathbb{R}\).
  • \(\sup \emptyset = -\infty\) and \(\inf \emptyset = +\infty\).

Definition A.4 (Limit Superior and Limit Inferior) For a sequence of real numbers \(\{x_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset \mathbb{R}\), we define: \[ \liminf_{n \to \infty} x_n := \lim_{n \to \infty} \left( \inf \{x_k : k \ge n\} \right) = \sup_{n \ge 1} \inf_{k \ge n} x_k, \tag{A.3}\] \[ \limsup_{n \to \infty} x_n := \lim_{n \to \infty} \left( \sup \{x_k : k \ge n\} \right) = \inf_{n \ge 1} \sup_{k \ge n} x_k. \tag{A.4}\] We also write \(\varliminf_{n \to \infty} x_n\) for \(\liminf_{n \to \infty} x_n\), and \(\varlimsup_{n \to \infty} x_n\) for \(\limsup_{n \to \infty} x_n\).

Remark A.2. Let \(a_n := \inf \{x_k : k \ge n\}\) and \(b_n := \sup \{x_k : k \ge n\}\). Since the set \(\{x_k : k \ge n+1\} \subset \{x_k : k \ge n\}\), taking infima and suprema yields that \(\{a_n\}\) is non-decreasing (\(a_n \le a_{n+1}\)) and \(\{b_n\}\) is non-increasing (\(b_n \ge b_{n+1}\)). Monotone sequences in \(\overline{\mathbb{R}}\) always have well-defined limits in \(\overline{\mathbb{R}}\), which establishes why the limits in Equation A.3 and Equation A.4 always exist.

Proposition A.1 Let \(\{a_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) and \(\{b_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) be sequences in \(\mathbb{R}\).

  1. \(\liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n \le \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n\). Furthermore, \(\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = A \in \overline{\mathbb{R}}\) exists if and only if \[ \liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n = \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n = A. \]
  2. There exists a subsequence \(\{a_{n_k}\}_{k=1}^\infty\) such that \(\lim_{k \to \infty} a_{n_k} = \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n\). Similarly, there exists a subsequence \(\{a_{m_k}\}_{k=1}^\infty\) such that \(\lim_{k \to \infty} a_{m_k} = \liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n\).
  3. The subadditivity of \(\limsup\) holds: \[ \limsup_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b_n) \le \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n + \limsup_{n \to \infty} b_n, \tag{A.5}\] provided the right-hand side is not of the indeterminate form \(\infty - \infty\).
  4. If \(a_n \ge 0\) and \(b_n \ge 0\) for all \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), then \[ \limsup_{n \to \infty} (a_n b_n) \le \left( \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n \right) \cdot \left( \limsup_{n \to \infty} b_n \right), \tag{A.6}\] provided the right-hand side is not of the form \(0 \cdot \infty\) or \(\infty \cdot 0\).

Proof (Proof of Proposition A.1). Proof of Item 1: Since \(\inf\{a_k : k \ge n\} \le \sup\{a_k : k \ge n\}\) for all \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), taking \(n \to \infty\) shows \(\liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n \le \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n\).

Now suppose \(\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = A \in \mathbb{R}\) exists. For every \(\epsilon > 0\), there exists \(N(\epsilon) \in \mathbb{N}\) such that \(|A - a_n| \le \epsilon\) for all \(n \ge N(\epsilon)\), which implies \[ A - \epsilon \le a_k \le A + \epsilon \quad \text{for all } k \ge N(\epsilon). \] Taking infimum and supremum over \(k \ge N(\epsilon)\) gives \[ A - \epsilon \le \inf_{k \ge N(\epsilon)} a_k \le \sup_{k \ge N(\epsilon)} a_k \le A + \epsilon. \] Taking \(n \to \infty\), we obtain \[ A - \epsilon \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n \le \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n \le A + \epsilon. \] Since \(\epsilon > 0\) is arbitrary, \(A = \liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n = \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n\).

If \(A = +\infty\), then for any \(M > 0\), \(a_k \ge M\) for all \(k \ge N(M)\). Thus \(\inf_{k \ge N} a_k \ge M\), which implies \(\liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n \ge M\). As \(M\) is arbitrary, \(\liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n = \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n = +\infty\). The case \(A = -\infty\) is symmetric.

Conversely, if \(\liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n = \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n = A \in \mathbb{R}\), then for any \(\epsilon > 0\), there exists \(N \in \mathbb{N}\) such that \(A - \epsilon \le \inf_{k \ge N} a_k \le \sup_{k \ge N} a_k \le A + \epsilon\). Consequently, \(A - \epsilon \le a_n \le A + \epsilon\) for all \(n \ge N\), proving that \(\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = A\).

Proof of Items 2–4: Left as an exercise.

Example A.1 To illustrate strict inequality in Equation A.5, set \(a_n = (-1)^n\) and \(b_n = (-1)^{n+1}\). Then \(a_n + b_n = 0\) for all \(n\), so \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b_n) = \limsup_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b_n) = \liminf_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b_n) = 0. \] However, \(\limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n = 1\) and \(\limsup_{n \to \infty} b_n = 1\). Thus: \[ 0 = \limsup_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b_n) < \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n + \limsup_{n \to \infty} b_n = 1 + 1 = 2. \] Similarly, \(\liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n = -1\) and \(\liminf_{n \to \infty} b_n = -1\), yielding: \[ 0 = \liminf_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b_n) > \liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n + \liminf_{n \to \infty} b_n = -2. \]

Remark A.3. If \(a_n \le b_n\) for a.a. \(n\), then \[ \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n \le \limsup_{n \to \infty} b_n \quad \text{and} \quad \liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} b_n. \] In particular, if \(a_n = b_n\) for a.a. \(n\), their limsups and liminfs are equal. Furthermore, for any constant \(b \in \mathbb{R}\), \(\limsup_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b) = \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n + b\) and \(\liminf_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b) = \liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n + b\).

Lemma A.2 If \(\{a_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset \mathbb{R}\) and \(\{b_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset \mathbb{R}\) such that \(b = \lim_{n \to \infty} b_n\) exists in \(\mathbb{R}\), then \[ \liminf_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b_n) = \liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n + b. \]

Proof (Proof of Lemma A.2). Given \(\epsilon > 0\), choose \(N \in \mathbb{N}\) such that \(b - \epsilon \le b_n \le b + \epsilon\) for all \(n \ge N\). Then for all \(n \ge N\), \[ a_n + b - \epsilon \le a_n + b_n \le a_n + b + \epsilon. \] Taking the \(\liminf\) across this inequality and using Remark A.3 yields \[ \liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n + b - \epsilon \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b_n) \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n + b + \epsilon. \] Since \(\epsilon > 0\) is arbitrary, taking \(\epsilon \downarrow 0\) yields \(\liminf_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b_n) = \liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n + b\). \(\blacksquare\)

A.4 Infinite Sums and Classical Convergence Theorems

A.5 Non-negative Infinite Sums

Definition A.5 For any sequence \(a_n \in [0, \infty]\), we define the infinite sum as \[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n := \lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{n=1}^N a_n = \sup_{N \in \mathbb{N}} \sum_{n=1}^N a_n. \tag{A.7}\]

Remark A.4. If \(a_n, b_n \in [0, \infty]\) and \(\lambda > 0\), then \[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty (a_n + \lambda b_n) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n + \lambda \sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n. \] Indeed, finite sums are linear, and taking limits of non-decreasing sequences preserves linear combinations: \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{n=1}^\infty (a_n + \lambda b_n) &= \lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{n=1}^N (a_n + \lambda b_n) = \lim_{N \to \infty} \left[ \sum_{n=1}^N a_n + \lambda \sum_{n=1}^N b_n \right] \\ &= \lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{n=1}^N a_n + \lambda \lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{n=1}^N b_n = \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n + \lambda \sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n. \end{aligned} \]

Proposition A.2 (Monotone Convergence Theorem (MCT) for Sums) Suppose that for each \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), \(\{f_n(i)\}_{i=1}^\infty\) is a sequence in \([0, \infty]\) such that \(f_n(i) \uparrow f(i)\) as \(n \to \infty\) for each \(i \in \mathbb{N}\) (i.e., \(f_n(i)\) is non-decreasing in \(n\) and \(\lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(i) = f(i)\)). Then \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty f_n(i) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(i) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty f(i). \tag{A.8}\]

Proof (Proof of Proposition A.2). Let \(M := \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty f_n(i)\) (which exists in \([0, \infty]\) as the sequence of sums is non-decreasing).

Since \(f_n(i) \le f(i)\) for all \(n\) and \(i\), taking sums yields \(\sum_{i=1}^\infty f_n(i) \le \sum_{i=1}^\infty f(i)\) for all \(n\). Taking \(n \to \infty\) yields \[ M \le \sum_{i=1}^\infty f(i). \] Conversely, for any fixed \(N \in \mathbb{N}\), using the linearity of finite limits gives \[ \sum_{i=1}^N f(i) = \sum_{i=1}^N \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(i) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^N f_n(i) \le \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty f_n(i) = M. \] Taking \(N \to \infty\) on the left side shows \(\sum_{i=1}^\infty f(i) \le M\). Combining both inequalities yields \(M = \sum_{i=1}^\infty f(i)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition A.3 (Tonelli’s Theorem for Sums) If \(\{a_{k,n}\}_{k,n=1}^\infty \subset [0, \infty]\), then \[ \sum_{k=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_{k,n}. \tag{A.9}\]

Proof (Proof of Proposition A.3). Proof 1 (via MCT). For each \(k\), define \(S_N(k) := \sum_{n=1}^N a_{k,n}\). Notice that \(S_N(k) \ge 0\) and \(S_N(k) \uparrow \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n}\) as \(N \to \infty\). Applying the Monotone Convergence Theorem for sums (Proposition A.2) with \(f_N(k) = S_N(k)\) yields \[ \lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^\infty S_N(k) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \lim_{N \to \infty} S_N(k) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n}. \] On the other hand, interchanging finite sums gives \[ \sum_{k=1}^\infty S_N(k) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^N a_{k,n} = \sum_{n=1}^N \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_{k,n}. \] Taking \(N \to \infty\) yields \(\lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^\infty S_N(k) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_{k,n}\), proving Equation A.9.

Proof 2 (via Supremum). Let \(M := \sup \left\{ \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{n=1}^N a_{k,n} : K, N \in \mathbb{N} \right\}\) and \(L := \sum_{k=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n}\).

For any fixed \(K, N \in \mathbb{N}\): \[ \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{n=1}^N a_{k,n} \le \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n} \le \sum_{k=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n} = L. \] Taking the supremum over \(K, N\) yields \(M \le L\).

Conversely, \(L = \lim_{K \to \infty} \lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{n=1}^N a_{k,n}\). Since \(\sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{n=1}^N a_{k,n} \le M\) for all \(K, N\), taking limits yields \(L \le M\). Thus \(L = M\). By exact symmetry of indices in the definition of \(M\), \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_{k,n} = M = L\). \(\blacksquare\)

A.6 Summable Sequences and Linearity

Definition A.6 (Absolute Summability) A sequence \(\{a_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset \mathbb{R}\) is said to be summable (or absolutely convergent) if \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty |a_n| < \infty\).

For a summable sequence \(\{a_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\), we define its positive and negative parts by \(a_n^+ := \max(a_n, 0)\) and \(a_n^- := \max(-a_n, 0)\), so that \(a_n = a_n^+ - a_n^-\) and \(|a_n| = a_n^+ + a_n^-\). The sum is defined as: \[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n := \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n^+ - \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n^-. \tag{A.10}\]

Remark A.5. From Equation A.10, the triangle inequality for infinite series holds: \[ \begin{align*} \left| \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n \right| &= \left| \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n^+ - \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n^- \right| \\ &\le \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n^+ + \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n^- \\ &= \sum_{n=1}^\infty (a_n^+ + a_n^-) \\ &= \sum_{n=1}^\infty |a_n|. \end{align*} \]

Proposition A.4 (Linearity of Infinite Sums) If \(\{a_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) and \(\{b_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) are summable sequences in \(\mathbb{R}\) and \(\lambda \in \mathbb{R}\), then \(\{a_n + \lambda b_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) is summable, and \[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty (a_n + \lambda b_n) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n + \lambda \sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n. \]

Proof. Proof. Let \(c_n := a_n + \lambda b_n\). By the triangle inequality, \(|c_n| \le |a_n| + |\lambda| |b_n|\). Thus: \[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty |c_n| \le \sum_{n=1}^\infty |a_n| + |\lambda| \sum_{n=1}^\infty |b_n| < \infty, \] proving \(\{c_n\}\) is summable.

First, assume \(\lambda \ge 0\). Since \(c_n = c_n^+ - c_n^- = a_n^+ - a_n^- + \lambda(b_n^+ - b_n^-)\), rearranging terms yields non-negative identities: \[ c_n^+ + a_n^- + \lambda b_n^- = c_n^- + a_n^+ + \lambda b_n^+. \] Summing both sides over \(n \in \mathbb{N}\) and using Remark A.4 gives \[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty c_n^+ + \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n^- + \lambda \sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n^- = \sum_{n=1}^\infty c_n^- + \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n^+ + \lambda \sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n^+. \] Rearranging finite real quantities gives \[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty c_n^+ - \sum_{n=1}^\infty c_n^- = \left( \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n^+ - \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n^- \right) + \lambda \left( \sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n^+ - \sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n^- \right), \] which is \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty c_n = \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n + \lambda \sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n\).

Finally, for \(\lambda = -1\), observe that \((-a_n)^+ = a_n^-\) and \((-a_n)^- = a_n^+\). Thus, \[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty (-a_n) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n^- - \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n^+ = -\left( \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n^+ - \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n^- \right) = -\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n. \] Combining this with \(\lambda \ge 0\) completes the proof for all \(\lambda \in \mathbb{R}\).

Proposition A.5 (Fubini’s Theorem for Sums) Suppose \(\{a_{k,n}\}_{k,n=1}^\infty \subset \mathbb{R}\) is double-indexed and satisfies \[ \sum_{k=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty |a_{k,n}| < \infty. \] Then: \[ \sum_{k=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_{k,n}. \tag{A.11}\]

A.7 Convergence Theorems: Fatou and DCT for Sums

Proposition A.6 (Fatou’s Lemma for Sums) Suppose that for each \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), \(\{h_n(i)\}_{i=1}^\infty\) is a sequence in \([0, \infty]\). Then \[ \sum_{i=1}^\infty \liminf_{n \to \infty} h_n(i) \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty h_n(i). \tag{A.12}\]

Proposition A.7 (Dominated Convergence Theorem (DCT) for Sums) Suppose that for each \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), \(\{f_n(i)\}_{i=1}^\infty \subset \mathbb{R}\) and \(\{g_n(i)\}_{i=1}^\infty \subset [0, \infty)\) satisfy:

  1. \(\sum_{i=1}^\infty g_n(i) < \infty\) for all \(n \in \mathbb{N}\).
  2. Pointwise limits \(f(i) := \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(i)\) and \(g(i) := \lim_{n \to \infty} g_n(i)\) exist for every \(i \in \mathbb{N}\).
  3. Domination condition: \(|f_n(i)| \le g_n(i)\) for all \(i, n \in \mathbb{N}\).
  4. \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty g_n(i) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty g(i) < \infty\).

Then: \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty f_n(i) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(i) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty f(i). \tag{A.13}\]

A.8 Examples and counterexamples

Example A.2 (Failure of Fubini / Interchanging Limits) Let \(\{S_{m,n}\}_{m,n=1}^\infty\) be a double sequence of real numbers such that \(\lim_{m \to \infty} S_{m,n} = 1\) for all \(n\) and \(\lim_{n \to \infty} S_{m,n} = 0\) for all \(m\). For instance, take \(S_{m,n} = \mathbf{1}_{\{m \ge n\}}\) or \(S_{m,n} = \frac{m}{m+n}\).

Define \(\{a_{i,j}\}_{i,j=1}^\infty\) via discrete mixed differences (with \(S_{m,0} = S_{0,n} = 0\)): \[ a_{m,n} := S_{m,n} - S_{m-1,n} - S_{m,n-1} + S_{m-1,n-1}. \] This choice ensures that \(S_{m,n} = \sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^n a_{i,j}\). Consequently: \[ \begin{align*} &\sum_{i=1}^\infty \sum_{j=1}^\infty a_{i,j} = \lim_{i \to \infty} \lim_{j \to \infty} S_{i,j} = 0\\ & \neq 1 = \lim_{j \to \infty} \lim_{i \to \infty} S_{i,j} = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \sum_{i=1}^\infty a_{i,j}. \end{align*} \tag{A.14}\]

Evaluating \(a_{m,n}\) explicitly for \(S_{m,n} = \mathbf{1}_{\{m \ge n\}}\) gives: \[ \begin{align*} a_{m,n} &= \mathbf{1}_{\{m \ge n\}} - \mathbf{1}_{\{m-1 \ge n\}} - \mathbf{1}_{\{m \ge n-1\}} + \mathbf{1}_{\{m-1 \ge n-1\}} \\ &= \mathbf{1}_{\{m = n\}} - \mathbf{1}_{\{m = n-1\}}. \end{align*} \tag{A.15}\] Thus, \(a_{m,n} = 1\) if \(m = n\), \(a_{m,n} = -1\) if \(n = m+1\), and \(a_{m,n} = 0\) otherwise.

Fixing \(n\), the row sum yields \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_{m,n} = \mathbf{1}_{\{n = 1\}}\). Fixing \(m\), the column sum yields \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{m,n} = 0\). Thus: \[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty \sum_{m=1}^\infty a_{m,n} = 1 \quad \neq \quad 0 = \sum_{m=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{m,n}. \] Notice that \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty |a_{m,n}| = \infty\), confirming why Fubini’s Theorem (Proposition A.5) does not apply.

Example A.3 (Continuous Counterexample on \(\mathbb{R}_+^2\)) A continuous analog of Example A.2 is constructed on \([0, \infty)^2\) via: \[ h(x,y) = \mathbf{1}_{\{x \le y \le x+1\}} - \mathbf{1}_{\{x+1 < y \le x+2\}}. \tag{A.16}\]

Integrating with respect to \(y\) for a fixed \(x \ge 0\): \[ \int_0^\infty h(x,y) \, dy = \int_x^{x+1} 1 \, dy - \int_{x+1}^{x+2} 1 \, dy = 1 - 1 = 0. \] Integrating with respect to \(x\) for a fixed \(y \ge 0\): \[ \int_0^\infty h(x,y) \, dx = y \mathbf{1}_{\{y \le 1\}} + (2-y) \mathbf{1}_{\{1 \le y \le 2\}}. \] Taking iterated integrals yields: \[ \int_0^\infty \left( \int_0^\infty h(x,y) \, dy \right) dx = \int_0^\infty 0 \, dx = 0, \] while \[ \int_0^\infty \left( \int_0^\infty h(x,y) \, dx \right) dy = \int_0^1 y \, dy + \int_1^2 (2-y) \, dy = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = 1. \] Hence, \(\int_0^\infty \int_0^\infty h(x,y) \, dy \, dx = 0 \neq 1 = \int_0^\infty \int_0^\infty h(x,y) \, dx \, dy\).

A.9 Exercises and Proof Solutions

A.10 Exercise 1.1: Fubini’s Theorem for Sums

Exercise A.1 Prove Proposition 1.15 (Proposition A.5).

Proof. Solution / Proof. Let \(a_{k,n}^+ := \max(a_{k,n}, 0)\) and \(a_{k,n}^- := \max(-a_{k,n}, 0)\). Then \(a_{k,n} = a_{k,n}^+ - a_{k,n}^-\) and \(|a_{k,n}| = a_{k,n}^+ + a_{k,n}^-\). Since \(a_{k,n}^+, a_{k,n}^- \le |a_{k,n}|\), Tonelli’s Theorem for non-negative double sums (Proposition A.3) implies: \[ \sum_{k=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n}^+ = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_{k,n}^+ \le \sum_{k=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty |a_{k,n}| < \infty, \] and \[ \sum_{k=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n}^- = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_{k,n}^- \le \sum_{k=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty |a_{k,n}| < \infty. \] For each \(k \in \mathbb{N}\), \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty |a_{k,n}| < \infty\), so \(S_k := \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n}\) converges absolutely and equals \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n}^+ - \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n}^-\). By Proposition A.4, \(\{S_k\}_{k=1}^\infty\) is summable because \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty |S_k| \le \sum_{k=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty |a_{k,n}| < \infty\).

Using the linearity of summable series: \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{k=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n} &= \sum_{k=1}^\infty \left( \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n}^+ - \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n}^- \right) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n}^+ - \sum_{k=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{k,n}^- \\ &= \sum_{n=1}^\infty \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_{k,n}^+ - \sum_{n=1}^\infty \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_{k,n}^- = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \left( \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_{k,n}^+ - \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_{k,n}^- \right) \\ &= \sum_{n=1}^\infty \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_{k,n}. \end{aligned} \] This completes the proof.

A.11 Exercise 1.2: Fatou’s Lemma for Sums

Exercise A.2 Prove Proposition 1.17 (Proposition A.6).

Proof. Solution / Proof. For each \(n \in \mathbb{N}\) and \(i \in \mathbb{N}\), define \(f_n(i) := \inf_{m \ge n} h_m(i)\).

By construction:

  1. \(f_n(i) \le h_n(i)\) for all \(n, i \in \mathbb{N}\).
  2. \(f_n(i)\) is non-decreasing in \(n\) for each \(i \in \mathbb{N}\).
  3. \(\lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(i) = \sup_{n \ge 1} \inf_{m \ge n} h_m(i) = \liminf_{n \to \infty} h_n(i)\).

Applying the Monotone Convergence Theorem for sums (Proposition A.2) to the non-decreasing sequence \(f_n(i)\) yields: \[ \sum_{i=1}^\infty \liminf_{n \to \infty} h_n(i) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(i) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty f_n(i) = \liminf_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty f_n(i). \] Since \(f_n(i) \le h_n(i)\) for all \(i\), monotonicity of sums implies \(\sum_{i=1}^\infty f_n(i) \le \sum_{i=1}^\infty h_n(i)\) for all \(n\). Taking the \(\liminf\) as \(n \to \infty\) gives: \[ \liminf_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty f_n(i) \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty h_n(i). \] Combining these two results proves Equation A.12.

A.12 Exercise 1.3: Dominated Convergence Theorem for Sums

Exercise A.3 Prove Proposition 1.18 (Proposition A.7).

Proof. Solution / Proof. Since \(|f_n(i)| \le g_n(i)\), we have \(g_n(i) + f_n(i) \ge 0\) and \(g_n(i) - f_n(i) \ge 0\) for all \(n, i \in \mathbb{N}\).

Step 1: Lower bound via \(g_n + f_n\). Define \(h_n(i) := g_n(i) + f_n(i) \ge 0\). Applying Fatou’s Lemma (Proposition A.6) to \(h_n\): \[ \sum_{i=1}^\infty \liminf_{n \to \infty} (g_n(i) + f_n(i)) \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty (g_n(i) + f_n(i)). \] Since \(\lim_{n \to \infty} g_n(i) = g(i)\) exists, \(\liminf_{n \to \infty} (g_n(i) + f_n(i)) = g(i) + \liminf_{n \to \infty} f_n(i) = g(i) + f(i)\). Using Lemma A.2 with \(b_n = \sum_{i=1}^\infty g_n(i) \to \sum_{i=1}^\infty g(i) < \infty\): \[ \sum_{i=1}^\infty g(i) + \sum_{i=1}^\infty f(i) \le \sum_{i=1}^\infty g(i) + \liminf_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty f_n(i). \] Subtracting the finite quantity \(\sum_{i=1}^\infty g(i)\) from both sides yields: \[ \sum_{i=1}^\infty f(i) \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty f_n(i). \tag{A.17}\]

Step 2: Upper bound via \(g_n - f_n\). Define \(k_n(i) := g_n(i) - f_n(i) \ge 0\). Applying Fatou’s Lemma (Proposition A.6) to \(k_n\): \[ \sum_{i=1}^\infty \liminf_{n \to \infty} (g_n(i) - f_n(i)) \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty (g_n(i) - f_n(i)). \] Using \(\liminf_{n \to \infty} (g_n(i) - f_n(i)) = g(i) - \limsup_{n \to \infty} f_n(i) = g(i) - f(i)\) and \(\liminf (-a_n) = -\limsup a_n\): \[ \sum_{i=1}^\infty g(i) - \sum_{i=1}^\infty f(i) \le \sum_{i=1}^\infty g(i) - \limsup_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty f_n(i). \] Subtracting \(\sum_{i=1}^\infty g(i)\) and multiplying by \(-1\) reverses the inequality: \[ \limsup_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty f_n(i) \le \sum_{i=1}^\infty f(i). \tag{A.18}\]

Step 3: Conclusion. Combining Equation A.17 and Equation A.18 with Proposition A.1 (Item 1) gives: \[ \sum_{i=1}^\infty f(i) \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty f_n(i) \le \limsup_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty f_n(i) \le \sum_{i=1}^\infty f(i). \] Thus \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^\infty f_n(i) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty f(i)\), completing the proof.
