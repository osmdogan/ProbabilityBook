Appendix B — Basic Metric and Topological Space Notions

These notes cover fundamental concepts in metric and topological space theory essential for advanced probability and measure theory.

B.1 Pseudo-Metrics and Metric Spaces

Definition B.1 (Pseudo-Metric and Metric Spaces) Let \(X\) be a non-empty set. A function \(d \colon X \times X \to [0, \infty)\) is called a pseudo-metric on \(X\) if \(d\) is symmetric and satisfies the triangle inequality:

  1. Symmetry: \(d(x, y) = d(y, x)\) for all \(x, y \in X\).
  2. Triangle Inequality: \(d(x, z) \le d(x, y) + d(y, z)\) for all \(x, y, z \in X\).

If we further assume that \(d\) is non-degenerate in the sense that \(d(x, y) = 0\) if and only if \(x = y\), then \(d\) is called a metric on \(X\). The pair \((X, d)\) is called a metric space.

Any subset \(Y \subseteq X\) of a (pseudo-)metric space \((X, d)\) is naturally a (pseudo-)metric space equipped with the restricted function \(\left.d\right|_{Y \times Y}\).

Example B.1 (Standard Metric Spaces)  

  1. Let \(X = \mathbb{R}\). The standard metric is \(d(x, y) = |y - x|\). Another bounded metric on \(\mathbb{R}\) is given by \(d(x, y) = |\tan^{-1}(y) - \tan^{-1}(x)|\).
  2. Let \(X = \mathbb{R}^d\). The standard Euclidean distance metric is given by \(d(x, y) = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d}(y_j - x_j)^2}\). Subspaces such as the unit sphere \(S^{d-1} \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) inherit this metric.
  3. Let \(X = C([0, 1], \mathbb{C})\) denote the space of continuous complex-valued functions on \([0, 1]\). Then \[d(f, g) := \max_{x \in [0, 1]} |f(x) - g(x)|\] is a metric on \(X\), whereas \[d_{1/2}(f, g) := \max_{x \in [0, 1/2]} |f(x) - g(x)|\] is a pseudo-metric on \(X\) (since non-zero functions vanishing on \([0, 1/2]\) satisfy \(d_{1/2}(f, 0) = 0\)).
  4. Any normed vector space \((X, \|\cdot\|)\) is a metric space with induced metric \(d(x, y) := \|x - y\|\). In particular, \(L^p(\mu)\) spaces (for \(p \in [1, \infty]\)) are metric spaces.
  5. Let \(X\) denote the space of \(C^1\)-periodic functions on \(\mathbb{R}\) with period \(1\). Then \[d(f, g) := \max_{x \in [0, 1]} |f'(x) - g'(x)|\] defines a pseudo-metric on \(X\).

Throughout this chapter, \((X, d)\) denotes a pseudo-metric space. We frequently refer to \((X, d)\) simply as a metric space even when \(d\) is allowed to be degenerate, unless explicitly noted otherwise.

Definition B.2 (Open and Closed Balls) Let \((X, d)\) be a metric space.

  1. The open ball centered at \(x \in X\) with radius \(\delta > 0\) is defined by \[B(x, \delta) \equiv B_x(\delta) := \{y \in X \colon d(x, y) < \delta\}.\]
  2. The closed ball centered at \(x \in X\) with radius \(\delta > 0\) is defined by \[C_x(\delta) := \{y \in X \colon d(x, y) \le \delta\}.\]

Definition B.3 (Sequence Convergence) A sequence \(\{x_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty} \subset X\) is said to converge to a point \(x \in X\) (denoted \(x_n \to x\) as \(n \to \infty\)) if \(\lim_{n \to \infty} d(x, x_n) = 0\).

If \(x_n \to x\) and \(x_n \to y\), then by the triangle inequality: \[d(x, y) \le d(x, x_n) + d(x_n, y) \to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty,\] which implies \(d(x, y) = 0\). When \(d\) is non-degenerate (a metric), \(x = y\), ensuring unique limits. For general pseudo-metrics, limits are unique up to distance zero.

Definition B.4 (Bounded, Closed, and Open Sets)  

  1. A set \(E \subset X\) is bounded if \(E \subset B(x, R)\) for some \(x \in X\) and \(R < \infty\).
  2. A set \(F \subset X\) is closed if every convergent sequence \(\{x_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty} \subset F\) has its limit in \(F\). We denote this by \(F \sqsubset X\).
  3. A set \(V \subset X\) is open if its complement \(V^c := X \setminus V\) is closed. We denote this by \(V \subset_o X\).
  4. The collection of all open subsets of \(X\) relative to \(d\) is denoted by \(\tau_d\).

Exercise B.1 Let \(\mathcal{F}\) be a collection of closed subsets of \(X\). Show that \(\bigcap \mathcal{F} := \bigcap_{F \in \mathcal{F}} F\) is closed. Furthermore, show that finite unions of closed sets are closed: if \(\{F_k\}_{k=1}^{K}\) are closed sets, then \(\bigcup_{k=1}^{K} F_k\) is closed. (By taking complements, this implies that \(\tau_d\) is closed under finite intersections and arbitrary unions.) Show by example that a countable union of closed sets need not be closed.

Proof.

  1. Arbitrary Intersections: Let \(\{x_n\} \subset \bigcap_{F \in \mathcal{F}} F\) with \(x_n \to x\). For each \(F \in \mathcal{F}\), \(\{x_n\} \subset F\). Since \(F\) is closed, \(x \in F\). Thus \(x \in \bigcap_{F \in \mathcal{F}} F\), proving the intersection is closed.
  2. Finite Unions: Let \(\{x_n\} \subset \bigcup_{k=1}^{K} F_k\) with \(x_n \to x\). By the pigeonhole principle, there exists at least one \(k_0 \in \{1, \dots, K\}\) such that \(F_{k_0}\) contains an infinite subsequence \(\{x_{n_j}\}\). Since \(x_{n_j} \to x\) and \(F_{k_0}\) is closed, \(x \in F_{k_0} \subset \bigcup_{k=1}^{K} F_k\).
  3. Counterexample: In \(\mathbb{R}\) with the standard metric, \(F_n = [1/n, 1 - 1/n]\) is closed for each \(n \ge 2\), but \(\bigcup_{n=2}^{\infty} F_n = (0, 1)\), which is open and not closed.

Exercise B.2 Show that \(V \subset X\) is open if and only if for every \(x \in V\) there exists \(\delta > 0\) such that \(B_x(\delta) \subset V\). In particular, show \(B_x(\delta)\) is open for all \(x \in X\) and \(\delta > 0\).

Proof.

  1. \((\Rightarrow)\) Suppose \(V\) is open, so \(V^c\) is closed. Assume for contradiction that there exists \(x \in V\) such that for every \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), \(B_x(1/n) \not\subset V\). Then choose \(x_n \in B_x(1/n) \cap V^c\). Then \(x_n \in V^c\) and \(d(x_n, x) < 1/n \to 0\), so \(x_n \to x\). Since \(V^c\) is closed, \(x \in V^c\), contradicting \(x \in V\).
  2. \((\Leftarrow)\) Suppose for every \(x \in V\), there exists \(\delta_x > 0\) such that \(B_x(\delta_x) \subset V\). Let \(\{x_n\} \subset V^c\) be a sequence with \(x_n \to x\). If \(x \in V\), there exists \(\delta > 0\) such that \(B_x(\delta) \subset V\). But \(x_n \to x\) implies \(x_n \in B_x(\delta) \subset V\) for large \(n\), contradicting \(x_n \in V^c\). Thus \(x \in V^c\), so \(V^c\) is closed and \(V\) is open.
  3. Open Ball Openness: Let \(y \in B_x(\delta)\) and set \(\epsilon = \delta - d(x, y) > 0\). If \(z \in B_y(\epsilon)\), then \(d(x, z) \le d(x, y) + d(y, z) < d(x, y) + \epsilon = \delta\). Thus \(B_y(\epsilon) \subset B_x(\delta)\), proving \(B_x(\delta)\) is open.

Definition B.5 (Neighborhoods) A subset \(A \subset X\) is a neighborhood of \(x \in X\) if there exists an open set \(V \subset_o X\) such that \(x \in V \subset A\). \(A\) is an open neighborhood of \(x\) if \(A\) is open and \(x \in A\).

Lemma B.1 (Continuity of Distance to a Set) For any non-empty subset \(A \subset X\), define the distance function \(d_A \colon X \to [0, \infty)\) by \[d_A(x) := \inf \{d(x, a) \colon a \in A\}.\] Then \(d_A\) is 1-Lipschitz continuous: \[|d_A(x) - d_A(y)| \le d(x, y) \quad \forall x, y \in X. \tag{B.1}\]

In particular, if \(x_n \to x\) in \(X\), then \(d_A(x_n) \to d_A(x)\) as \(n \to \infty\). Moreover, for any \(\epsilon > 0\), the set \(F_\epsilon := \{x \in X \colon d_A(x) \ge \epsilon\}\) is closed in \(X\).

Proof. Let \(a \in A\) and \(x, y \in X\). By the triangle inequality: \[d_A(x) \le d(x, a) \le d(x, y) + d(y, a).\] Taking the infimum over \(a \in A\) yields \[d_A(x) \le d(x, y) + d_A(y) \implies d_A(x) - d_A(y) \le d(x, y).\] Swapping \(x\) and \(y\) gives \(d_A(y) - d_A(x) \le d(x, y)\), establishing Equation B.1.

If \(x_n \to x\), then \(|d_A(x) - d_A(x_n)| \le d(x, x_n) \to 0\), proving continuity.

Now let \(\{x_n\} \subset F_\epsilon\) with \(x_n \to x\). Since \(d_A(x_n) \ge \epsilon\) for all \(n\), continuity implies \[d_A(x) = \lim_{n \to \infty} d_A(x_n) \ge \epsilon,\] so \(x \in F_\epsilon\), proving that \(F_\epsilon\) is closed.

Corollary B.1 (Joint Continuity of Metric) The metric function \(d \colon X \times X \to [0, \infty)\) satisfies \[|d(x, y) - d(x', y')| \le d(x, x') + d(y, y'). \tag{B.2}\]

Proof. Applying Lemma B.1 with singletons \(A = \{y\}\) and \(A = \{y'\}\) alongside the real triangle inequality: \[\begin{align*} |d(x, y) - d(x', y')| &\le |d(x, y) - d(x, y')| + |d(x, y') - d(x', y')| \\ &\le d(y, y') + d(x, x'). \tag*{$\blacksquare$} \end{align*}\]

Example B.2 For \(x \in X\) and \(\delta > 0\), \(C_x(\delta)\) and \(B_x(\delta)^c\) are closed. Indeed, if \(\{y_n\} \subset C_x(\delta)\) with \(y_n \to y\), then \(d(y_n, x) \le \delta\) for all \(n\). By Corollary B.1, \(d(y, x) = \lim_{n \to \infty} d(y_n, x) \le \delta\), so \(y \in C_x(\delta)\).

Lemma B.2 (Approximating Open Sets from Inside by Closed Sets) Let \(A \subset X\) be closed and let \(F_\epsilon := \{x \in X \colon d_A(x) \ge \epsilon\}\). Then \(F_\epsilon \uparrow A^c\) as \(\epsilon \downarrow 0\).

Proof. For any \(x \in A\), \(d_A(x) = 0\), so \(F_\epsilon \subset A^c\) for all \(\epsilon > 0\), implying \(\bigcup_{\epsilon > 0} F_\epsilon \subset A^c\). Conversely, let \(x \in A^c\). Since \(A^c\) is open, there exists \(\epsilon > 0\) such that \(B_x(\epsilon) \subset A^c\), which means \(d(x, y) \ge \epsilon\) for all \(y \in A\). Thus \(d_A(x) \ge \epsilon\), so \(x \in F_\epsilon\). Finally, \(F_\epsilon \subset F_{\epsilon'}\) whenever \(\epsilon' \le \epsilon\), completing the monotonic union \(F_\epsilon \uparrow A^c\).

Definition B.6 (Closure, Density, and Separability) Let \(A \subset X\).

  1. The closure of \(A\), denoted \(\overline{A}\), is defined as \[\overline{A} := \{x \in X \colon \exists \{x_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty} \subset A \text{ with } x_n \to x\}.\]
  2. \(A\) is dense in \(X\) if \(\overline{A} = X\).
  3. A metric space \((X, d)\) is separable if it contains a countable dense subset \(D \subset X\).

Exercise B.3 Given \(A \subset X\), show that \(\overline{A}\) is closed and satisfies \[\overline{A} = \bigcap \{F \subset X \colon A \subset F \text{ and } F \text{ is closed}\}. \tag{B.3}\] That is, \(\overline{A}\) is the smallest closed set containing \(A\).

Proof. Let \(C = \bigcap \{F \subset X \colon A \subset F, F \text{ is closed}\}\).

  1. \(\overline{A} \subset C\): Let \(x \in \overline{A}\), so \(x_n \to x\) for some \(\{x_n\} \subset A\). If \(F\) is any closed set containing \(A\), then \(\{x_n\} \subset F\), so its limit \(x \in F\). Thus \(x \in C\).
  2. \(C \subset \overline{A}\): It suffices to show \(\overline{A}\) is closed (since \(A \subset \overline{A}\) trivially). Let \(\{y_k\} \subset \overline{A}\) with \(y_k \to y\). For each \(k\), choose \(x_k \in A\) with \(d(x_k, y_k) < 1/k\). Then \(d(x_k, y) \le d(x_k, y_k) + d(y_k, y) \to 0\), so \(x_k \to y \implies y \in \overline{A}\). Hence \(\overline{A}\) is closed, which implies \(C \subset \overline{A}\).

Exercise B.4 Let \(D \subset X\) be dense in \((X, d)\) and let \(E \subset X\) be such that for every \(x \in D\), there exists a sequence \(\{x_n\} \subset E\) converging to \(x\). Show that \(E\) is dense in \(X\).

Proof. Let \(x \in X\) and \(\epsilon > 0\). Since \(D\) is dense in \(X\), there exists \(y \in D\) with \(d(x, y) < \epsilon / 2\). Since \(E\) approximates \(D\), there exists \(z \in E\) with \(d(y, z) < \epsilon / 2\). By the triangle inequality, \(d(x, z) < \epsilon\). Thus \(x \in \overline{E}\), proving \(\overline{E} = X\).

Exercise B.5 Suppose \((X, d)\) contains an uncountable subset \(\Lambda \subset X\) such that there exists \(\epsilon > 0\) with \(d(a, b) \ge \epsilon\) for all \(a, b \in \Lambda\) with \(a \neq b\). Show that \((X, d)\) is not separable.

Proof. Suppose \(D \subset X\) is dense. For each \(a \in \Lambda\), the open ball \(B_a(\epsilon/2)\) must contain at least one point \(d_a \in D\). Since \(d(a, b) \ge \epsilon\) for distinct \(a, b \in \Lambda\), the balls \(\{B_a(\epsilon/2)\}_{a \in \Lambda}\) are pairwise disjoint. Thus the map \(a \mapsto d_a\) is an injection from the uncountable set \(\Lambda\) into \(D\), implying \(D\) must be uncountable. Hence no countable dense subset exists.

B.2 Metric Spaces as Topological Spaces

Definition B.7 (Topological Space) Let \(X\) be a set. A topology on \(X\) is a collection \(\tau\) of subsets of \(X\) satisfying:

  1. \(\emptyset \in \tau\) and \(X \in \tau\).
  2. \(\tau\) is closed under arbitrary unions: if \(\{V_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A} \subset \tau\), then \(\bigcup_{\alpha \in A} V_\alpha \in \tau\).
  3. \(\tau\) is closed under finite intersections: if \(V_1, \dots, V_n \in \tau\), then \(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n} V_i \in \tau\).

The pair \((X, \tau)\) is a topological space, and sets \(V \in \tau\) are called open. A set \(F \subset X\) is closed if \(F^c \in \tau\). Let \(\tau_x = \{V \in \tau \colon x \in V\}\) denote the open neighborhoods of \(x\). Every metric space \((X, d)\) naturally forms a topological space \((X, \tau_d)\).

Definition B.8 (Topological Operations) Let \((X, \tau)\) be a topological space and \(A \subset X\).

  1. Closure: \(\overline{A} := \bigcap \{F \subset X \colon A \subset F, F^c \in \tau\}\).
  2. Interior: \(A^\circ := \bigcup \{V \in \tau \colon V \subset A\}\).
  3. Neighborhood: \(A\) is a neighborhood of \(x\) if \(x \in A^\circ\).
  4. Accumulation Points: \(\mathrm{acc}(A) := \{x \in X \colon V \cap (A \setminus \{x\}) \neq \emptyset \text{ for all } V \in \tau_x\}\).
  5. Boundary: \(\mathrm{bd}(A) := \overline{A} \setminus A^\circ\).

Remark B.1. The dual relations between interior and closure are given by: \[(A^\circ)^c = \overline{A^c} \quad \text{and} \quad (\overline{A})^c = (A^c)^\circ. \tag{B.4}\] Hence the boundary satisfies \(\mathrm{bd}(A) = \overline{A} \cap \overline{A^c}\).

B.2.1 Continuity

Definition B.9 (Pointwise Continuity (Metric)) Let \((X, \rho)\) and \((Y, d)\) be metric spaces. A function \(f \colon X \to Y\) is continuous at \(x \in X\) if for all \(\epsilon > 0\), there exists \(\delta > 0\) such that \[d(f(x), f(x')) < \epsilon \quad \text{whenever} \quad \rho(x, x') < \delta. \tag{B.5}\] \(f\) is continuous if it is continuous at every \(x \in X\).

Lemma B.3 (Local Continuity Characterizations) Let \((X, \rho)\) and \((Y, d)\) be metric spaces and \(f \colon X \to Y\). The following are equivalent for \(x \in X\):

  1. \(f\) is continuous at \(x\).
  2. For all neighborhoods \(A \subset Y\) of \(f(x)\), \(f^{-1}(A)\) is a neighborhood of \(x\).
  3. For all sequences \(\{x_n\} \subset X\) with \(x_n \to x\), \(f(x_n) \to f(x)\) in \(Y\).

Proof.

  • \((1 \Rightarrow 2)\): If \(A\) is a neighborhood of \(f(x)\), there exists \(\epsilon > 0\) such that \(B_{f(x)}(\epsilon) \subset A\). By continuity, pick \(\delta > 0\) satisfying Equation B.5. Then \(B_x(\delta) \subset f^{-1}(B_{f(x)}(\epsilon)) \subset f^{-1}(A)\), so \(f^{-1}(A)\) is a neighborhood of \(x\).
  • \((2 \Rightarrow 3)\): Suppose \(x_n \to x\). For any \(\epsilon > 0\), \(B_{f(x)}(\epsilon)\) is a neighborhood of \(f(x)\), so \(f^{-1}(B_{f(x)}(\epsilon))\) is a neighborhood of \(x\), containing \(B_x(\delta)\) for some \(\delta > 0\). Since \(x_n \to x\), \(x_n \in B_x(\delta)\) for almost all \(n\), so \(f(x_n) \in B_{f(x)}(\epsilon)\) for almost all \(n\). Thus \(f(x_n) \to f(x)\).
  • \((3 \Rightarrow 1)\): We prove \(\neg 1 \Rightarrow \neg 3\). If \(f\) is not continuous at \(x\), there exists \(\epsilon > 0\) such that for each \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), there is \(x_n \in X\) with \(\rho(x_n, x) < 1/n\) but \(d(f(x_n), f(x)) \ge \epsilon\). Then \(x_n \to x\) but \(f(x_n) \not\to f(x)\).

Lemma B.4 (Global Continuity Characterizations) Let \((X, \rho)\) and \((Y, d)\) be metric spaces and \(f \colon X \to Y\). The following are equivalent:

  1. \(f\) is continuous on \(X\).
  2. \(f^{-1}(V) \in \tau_\rho\) for all \(V \in \tau_d\) (preimages of open sets are open).
  3. \(f^{-1}(C)\) is closed in \(X\) for all closed \(C \subset Y\).
  4. For all convergent sequences \(x_n \to x\) in \(X\), \(f(x_n) \to f(x)\) in \(Y\).

Proof. Equivalence of (2) and (3) follows immediately from \(f^{-1}(C^c) = [f^{-1}(C)]^c\). Equivalence of (1), (2), and (4) follows from applying Lemma B.3 pointwise across \(X\).

Definition B.10 (Continuity in Topological Terms) Let \((X, \tau_X)\) and \((Y, \tau_Y)\) be topological spaces.

  1. \(f \colon X \to Y\) is continuous at \(x \in X\) if for every open neighborhood \(V \in \tau_Y\) of \(f(x)\), there exists an open neighborhood \(U \in \tau_X\) of \(x\) such that \(U \subset f^{-1}(V)\).
  2. \(f\) is globally continuous if \(f^{-1}(\tau_Y) \subset \tau_X\). The set of continuous functions is denoted \(C(X, Y)\).

Exercise B.6 Show \(f \colon X \to Y\) is continuous under Definition 2.19 if and only if \(f\) is continuous at all points \(x \in X\).

Proof.

  1. \((\Rightarrow)\) If \(f^{-1}(V) \in \tau_X\) for all \(V \in \tau_Y\), then given \(x \in X\) and \(V \in \tau_Y\) with \(f(x) \in V\), set \(U = f^{-1}(V) \in \tau_X\). Then \(x \in U\) and \(U \subset f^{-1}(V)\), so \(f\) is continuous at \(x\).
  2. \((\Leftarrow)\) Let \(V \in \tau_Y\). For every \(x \in f^{-1}(V)\), continuity at \(x\) provides \(U_x \in \tau_X\) with \(x \in U_x \subset f^{-1}(V)\). Then \(f^{-1}(V) = \bigcup_{x \in f^{-1}(V)} U_x\), which is open as an arbitrary union of open sets.

Exercise B.7 Show \(f \colon X \to Y\) is continuous if and only if \(f^{-1}(C)\) is closed in \(X\) for all closed subsets \(C \subset Y\).

Proof. This follows directly from set operations: \(C \subset Y\) is closed \(\iff Y \setminus C\) is open. Since \(f^{-1}(Y \setminus C) = X \setminus f^{-1}(C)\), \(f^{-1}(Y \setminus C)\) is open \(\iff f^{-1}(C)\) is closed.

Definition B.11 (Homeomorphism) A map \(f \colon X \to Y\) between topological spaces is a homeomorphism if \(f\) is bijective, \(f\) is continuous, and its inverse \(f^{-1} \colon Y \to X\) is continuous.

Example B.3 By Lemma B.1, \(d_A \colon X \to [0, \infty)\) is continuous. Setting \(A = \{x\}\), the map \(y \mapsto d(y, x)\) is continuous for each fixed \(x \in X\).

Exercise B.8 (Joint Continuity Criterion) Let \(X, Y, Z\) be metric spaces and \(F \colon X \times Y \to Z\) be a function such that:

  1. For each \(x \in X\), \(y \mapsto F(x, y)\) is continuous, and these maps are locally equi-continuous in \(x\): for all \((a, b) \in X \times Y\), \[\lim_{y \to b} \sup_{x \in B_X(a, r)} d(F(x, y), F(x, b)) = 0 \quad \text{for some } r > 0. \tag{B.6}\]
  2. There exists a dense subset \(Y_0 \subset Y\) such that \(x \mapsto F(x, y)\) is continuous for fixed \(y \in Y_0\).

Show: 1. \(x \mapsto F(x, y)\) is continuous for all \(y \in Y\). 2. \(F \colon X \times Y \to Z\) is jointly continuous on \(X \times Y\).

Proof.

  1. Fix \(y \in Y\) and \(a \in X\). Let \(b = y\). By local equicontinuity, choose \(r > 0\) such that Equation B.6 holds. For \(\epsilon > 0\), choose \(y_0 \in Y_0 \cap B_Y(b, \delta)\) such that \(\sup_{x \in B_X(a, r)} d(F(x, y_0), F(x, y)) < \epsilon / 3\). Since \(x \mapsto F(x, y_0)\) is continuous, choose \(\delta_x > 0\) such that \(d(F(x, y_0), F(a, y_0)) < \epsilon / 3\) whenever \(d(x, a) < \delta_x\). By triangle inequality: \[d(F(x, y), F(a, y)) \le d(F(x, y), F(x, y_0)) + d(F(x, y_0), F(a, y_0)) + d(F(a, y_0), F(a, y)) < \epsilon.\]
  2. To prove joint continuity at \((a, b)\), write: \[d(F(x, y), F(a, b)) \le d(F(x, y), F(x, b)) + d(F(x, b), F(a, b)).\] For \(\epsilon > 0\), local equicontinuity bounds the first term by \(\epsilon / 2\) for \(y\) near \(b\) uniformly in \(x \in B_X(a, r)\). Continuity of \(x \mapsto F(x, b)\) bounds the second term by \(\epsilon / 2\) for \(x\) near \(a\).

Lemma B.5 (Urysohn’s Lemma for Metric Spaces) Let \((X, d)\) be a metric space and let \(A, B \subset X\) be disjoint closed subsets. Then \[f(x) = \frac{d_B(x)}{d_A(x) + d_B(x)} \tag{B.7}\] defines a continuous function \(f \colon X \to [0, 1]\) such that \(\left.f\right|_A \equiv 1\) and \(\left.f\right|_B \equiv 0\).

Proof. By Lemma B.1, \(d_A\) and \(d_B\) are continuous. Since \(A\) and \(B\) are closed and disjoint, \(d_A(x) + d_B(x) > 0\) for all \(x \in X\). Thus \(f\) is continuous as the quotient of continuous functions with a non-zero denominator. If \(x \in A\), \(d_A(x) = 0 \implies f(x) = 1\). If \(x \in B\), \(d_B(x) = 0 \implies f(x) = 0\).

Corollary B.2 If \(A, B \subset X\) are disjoint closed sets, there exist disjoint open sets \(U, V \subset X\) such that \(A \subset U\) and \(B \subset V\).

Proof. Let \(f\) be the Urysohn function from Lemma B.5. Set \(U = \{f > 1/2\}\) and \(V = \{f < 1/2\}\).

B.3 Completeness in Metric Spaces

Definition B.12 (Cauchy Sequences and Completeness)  

  1. A sequence \(\{x_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\) in \((X, d)\) is Cauchy if \(\lim_{m, n \to \infty} d(x_n, x_m) = 0\).
  2. A metric space \((X, d)\) is complete if every Cauchy sequence converges to a limit in \(X\).

Exercise B.9 Let \((X, d)\) be a pseudo-metric space.

  1. Show every convergent sequence is Cauchy.
  2. If \(x_n \to x\) and \(y_n \to y\), show \(d(x_n, y_n) \to d(x, y)\).
  3. If \(\{x_n\}, \{y_n\}\) are Cauchy, show \(\lim_{n \to \infty} d(x_n, y_n)\) exists in \([0, \infty)\).

Proof.

  1. If \(x_n \to x\), then \(d(x_n, x_m) \le d(x_n, x) + d(x, x_m) \to 0\) as \(n, m \to \infty\).
  2. By Corollary B.1, \(|d(x_n, y_n) - d(x, y)| \le d(x_n, x) + d(y_n, y) \to 0\).
  3. By Corollary B.1, \(|d(x_n, y_n) - d(x_m, y_m)| \le d(x_n, x_m) + d(y_n, y_m) \to 0\), so \(\{d(x_n, y_n)\} \subset \mathbb{R}\) is Cauchy and hence converges.

Exercise B.10 Let \((X, d)\) be complete and \(A \subset X\). Show \((A, \left.d\right|_{A \times A})\) is complete if and only if \(A\) is closed in \(X\).

Proof.

  1. \((\Rightarrow)\) Let \(\{x_n\} \subset A\) with \(x_n \to x \in X\). Then \(\{x_n\}\) is Cauchy in \(A\), so completeness of \(A\) implies \(x_n \to a \in A\). Uniqueness of limits gives \(x = a \in A\), so \(A\) is closed.
  2. \((\Leftarrow)\) Let \(\{x_n\} \subset A\) be Cauchy. Since \(X\) is complete, \(x_n \to x \in X\). Since \(A\) is closed, \(x \in A\).

Exercise B.11 (Completion of a Metric Space) Given a metric space \((X, d)\), construct its complete completion \((\overline{X}, \overline{d})\) with an isometry \(i \colon X \to \overline{X}\) having dense image \(i(X) \subset \overline{X}\).

Proof.

  1. Let \(\mathcal{C}\) be the set of Cauchy sequences in \(X\). For \(a, b \in \mathcal{C}\), define \(d_{\mathcal{C}}(a, b) = \lim_{n \to \infty} d(a_n, b_n)\). \(d_{\mathcal{C}}\) satisfies symmetry and triangle inequality.
  2. Define \(a \sim b \iff d_{\mathcal{C}}(a, b) = 0\). This is an equivalence relation. Let \(\overline{X} = \mathcal{C} / \sim\) and \(\overline{d}(\overline{a}, \overline{b}) = d_{\mathcal{C}}(a, b)\). \((\overline{X}, \overline{d})\) is a valid metric space.
  3. Map \(i(x) = \overline{(x, x, x, \dots)}\). \(i\) is isometric and \(i(X)\) is dense in \(\overline{X}\).
  4. Completeness of \(\overline{X}\): Let \(\{\overline{a}^{(m)}\}\) be Cauchy in \(\overline{X}\). For each \(m\), pick \(b_m \in X\) such that \(\overline{d}(i(b_m), \overline{a}^{(m)}) < 1/m\). Then \(b = \{b_m\} \in \mathcal{C}\) and \(\overline{a}^{(m)} \to \overline{b}\).

Definition B.13 (Lipschitz Functions) Let \((X, d)\) be a pseudo-metric space and \(U \subset X\). A function \(f \colon U \to \mathbb{C}\) is \(\mathrm{Lip}\)-\(K\) on \(U\) (for \(K > 0\)) if \[|f(y) - f(x)| \le K d(x, y) \quad \forall x, y \in U. \tag{B.8}\]

Lemma B.6 (Extension of Lipschitz Functions) Let \(f \colon U \to \mathbb{C}\) be \(\mathrm{Lip}\)-\(K\) on \(U \subset X\). Then \(f\) extends uniquely to a \(\mathrm{Lip}\)-\(K\) function \(\hat{f} \colon \overline{U} \to \mathbb{C}\).

Proof. For \(x \in \overline{U}\), pick \(\{x_n\} \subset U\) with \(x_n \to x\). Since \(|f(x_n) - f(x_m)| \le K d(x_n, x_m)\), \(\{f(x_n)\} \subset \mathbb{C}\) is Cauchy. Define \(\hat{f}(x) = \lim_{n \to \infty} f(x_n)\). This is independent of the sequence choice and satisfies \(|\hat{f}(x) - \hat{f}(y)| \le K d(x, y)\) for all \(x, y \in \overline{U}\).

B.4 Sequential Compactness

Definition B.14 (Sequential Compactness) A metric space \((Y, \rho)\) is sequentially compact if every sequence \(\{y_n\} \subset Y\) contains a convergent subsequence.

B.4.1 A “Little” Tychonoff Theorem

Let \(\Lambda\) be a countable index set and let \((Y_\lambda, \rho_\lambda)\) be compact metric spaces for each \(\lambda \in \Lambda\). Given sequences \(\{y_\lambda(n) \colon n \in \mathbb{N}\} \subset Y_\lambda\), there exists an increasing sequence of indices \(\{n_k\}_{k=1}^{\infty} \subset \mathbb{N}\) such that \(\lim_{k \to \infty} y_\lambda(n_k)\) exists in \(Y_\lambda\) for all \(\lambda \in \Lambda\).

Proof. Without loss of generality, let \(\Lambda = \mathbb{N}\). Since \(Y_1\) is compact, there exists an infinite sequence of indices \(\Gamma_1 \subset \mathbb{N}\) such that \(\{y_1(n)\}_{n \in \Gamma_1}\) converges. Inductively, for \(\lambda \ge 2\), choose an infinite subset \(\Gamma_\lambda \subset \Gamma_{\lambda-1}\) such that \(\{y_\lambda(n)\}_{n \in \Gamma_\lambda}\) converges.

Construct the diagonal sequence \(n_k\) by: \[n_1 = \min \Gamma_1, \quad n_2 = \min (\Gamma_2 \cap (n_1, \infty)), \quad \dots, \quad n_k = \min (\Gamma_k \cap (n_{k-1}, \infty)).\] By construction, \(n_k \in \Gamma_\lambda\) for all \(k \ge \lambda\). Thus \(\{y_\lambda(n_k)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\) is a tail-subsequence of \(\{y_\lambda(n)\}_{n \in \Gamma_\lambda}\), so \(\lim_{k \to \infty} y_\lambda(n_k)\) exists for every \(\lambda \in \Lambda\).

B.5 Supplementary Remarks

B.5.1 Boundary and Closed Ball Warnings

Lemma B.7 Let \((X, \|\cdot\|)\) be a normed vector space with metric \(d(x, y) = \|x - y\|\). Then \[\overline{B_x(\epsilon)} = C_x(\epsilon) \quad \text{and} \quad \mathrm{bd}(B_x(\epsilon)) = \{y \in X \colon d(x, y) = \epsilon\}. \tag{B.9}\]

Proof. We show \(C_x(\epsilon) \subset \overline{B_x(\epsilon)}\). Let \(y \in C_x(\epsilon)\) and \(v = y - x\), so \(\|v\| \le \epsilon\). Define \(\alpha_n = 1 - 1/n\) and \(y_n = x + \alpha_n v\). Then \(d(x, y_n) = \alpha_n \|v\| < \epsilon\), so \(y_n \in B_x(\epsilon)\). Moreover, \(d(y, y_n) = (1 - \alpha_n) \|v\| \to 0\), showing \(y_n \to y \implies y \in \overline{B_x(\epsilon)}\).

B.6 Chapter Exercises

Exercise B.12 (Fast Cauchy Criterion) Let \(\{x_n\} \subset X\) and \(\epsilon_n = d(x_n, x_{n+1})\). Show that for \(m > n\), \[d(x_n, x_m) \le \sum_{k=n}^{m-1} \epsilon_k \le \sum_{k=n}^{\infty} \epsilon_k. \tag{B.10}\] Conclude that if \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} d(x_n, x_{n+1}) < \infty\), then \(\{x_n\}\) is Cauchy. Furthermore, show \(d(x, x_n) \le \sum_{k=n}^{\infty} \epsilon_k\) when \(x_n \to x\).

Proof. Repeated triangle inequality gives \(d(x_n, x_m) \le \sum_{k=n}^{m-1} d(x_k, x_{k+1}) \le \sum_{k=n}^{\infty} \epsilon_k\). If \(\sum \epsilon_k < \infty\), the tail sum goes to \(0\) as \(n \to \infty\), making \(\{x_n\}\) Cauchy. Taking \(m \to \infty\) in \(d(x_n, x_m) \le \sum_{k=n}^{\infty} \epsilon_k\) yields \(d(x_n, x) \le \sum_{k=n}^{\infty} \epsilon_k\).

Exercise B.13 (Completeness via Absolutely Summable Series) Show that \((X, d)\) is complete if and only if every sequence \(\{x_n\} \subset X\) satisfying \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} d(x_n, x_{n+1}) < \infty\) converges in \(X\).

Proof.

  1. \((\Rightarrow)\) Follows directly from Exercise B.12.
  2. \((\Leftarrow)\) Let \(\{x_n\}\) be Cauchy. Choose a subsequence \(n_1 < n_2 < \dots\) such that \(d(x_n, x_m) < 2^{-j}\) for all \(n, m \ge n_j\). Set \(y_j = x_{n_j}\). Then \(d(y_j, y_{j+1}) < 2^{-j}\), so \(\sum d(y_j, y_{j+1}) < \infty\). By hypothesis, \(y_j \to x\). Since a Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges to the same limit, \(x_n \to x\).

Exercise B.14 Suppose \(f \colon [0, \infty) \to [0, \infty)\) is a \(C^2\) function with \(f(0) = 0, f' > 0, f'' \le 0\). Show that \(d(x, y) = f(\rho(x, y))\) is a metric for any metric space \((X, \rho)\). In particular, \(d(x, y) = \frac{\rho(x, y)}{1 + \rho(x, y)}\) is a metric.

Proof. Concavity (\(f'' \le 0\)) and \(f(0) = 0\) imply subadditivity: \(f(a + b) \le f(a) + f(b)\) for \(a, b \ge 0\). Thus \(d(x, z) = f(\rho(x, z)) \le f(\rho(x, y) + \rho(y, z)) \le f(\rho(x, y)) + f(\rho(y, z)) = d(x, y) + d(y, z)\).

Exercise B.15 Let \(\{(X_n, d_n)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\) be metric spaces and \(X = \prod_{n=1}^{\infty} X_n\). For \(x, y \in X\), define \[d(x, y) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 2^{-n} \frac{d_n(x(n), y(n))}{1 + d_n(x(n), y(n))}. \tag{B.11}\] Show that: 1. \((X, d)\) is a metric space. 2. \(x_k \to x\) in \((X, d)\) iff \(x_k(n) \to x(n)\) in \(X_n\) for all \(n \in \mathbb{N}\). 3. \((X, d)\) is complete if \((X_n, d_n)\) is complete for all \(n\).

Proof.

  1. Metric properties follow from Exercise B.14 and dominated convergence of the sum.
  2. \((\Rightarrow)\) \(2^{-n} \frac{d_n(x_k(n), x(n))}{1 + d_n(x_k(n), x(n))} \le d(x_k, x) \to 0 \implies x_k(n) \to x(n)\). \((\Leftarrow)\) DCT on counting measure.
  3. Componentwise Cauchy sequences converge componentwise; their product converges in \((X, d)\).

Exercise B.16 Let \(A \subset X\) be dense.

  1. If \(F, G \colon X \to Y\) are continuous and \(\left.F\right|_A = \left.G\right|_A\), then \(F = G\) on \(X\).
  2. If \(Y\) is complete and \(f \colon A \to Y\) is uniformly continuous, \(f\) extends uniquely to a continuous \(F \colon X \to Y\).
  3. Give an example of continuous \(f \colon \mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{R}\) that does not extend continuously to \(\mathbb{R}\).

Proof.

  1. Set \(C = \{x \in X \colon F(x) = G(x)\}\). \(C\) is closed (as preimage of diagonal under continuous map \((F, G)\)) and contains dense \(A\), so \(C = X\).
  2. For \(x \in X\), pick \(\{a_n\} \subset A\) with \(a_n \to x\). Uniform continuity implies \(\{f(a_n)\}\) is Cauchy in \(Y\). Define \(F(x) = \lim f(a_n)\).
  3. Let \(f(q) = 1/(q - \sqrt{2})\) for \(q \in \mathbb{Q}\). \(f\) is continuous on \(\mathbb{Q}\) but unbounded near \(\sqrt{2}\), so no continuous extension to \(\mathbb{R}\) exists.
