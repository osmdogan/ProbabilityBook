Preface

In this book, we collect the lecture notes provided by Todd Kemp. These notes are based on the book Probability Tools with Examples by Bruce Driver and were developed for a full-year graduate course on probability theory and stochastic processes. Our aim is to make these lecture notes more accessible by providing a more structured and organized presentation of the material. We also provide additional explanations, illustrations, and examples to help readers better understand the concepts. The book consists of three main parts: Probability Theory I, II, and III.

Probability Theory I

The first part consists of 41 lectures on the fundamentals of probability theory. We begin with the Banach–Tarski paradox by showing that we cannot define a set function on all subsets of a unit circle in the complex plane that satisfies the usual properties of a measure. This motivates the need for a rigorous theory of measure, which forms the foundation of probability theory. We then introduce sigma-algebras, measures, and related concepts, and discuss the construction of measures from pre-measures defined on algebras, showing how these measures can be extended to complete measures on sigma-algebras, including the construction of Lebesgue and Radon measures. This part then continues with measurable functions (random variables) and their properties. We introduce integration theory for measurable functions, including the Lebesgue integral, and develop three important convergence theorems: the Monotone Convergence Theorem, Fatou’s Lemma, and the Dominated Convergence Theorem. We then cover product measures and Tonelli’s and Fubini’s theorems for iterated integration, followed by independence of events and random variables.

We then discuss Kolmogorov’s extension theorem for constructing stochastic processes with specified finite-dimensional distributions, Kolmogorov’s zero-one law governing the long-run behavior of independent sequences, and convolution as a tool for computing the distribution of a sum of independent random variables. We introduce Kolmogorov’s convergence criterion, which paves the way for the Strong Law of Large Numbers and the study of almost sure convergence of series of independent random variables. We finally close this part by applying the Strong Law of Large Numbers to basic concepts in renewal theory.

Probability Theory II

The second part consists of 44 lectures. We start with Cramér’s Large Deviations Principle, which provides a framework for understanding the probabilities of rare events in terms of the Legendre transform of the cumulant generating function. We then introduce total variation distance and Wasserstein distance for quantifying the difference between probability measures. We then move on to study weak and vague convergence of probability measures, and explore their implications for the convergence of random variables. We cover Prokhorov’s theorem and its applications to tightness and relative compactness of probability measures. The skorokhod representation theorem is also discussed, providing a powerful tool for constructing almost surely convergent sequences of random variables that mirror the weak convergence of their distributions.

We then discuss characteristic functions and their role in studying the distributions of random variables. Characteristic functions provide a powerful tool for analyzing convergence in distribution, as well as for deriving properties of sums of independent random variables. This sets the stage for the central limit theorem and other limit theorems in probability theory.

The conditional probability and expectation are covered in detail, including their definitions and fundamental properties through seven lectures. We cover probability kernels and their role in defining conditional distributions and constructing new probability measures from existing ones through three lectures. This sets the stage for a rigorous treatment of stochastic processes: we introduce the Poisson process, the Markov property, and the construction of Markov processes from transition kernels via Kolmogorov’s extension theorem on path space. We close this part by specializing to time-homogeneous Markov chains on finite or countable state spaces, where transition probabilities are encoded by Markov matrices and their continuous-time analogues by infinitesimal generators and the matrix exponential.

Definitions, Examples, and Theorems

To separate definitions, examples, propositions, and theorems from the main text, we use boxes with a white background for better visual distinction. The following examples illustrate this approach.

Definition 4.1 (Measure and Measure Space) Let \(\Omega\) be a sample space and \(\mathcal{F}\) be a \(\sigma\)-field (\(\sigma\)-algebra) over \(\Omega\). The pair \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) is called a measurable space, and elements \(E \in \mathcal{F}\) are called measurable sets (or events).

A set function \(\mu: \mathcal{F} \to [0, \infty]\) is called a measure if it satisfies the following two conditions:

  • Empty Set: \(\mu(\emptyset) = 0\).
  • Countable Additivity (\(\sigma\)-additivity): For any countable sequence of pairwise disjoint sets \(\{E_j\}_{j=1}^\infty \subseteq \mathcal{F}\) (i.e., \(E_i \cap E_j = \emptyset\) for all \(i \neq j\)): \[ \mu\left( \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j \right) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu(E_j). \tag{4.1}\]

Note that these two conditions are independent: countable additivity alone does not rule out the degenerate set function \(\mu \equiv \infty\), which satisfies Equation 4.1 vacuously but violates \(\mu(\emptyset) = 0\).

The triple \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) is called a measure space.

Proposition 4.1 (Validity of Dirac Measure) Fix \(\omega_0 \in \Omega\) and define the Dirac (point-mass) measure at \(\omega_0\) by \(\delta_{\omega_0}(E) := \mathbb{I}_E(\omega_0)\) for \(E \in \mathcal{F}\), i.e., \(\delta_{\omega_0}(E) = 1\) if \(\omega_0 \in E\) and \(\delta_{\omega_0}(E) = 0\) otherwise. Then \(\delta_{\omega_0}\) is a valid probability measure on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\).

Proofs

To state proofs, we use the Quarto div proof environment. This environment ends with a filled black square as the QED symbol to indicate the conclusion of the proof.

Proof (Proof of Proposition 4.1). First, \(\delta_{\omega_0}(\emptyset) = 0\) since \(\omega_0 \notin \emptyset\), and \(\delta_{\omega_0}(\Omega) = 1\) since \(\omega_0 \in \Omega\); this verifies the Empty Set axiom and normalization to a probability measure.

Next, let \(\{E_j\}_{j=1}^\infty \subseteq \mathcal{F}\) be a countable sequence of pairwise disjoint measurable sets. Since the sets are disjoint, the point \(\omega_0\) can belong to at most one set \(E_k\):

  • Case 1: There exists a unique index \(k \ge 1\) such that \(\omega_0 \in E_k\). Then \(\omega_0 \in \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j\), so \(\delta_{\omega_0}\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j\right) = 1\). On the sum side, \(\delta_{\omega_0}(E_k) = 1\) and \(\delta_{\omega_0}(E_j) = 0\) for all \(j \neq k\). Thus: \[\sum_{j=1}^\infty \delta_{\omega_0}(E_j) = \delta_{\omega_0}(E_k) + \sum_{j \neq k} 0 = 1 + 0 = 1.\]
  • Case 2: \(\omega_0 \notin E_j\) for all \(j \ge 1\). Then \(\omega_0 \notin \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j\), so \(\delta_{\omega_0}\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j\right) = 0\). On the sum side, \(\delta_{\omega_0}(E_j) = 0\) for all \(j \ge 1\), yielding \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \delta_{\omega_0}(E_j) = 0\).

In both cases, \(\delta_{\omega_0}\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j\right) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \delta_{\omega_0}(E_j)\), proving countable additivity. Together with the Empty Set axiom verified above, \(\delta_{\omega_0}\) is a measure; since \(\delta_{\omega_0}(\Omega) = 1\), it is a probability measure.
